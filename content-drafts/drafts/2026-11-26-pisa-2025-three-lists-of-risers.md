On 8 September 2026 the OECD published the results of PISA 2025, and the headline was a floor.
**"Student performance across OECD countries has continued to decline, reaching the lowest average ever
recorded by the OECD Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) in mathematics and reading."**

That sentence is true, and it is the release's first. The same release also names the systems
that went the other way. It does so three times, in three different lists, and each list runs on its own
clock. A ministry that reads one list as another will credit the wrong years. A reader who compares two
systems taken from two different lists is comparing two different questions.

Madār's Edition 06 opens this week and reads PISA 2025 system by system. Every piece that follows will cite
one of these lists, so this one comes first. It sets the three side by side, dates each from the OECD's own
words, and reports who is on all three.

## The floor, in the OECD's figures

The assessment was large. **"PISA 2025 was the largest assessment to date, involving 760 000 students across
91 countries and economies."** The Volume I page adds what that sample stands for: **"representing around 33
million 15-year-olds"**.

Science, this round's main subject, held between 2022 and 2025. Average performance **"was stable between 2022 and 2025 across
OECD countries, after having trended downward between 2009 and 2022."** Over the longer span it did not
hold. Average science scores **"fell modestly across OECD countries between 2015 and 2025"**, and the Volume I
page gives the OECD average in science as **489** points in 2015 and **482** in 2025, with non-OECD
participants **"broadly stable"** at **436** and **437**.

Mathematics and reading fell much further. Between 2015 and 2025 the OECD average in mathematics went from
**485** to **463** points, and in reading from **489** to **461**. The press release converts those falls
into time. Reading's 28 points are **"equivalent to about one and a half years of learning"**, and
mathematics' 22 points are **"equivalent to just over one year of learning."** Among non-OECD participants,
reading fell from **428** to **412**.

The most recent step is in the launch speech of the OECD's Secretary-General, Mathias Cormann. **"Across OECD countries, mathematics
scores fell by 9 points between 2022 and 2025."** The speech set it beside the round before: **"it comes on top of
the record 15-point drop between 2018 and 2022."**

The count at the bottom grew. **"Across the OECD, 20% of 15-year-old students are now estimated to be low
performers across all three subjects – up from 16% in 2022."**

And the OECD does not put this down to the pandemic alone. Its Volume I page: **"The weakening of performance
predates the COVID-19 pandemic in many countries"**.

## Three lists, three clocks

This is where the release asks for care. It names improving systems in three places, and the three are not
the same list.

**List A, since 2022.** The Volume I page: **"Within the OECD, science scores rose in Costa Rica, the Slovak
Republic, Türkiye and the United Kingdom. Among non-OECD members, scores also went up in Cambodia, El
Salvador, Georgia, Jordan, Mongolia, Montenegro, the Philippines, Saudi Arabia, Thailand, the United Arab
Emirates, Uruguay and Uzbekistan."** That sentence carries no period. The launch speech supplies it:
**"Since 2022, science scores went up in Costa Rica, Slovakia, Türkiye and the United Kingdom – and in 12
other countries and economies."** Four OECD members and twelve others. The speech and the page count the
same list, and it covers three years.

**List B, since 2015.** The press release, with its dates in the sentence before: **"Between 2015 and 2025,
some education systems improved performance in contrast to broader trends. Science performance rose in
Lithuania, the Slovak Republic, Türkiye, the Dominican Republic, Georgia, Macao (China), Montenegro, Qatar,
Thailand, the United Arab Emirates, and Uruguay."** Eleven names, over ten years.

**List C, both ends at once.** The press release again: **"Türkiye, the Slovak Republic, Montenegro, Qatar
and the United Arab Emirates increased their share of top performers while reducing their share of low
performers in science."** This is a different test from the other two. A rising average can come from
strong students getting stronger. List C asks whether the bottom shrank and the top grew together. Its own
sentence carries no period either, and the speech dates it: **"Over the past decade, Türkiye, the United
Arab Emirates, Montenegro, Qatar and Slovakia all cut their share of low performers and grew their share of
top performers at the same time."**

So the three lists answer three questions. A asks whose science average rose in the last three years. B
asks whose rose over ten. C asks who, over ten, cut the bottom and grew the top.

## Who is on all three

Four systems: **Türkiye, Slovakia, Montenegro and the United Arab Emirates.** Each rose in science over three
years and over ten, and over ten moved both ends of the distribution the right way.

Qatar is on two. It is on List B and List C, and it is not on List A. That is all the three registers print.
They do not say why, and they do not say that Qatar's science score fell after 2022. What can be said is
narrower: Qatar is not named on the OECD's own three-year list.

Jordan and Saudi Arabia run the other way. Both are on List A and neither is on List B or List C. The OECD
reports their science average rising over three years, and does not report it rising over ten.

Georgia, Thailand and Uruguay are on A and B but not on C. Their averages rose on both clocks. The release
does not report them as having shrunk the bottom and grown the top at once.

Three systems appear on List B alone: Lithuania, the Dominican Republic and Macao (China). Nine appear on
List A alone: Costa Rica, the United Kingdom, Cambodia, El Salvador, Jordan, Mongolia, the Philippines,
Saudi Arabia and Uzbekistan.

One rise on List A carries its own figure in the speech. **"Cambodia gained 35 points in just three years –
well over a year of learning."**

And one system's arc runs longer than any of the three clocks. **"Over two decades, Türkiye has moved from
below the OECD average in science to above it."**

## What the lists are not

They are not a ranking. The top of PISA is a separate sentence, and it names other systems: **"Most
participating Chinese jurisdictions, together with Estonia, Japan, Korea, Singapore, Chinese Taipei and the
United Kingdom, ranked among the ten highest-performing education systems in science, mathematics and
reading."** One system is named both there and on a list of risers. The United Kingdom is among the ten
highest performers in all three subjects, and its science average rose after 2022. Macao (China) is on List
B, and the top-ten sentence speaks of "most participating Chinese jurisdictions" without naming them, so the
registers read here do not say whether Macao is among them.

They are not about mathematics or reading. Every list above is about science, the one subject that was stable between 2022 and 2025. A
system can be on List A and still have fallen in reading. The registers read here do not say which ones did.

And they are not explanations. The OECD gives its own view of what works, in the Secretary-General's words
in the press release: **"The most successful education systems focus on fewer areas in greater depth, invest
in teachers, engage parents and provide targeted support for the students and schools that need it the
most"**. That is a statement of the OECD's priorities. It is not a finding about any system named above,
and it is not carried here as one.

## The one equity number, and why it moved

The same release reports a gap that narrowed between 2022 and 2025. **"Across OECD countries, advantaged students scored 85 points
higher in science than disadvantaged students."** The speech puts it in years: **"That is a gap of about four
years of learning."** Then the release says why it narrowed: **"largely because advantaged students performed
less well rather than improvements among disadvantaged students."**

That sentence is the reason List C matters. A gap can close because the bottom rises or because the top
falls. Across the OECD as a whole, between 2022 and 2025, it closed largely from the top. List C names the
systems where, in science over a decade, the bottom shrank and the top grew. Its span is longer than the
gap's, so the two are not one measurement. But its direction runs against the OECD-wide story, and it has
five names on it.

## Reading the next ministry statement

In the weeks ahead, ministries will describe their PISA 2025 results in their own words. The three lists
give a plain test for each statement. A claim to have improved should name its years, because 2015 and 2022
are both in the OECD's release and they produce different lists. A rise in science is not a rise in reading.
And a higher average is not the same claim as fewer students at the bottom.

Each piece in this edition will carry the span it is about in the sentence that makes the claim, because the
OECD's own release shows how much depends on it.
