Mongolia sits the Programme for International Student Assessment because a law says it must. The General
Education Law's article 35.6 is quoted on the register of the body that runs the test there, and it sets an
interval rather than an ambition: **"Боловсролын чанарыг гурван жил тутам олон улсын эрх бүхий
байгууллагаар үнэлүүлнэ"** — the quality of education is to be assessed every three years by an authorised
international body. The sentence that follows says the obvious consequence. Mongolia took part in PISA 2025.

It has taken part twice. The first time was 2022 — **"Монгол Улс 2022 онд анх удаа PISA үнэлгээнд
амжилттай хамрагдаж"**, Mongolia took part in the PISA assessment for the first time — with **7,276**
fifteen-year-olds working through mathematics, science, reading and creative thinking on computers. In
2025 the sample was **191 schools and 7,146 students**. Those are the two data points Mongolia has.

Two is enough to be measured. It is not enough to be trended, and the OECD's performance chapter is built
so that the difference is visible to anyone who reads its notes.

## What the three-year table says

Table I.2.6 reports the change between 2022 and 2025 in all three core subjects. Mongolia's row reads
**science 12, reading −4, mathematics −6**, in score points. The table carries its own comparison group one
row along, an **"OECD average-35"** at **−3, −14 and −9**.

Set the two rows side by side and the plain reading is awkward in a useful way. Two of Mongolia's three
figures are falls. All three of them are better than the comparison group's. The second sentence is as much
about the thirty-five countries in that average as it is about Mongolia: in a cycle where the OECD's own
average fell in every subject, falling by four points in reading was above the line.

Mongolia's science rise is not reported here as a rank, and the reason is in the table. The note says the
table is **"ranked, in descending order of the change in science performance between 2022 and 2025"**, and
at 12 score points Mongolia is in a three-way tie — with the United Kingdom and El Salvador — behind eleven
systems that rose further. A position inside a tie is a statement about how a list was sorted, not about a
country.

One thing in the table is Mongolia's alone. Thirty of the seventy-four systems in it rose in science.
Twelve rose in all three subjects. **Thirteen rose in science while both reading and mathematics fell — and
Mongolia's 12 is the largest science rise of the thirteen**, with Uruguay's 9 next. Those counts are this
publication's own, made by reading the table's rows in full; the OECD does not print them.

Significance is not discussed anywhere below, and this is where to say why. This chapter marks statistical
significance by the colour of a table cell. A colour is not text. Nothing here says that any Mongolian
movement is or is not significant, because the register as served does not tell us.

## The subject the cycle was built around

The centre's register prints something the chapter does not put beside Mongolia's row: which subject each
cycle was about. PISA runs one major domain per cycle and two minor ones. **"PISA 2025 судалгааны үндсэн
чиглэл нь байгалийн ухаан байсан"** — the major domain of the PISA 2025 study was science. The register's
own cycle table gives 2022's major domain as mathematics, with science and reading minor.

So Mongolia's two cycles sit on either side of a switch. Its mathematics fell six points between the cycle
built around mathematics and the cycle in which mathematics was minor. Its science rose twelve across the
same switch, in the other direction.

**Neither register joins those two facts, and this piece does not join them either.** The centre prints the
domain structure and prints that it took part; the chapter prints the changes and says nothing about major
domains in the paragraphs around Mongolia's row. Whether a major domain moves a country's score is a real
question about the instrument, and the answer is not in anything read here. It is set down because a reader
who has both numbers will ask it, and because the honest thing to report is that it is open.

## The ten-year table has no row for Mongolia

Two tables in this chapter measure change. One measures three years. The other, Table I.2.9, fits a trend
across cycles — a **"decennial"** trend, the average change per ten-year period between PISA 2015 and PISA
2025, calculated by linear regression. This publication has already written about what happens when a
system reads its two rulers as one, on Saudi Arabia, whose reading was up five on the first and down
eighteen on the second.

Mongolia cannot be read on the second ruler at all, and the table says so twice over. Its note admits only
systems that **"can compare results in all three subjects in PISA 2025 and at least two previous
assessments among PISA 2015, PISA 2018 and PISA 2022"**, and adds that the trend itself is **"only computed
for countries with comparable data in at least three PISA assessments over the period considered."**
Mongolia's first assessment was 2022. It has one previous assessment among the three named, and two in
total. It fails both conditions.

That is a deduction with two operands, and both are quoted above: the table's note, and the centre's own
*анх удаа*. The OECD does not say it about Mongolia; it is what the note and the register say when read
together.

Across the chapter's fourteen numbered tables, Mongolia has a row in nine — I.2.1, I.2.3, I.2.4, I.2.6,
I.2.8, I.2.10, I.2.11, I.2.12 and I.2.13 — and in none of the others. Of the ones that could have carried a
country row and did not, the decade is the only one. **The absence is a fact about the instrument and not a
measurement of the country**, which is the same shape this publication found on Uzbekistan, where two
tables stood empty because of what they asked of a subject. Here it is not the subject. It is the clock.

## The gap that narrowed from below

On 26 November this publication printed the OECD's own account of why the equity gap closed: the release
said it was **"largely because advantaged students performed less well rather than improvements among
disadvantaged students."** Table I.2.10 is the table that sentence sits on, and it can be counted.

It holds seventy-three systems and a comparison row. The socio-economic gap in science narrowed in
**forty-nine** of the seventy-three. Of those forty-nine, **thirty-four narrowed with the advantaged column
falling**. **Fifteen narrowed with both columns rising.** The **"OECD average-35"** row is one of the
thirty-four: disadvantaged **+5**, advantaged **−8**, gap **−13**.

Mongolia is one of the fifteen. Its disadvantaged students rose **19** score points, its advantaged
students rose **8**, and the gap between them closed by **11**. The release's sentence is accurate about the
average and about two systems in three that narrowed. It is not what happened in Mongolia, and the
chapter's own table is where you find that out.

The low end moved on the other measure too. In Table I.2.8, in percentage points — a different unit from
everything above, and not comparable with a score point — the share of Mongolian students below the
baseline in science fell **6.6 points**, against an OECD average-35 that rose **1.2**. The share of top
performers moved **0.1**.

Where the chapter does single Mongolia out in prose, it is on gender, and it is careful. The gender gap
**"widened in Mongolia, mainly driven by an increase in mean performance among girls (by 16 score points)
while boys' performance remained more stable."** Table I.2.12 gives the boys' figure as **7**, so *more
stable* is the chapter's word for a rise of seven points, and it is kept here because it is the chapter's.
Mongolia is also named among the systems where girls lead boys in science by **"about 20 score points or
more"**, and among those where the share of boys below baseline exceeds the share of girls **"by between 10
and 16 percentage points."** A country can raise its disadvantaged students by nineteen points and still
have its boys further below the line than its girls. Both are in the same chapter.

## What Mongolia measured, and what a Mongolian reader cannot read

The centre did not only sit the test. This cycle it added questionnaires the previous one did not run: the
students' parents, and **1,331** science teachers. Its register gives the reason — to study attainment
alongside family, school, teacher and learning conditions rather than at the level of the result — and
notes that for Mongolia this began with this cycle.

That is a country collecting more about itself than the international comparison requires, which makes the
next fact the awkward one. The register's timeline ends on a step dated **2026.09**: the national PISA 2025
report and its results were presented to the public. The page links that report by name,
**"ПИСА-2025 Үндэсний тайлан"**, as a button.

The link does not resolve. It returns 404, and so does the 2022 report linked beside it. Both were tested
on the register's three hosts and on three of its subdomains, and in both Cyrillic encodings, because a
Cyrillic path is exactly where an encoding mistake would counterfeit a 404. The Internet Archive holds no
snapshot of either file. The cause is legible in the addresses: they point into a `wp-content/uploads`
tree, and the register is now built on different software. The site was rebuilt and the documents did not
move with it.

**Nothing here says the report does not exist.** Its author says in writing that it was presented in
September 2026, and there is no reason to doubt that and no way to read it. Eleven days into this edition
Uruguay's own executive report on its own results was fetched, hashed and reproduced to the byte. Mongolia's
equivalent is named, dated, linked by the body that wrote it, and not served.

The body that owes that account is the Education Evaluation Centre — the name is its own, in Mongolian,
**Боловсролын үнэлгээний төв**, and the English words in this sentence are a description and not a name it
uses of itself. The last time its register named the person at its head was 17 April 2026, on a page that
the rebuild deleted and the archive kept: **Боловсролын үнэлгээний төвийн захирал Э.Цэрэнцоодол**, the
director of the Education Evaluation Centre. Whether he still holds the post is not something this
publication can read anywhere, and the register as it stands today names nobody at all.
