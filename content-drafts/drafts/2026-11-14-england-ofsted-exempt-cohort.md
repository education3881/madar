item 3 prints *"Only 17% of previously exempt schools that had a graded inspection this year remained

we inspected nearly 500 schools that were previously exempt."** and **"Of these, 17% retained their

and **367** had graded inspections (Figure 3: 263 primary, 104 secondary). The body uses the register's
own two sentences and states which denominator each percentage sits on.

**A2 · THE 17% COLLISION IS WORSE THAN THE BRIEF KNEW, AND IT IS INSIDE 2022.** Guardrail 2 warns about
two 17%s, one from 2022 and one from 2026. There is a **third**, and the first two are **fourteen weeks

| Register | Date | What its 17% is |
|---|---|---|
| Statistical release, *…as at 31 August 2022* | updated 15 Dec 2022 | the previously exempt schools that **retained outstanding** |
| News release, *Hundreds of formerly outstanding schools reinspected* | 22 Nov 2022 | the previously exempt schools **rated requires improvement** — *"around a fifth were rated requires improvement (17%) or inadequate (4%)"* |

Both are correctly cited; both are Ofsted's; they are near-opposites. **No numeric check this operation
owns could catch a confusion between them**, which is guardrail 2's point, and the body therefore prints

**A3 · The two registers' arithmetic does not close, and this piece does not close it.** The news release

**A4 · Two sentences the brief carries were not confirmed and are therefore not in the body.** The brief

## THE BODY

### The cohort that was frozen by rule

England ran an experiment on its own school inspectorate for eight years, and the thing that makes it an
experiment is that nobody designed it.

Between May 2012 and November 2020, England's schools regulator did not routinely inspect the schools it
had graded outstanding. The regulator's own words: **"Between May 2012 and November 2020, outstanding
primary and secondary schools were exempt from routine inspection."** It kept a door open —
**"In this period we could still inspect these schools if we had cause for concern"** — and then the
policy ended: **"In November 2020, the government lifted its exemption."** The regulator's news release
of 22 November 2022 gives the stock at both ends: **"There were 3,900 outstanding primary and secondary
schools when the exemption was introduced,"** and **"3,400 were outstanding when it ended."**

What the exemption produced, without meaning to, is a cohort whose grade was **held constant by rule**
while the world around it moved. Then the rule was lifted and the regulator went back in with the
instrument of the day. The same news release reports how long the gap had been:
**"The average for schools inspected last year was 13 years."**

The result is the one number this publication has been looking for and had said, in print, did not exist.

### The yield, in the register's own two sentences

The statistical release *State-funded schools inspections and outcomes as at 31 August 2022* reports:
**"In 2021/22, we inspected nearly 500 schools that were previously exempt."** And: **"Of these, 17%
retained their outstanding grade, while 21% were judged requires improvement or inadequate."** The
release's Figure 3 table puts **367** of those schools through a *graded* inspection — **263 primary and
104 secondary** — and the percentages above sit on the graded group, not on the nearly 500.

Set that beside the stock the same release publishes. **"Overall, 88% of all state-funded schools were
judged good or outstanding at their most recent inspection."** The Figure 1 row for 31 August 2022 reads
**21,725** schools at **18% outstanding, 70% good, 9% requires improvement, 3% inadequate**.

So: a national distribution in which 18 schools in every hundred were outstanding, and a sub-population
whose outstanding grade had been frozen for an average of thirteen years in which, re-measured, about
**one in six** held it.

This is where a reader has to be careful, and so does a publication. The regulator's **news** release of
22 November 2022 describes the same cohort with a different arithmetic: **"over 80% (308) of these
schools that had a graded inspection last year did not retain the outstanding grade"**, and
**"around a fifth were rated requires improvement (17%) or inadequate (4%)."** That 17% is the share
rated *requires improvement*. The statistical release's 17% is the share that *retained outstanding*.
**Two seventeens, from one regulator, fourteen weeks apart, about one cohort, meaning near-opposite
things.** The two releases' denominators are also close but not equal, and this piece does not add them
together or reconcile them: each figure is reported as the register that printed it printed it.

### The split nobody would have predicted

The most interesting figure in the release is not the headline. It is the difference between two school
phases that were exempt under the same rule for the same eight years.

**"Fourteen per cent of previously exempt primary schools inspected this year remained outstanding"** —
**"compared with 25% of secondary schools."** Nearly double, on the same instrument, in the same year.
And the decline was not correspondingly lopsided: **"Twenty-one per cent of the primary schools declined
to requires improvement or inadequate"**, **"with the same proportion seen in secondary schools."**

Which means the two phases differ in how much *outstanding* survived re-measurement, and not in how much
fell to the bottom two grades. That is a shape an averaged national figure cannot show, and a shape the
release shows because it published the cohort separately.

### What the frozen cohort does and does not prove

The exemption removed **one** confound and left the others standing, and the honest sentence says which.

Thirteen years is a long time. A school that was outstanding in 2009 and good in 2022 may have changed —
its leadership, its intake, its neighbourhood, its funding. The cohort holds the *grade on the record*
constant; it does not hold the school constant. What it rules out is the ordinary objection that a
distribution's movement is just schools being re-sorted by a ruler they were already measured on
recently, because **these schools had not been measured recently at all**.

The regulator also publishes the sentence that undercuts its own stock figure, and it is the reason the
88% was never as solid as it reads: **"Sixty-four per cent of all schools have not had a graded
inspection in the last five academic years,"** and **"14% have not had one in the last 10 academic
years."** A national grade distribution in which two thirds of the grades are at least five years old is
partly a photograph of the inspection schedule. Some of what the frozen cohort reveals about the
instrument was already visible in the interval.

Amanda Spielman, then Ofsted's Chief Inspector, put the policy on the record when the figures were
published. **"The exemption was a policy founded on the hope that high standards, once achieved, would
never drop,"** and **"Exempting outstanding schools deprived parents of up-to-date information."** On
what the re-grading showed: **"These outcomes show that removing a school from scrutiny does not make it
better."**

### And then the ruler was taken away, twice, by two different hands

A publication that treats this as a story about measurement has to carry what happened to the measure.

On **2 September 2024** the **Department for Education** announced: **"Single headline grades for schools
will be scrapped with immediate effect."** Its stated reason: **"Reductive single headline grades fail to
provide a fair and accurate assessment"** of a school's performance. What remained was the sub-structure
— **"parents will see four grades across the existing sub-categories"**, namely **"quality of education,
behaviour and attitudes, personal development and leadership & management."** This was the Department's
act, not the regulator's, and it is worth saying because the two are routinely merged: the grade was
removed by the minister, from the inspectorate.

Fourteen months later, on **10 November 2025**, a renewed education inspection framework (EIF) came into
effect — the regulator's own act. Its scale has five names, not four grades and an overall:
**urgent improvement · needs attention · expected standard · strong standard · exceptional**, across six
evaluation areas.

And the regulator tells parents, in writing, that the series does not join. **"The main change is that we
no longer give one overall judgement, such as 'good', 'outstanding' or 'requires improvement'."** Then:
**"It's important to understand that the new grades can't be compared to the old ones."** That guidance
was published 1 December 2025 and last updated 2 November 2026.

Replacing the single grade is defensible on its own terms, and the terms are the register's: the
guidance's stated audience is parents and carers, not statisticians. A parent choosing a school is not
maintaining a time series. The regulator never undertook to keep one for us, and a piece that treats the
removal as vandalism against a dataset is making an argument nobody owed it.

### The first year of the new instrument, and the sentence that must travel with it

On **10 November 2026**, Ofsted published the first annual release under the renewed framework.
**"There are currently 21,957 state-funded schools."** **"Between 10 November 2025 and 31 August 2026,
we have carried out 3,262 inspections, of which 3,188 were full inspections."** Graded *expected
standard* or better: **90% inclusion, 78% curriculum and teaching, 72% achievement, 84% attendance and
behaviour, 95% personal development and wellbeing, 85% leadership and governance**.
**"Safeguarding standards were met in 99% of full inspections."**

Those percentages may not be read against the 2022 stock, and the reason is printed in the release that
produced them: **"The 3,188 full inspections carried out represent only 15% of all state-funded schools,"
"so the grade profile is not representative of all schools."** The release also labels itself:
**"These statistics are published as official statistics in development."**

So the measurement England now publishes is a year's inspections, correctly described as a year's
inspections, on a scale whose owner says it does not join the previous one. The eight-year freeze has
been measured exactly once, and the instrument that measured it has since been withdrawn by its
commissioning department and replaced by its author.

### Our own correction

On 26 May 2026 this publication wrote that Bahrain's authority grades its schools in public. On
**9 November 2026** we published a successor on five cycles of those grades, and it closed by saying that
how much of a published distribution belongs to the schools and how much to the instrument *"is one
nobody publishes anywhere."*

That clause is wrong, and this is where it is corrected. One regulator does publish it — not because it
set out to, but because a policy froze a cohort for eight years and then the policy ended. The
measurement exists once, in England, for 367 schools, in a release from 2022, on a scale that no longer
exists.
