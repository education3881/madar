Five pieces of this edition have ended the same way. *Qatar's decade and its three years*, *The UAE is on
every list but one line*, *Saudi Arabia's reading, up five and down eighteen*, *Thailand's fourth list* in
part, and *The largest fall in the release* in full: each closes by naming the doors it knocked on and
reporting that no national authority's own reading of PISA 2025 could be read in served text. The
sentence has become a fixture.

Uruguay is the sixth, and it breaks the pattern. Its national PISA programme has published **an executive
report, three volumes and a technical report** on PISA 2025, dated **September 2026** — the same month the
OECD launched the results — and they answer this runner at **200**. The volumes are numbered **2, 4 and
5**, not 1 to 3: Uruguay has numbered its national volumes to the OECD's own volume scheme, so the two
report sets are meant to be read against each other.

So for the first time this edition can do the thing it has been describing the absence of: put the OECD's
account of a country beside that country's account of itself, both read in served text, and say where
they agree.

## They agree

The OECD's Chapter 2 gives Uruguay's 2025 science mean in **Table I.2.3**, whose first column is the
overall science scale: **445**, against an **OECD average of 482**.

Uruguay's own executive report gives the same figure to one decimal place. Its sentence covers the whole
of the tested population — students in public and private secondary schools, technical schools and rural
schools carrying the seventh, eighth and ninth grades — and reads: **"fue de 444,5 puntos en ciencias,
423,2 en lectura y 404,9 puntos en matemática"**.

**444.5 and 445 are one number.** That is the entire disagreement between the two accounts on the figure
they both publish, and it is a rounding convention rather than a difference. It is worth saying plainly,
because it is the result most readers would not predict and the one that makes everything below usable:
the national register and the international one are describing the same measurement, and neither is
quietly adjusting the other.

## What the national report prints that the international one does not

The agreement is the small half. The interesting half is the two figures in that Uruguayan sentence that
**have no counterpart anywhere in the OECD's chapter**: reading at **423.2** and mathematics at **404.9**.

*Saudi Arabia's reading, up five and down eighteen* established the property those two numbers are
filling in. Chapter 2 carries fourteen numbered tables and not one of them prints a reading mean for any
system. Reading appears in four of them and never as a level — a correlation coefficient, a yearly
learning gain for ten named systems, and a change in two tables. The chapter will tell a reader that
Uruguay's reading moved **−7** between 2022 and 2025 and **−10** per ten years on the fitted decennial
trend. It will not tell them what Uruguayan fifteen-year-olds actually scored.

Uruguay's own report does, in the same sentence as the science figure, without comment, as though it were
the ordinary thing to publish. The report is the work of the PISA Uruguay programme of the
**Administración Nacional de Educación Pública**, whose national coordinator is **Laura Noboa**, and whose
own page names eight people with the function each holds — data analysis, statistics, the science area,
the mathematics area. The figure has an address. It also records the order the three subjects fall in and says it is not
new: **"manteniendo, al igual que en ciclos anteriores, mejores logros en ciencias, seguidos de lectura y
matemática"** — science ahead, then reading, then mathematics, as in previous cycles.

**This is not a correction of the OECD and it is not a scoop.** The international report is a comparative
instrument and it has reasons for publishing a change where it does not publish a level. The point is
narrower and it is about where a reader has to go: **the question *what did this country score in
reading* is answered by the country, in Spanish, and by nobody else.**

## Where that places Uruguay, in its own report's terms

The national report sets its figures against two reference groups, and the piece uses its framing rather
than building one. Against the region, Uruguay reports a science mean **46 points above the Latin
American average**, and **35 points above it** in mathematics and reading. Against the OECD, the same
report is blunter: **"Uruguay alcanza aproximadamente 40 puntos menos que la OECD en las áreas de ciencias
y lectura"**, and close to sixty points below the OECD average in mathematics. The hedge
*aproximadamente* is the register's own and travels with the figure.

On the regional ranking, the report places Uruguay and Chile at the top of Latin America in science, with
no statistically significant difference between them, and the same in mathematics. In reading it does not
claim the top: **"En lectura, Uruguay se ubica en segundo lugar, por debajo de Chile y por encima del
resto de los sistemas educativos latinoamericanos participantes"** — second, below Chile.

A national report that prints its own second place in the subject where it chose to publish a number
nobody else published is doing something worth noticing. It would have been easier not to print the
reading mean at all.

## The two clocks, which for Uruguay agree

This edition has asked of every system which clock a riser list is running on, because the OECD's
three-year and ten-year instruments can point different ways. For Uruguay they do not.

**Table I.2.6**, the change between 2022 and 2025, gives Uruguay **9 in science, −7 in reading, −4 in
mathematics**. **Table I.2.9**, the fitted average decennial trend, gives **11, −10 and −14**. Science is
positive on both. Reading is negative on both. Mathematics is negative on both. Nothing changes sign with
the table.

*(The two tables are two instruments and this piece does not set one against the other: 9 is an arithmetic
difference between two assessments and 11 is a line fitted across at least three. That their signs agree
makes the temptation to compare them worse rather than better. Agreement is not commensurability.)*

**And Uruguay is on both of the release's decade science lists all the same** — the press release's list
of eleven systems whose science performance rose between 2015 and 2025, and the front page's list of
systems **"bucking the trend"**. Both of those lists are about science alone.

That is this edition's lesson arriving from a new direction. *Three lists of risers* found that an
improver list hides which clock it runs on. Uruguay shows the other thing a riser list hides: **the
subject**. A system can sit on a decade's improvement list, correctly, while two of its three core
subjects fall on every instrument the report prints.

In the unit that counts students rather than score points, **Table I.2.8** gives Uruguay **−4.7** and
**0.0** percentage points for its low and top performer shares over 2022 to 2025, against an OECD
average-35 of 1.2 and −0.3. The second figure is a change of zero to one decimal place and not an absence
of top performers; percentage points are not score points, and no figure from that table is set beside
one from the score tables here.

## The register checked against itself

One more thing is worth reporting because it tests the national report rather than quoting it. Uruguay's
executive report gives its own account of the top of the international distribution: the ten
highest-scoring systems in science, which it names as B-S-J-Z (China), Singapore, Macao (China), Taipei
(China), Japan, Estonia, Korea, the United Kingdom, Canada and New Zealand, with means running from 509
to 597.

Read against **Table I.2.1** of the OECD's own chapter, that is **the same ten systems in the same order**,
and 597 and 509 are exactly the first and last of them. A national report retelling an international one
is a place where errors accumulate quietly, and this one reproduces the source table without drift.

It also reports, for its own students, a science range of **330 to 560 points**, and that the widest
spread of the three subjects is in reading — which is, again, the subject the international chapter
publishes no level for.

## Who publishes this

The 2025 cycle tested **"aproximadamente 6.500 estudiantes uruguayos de 15 años de educación media"** —
approximately 6,500 fifteen-year-olds in secondary education, the hedge being the register's.

That is a small thing to be able to write, and this edition has not been able to write it once in five
pieces. Every figure in the other five is the OECD's, correctly read and honestly fenced, and every one
of those pieces says so in its last paragraph. Here the figures have two authors who agree.

## What is still owed

**The comparison in this piece is confined to the figures both accounts publish.** Uruguay's volumes on
equity, on science achievement and on mathematics achievement were not read for this piece; neither was
the technical report. Nothing above characterises what is in them.

**And the executive report is cited by page rather than quoted at length, for a reason that is about us
and not about Uruguay.** Its text layer is kerned, and a plain extraction breaks words across it —
*reporte* comes out as `R eport e`, and hyphenated line breaks survive into the text as real hyphens. Six
spans were quoted above and each was verified character by character against a re-extraction whose method
is set out in this piece's sources; two further sentences that would have been useful carry an
extraction hyphen inside a word and are therefore **not quoted here at all**, because a quotation that
reproduces our own tooling's damage is a claim about our tooling.
