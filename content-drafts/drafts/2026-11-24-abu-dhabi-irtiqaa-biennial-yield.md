## The body

Abu Dhabi's school regulator published a number on 1 October 2025, and the number is small enough to read
in one breath: 23 private schools had moved up a grade. The page says it twice. The first time, in its own
summary line, it says that *"23 private schools have advanced in Irtiqa'a inspection results from
Acceptable to Good in one academic year."* The second time, in the body, it says that *"23 schools advanced
to Good within a single year according to the latest Irtiqa'a results."*

Those two sentences are not the same sentence. *In one academic year* names an interval that a school
travelled. *Within a single year according to the latest results* names a year of publishing. One is a
claim about schools, the other a claim about a release, and a parent reading the page has no way to tell
which one the 23 is a count of. This piece does not resolve it either, and the reason it does not is the
subject.

What the page also says is how often the measurement happens: *"Irtiqa'a inspections are conducted
biennially and evaluate schools across six core standards and 17 performance indicators."* Flat, in the
present tense, with no qualification. The regulator's own policy document says it differently. ADEK's
*School Policy on Quality Assurance*, version 1.1, states that *"the typical inspection cycle for a School
is 2 years"* — and the sentence immediately after it reads *"ADEK reserves the right to conduct any type of
inspection or visit at its discretion."*

Two registers, one owner, and the stronger claim is on the page a parent reads. *Typical* is a word that
admits exceptions; *biennially* is a word that does not. The discretion clause sits one sentence away from
the first and nowhere near the second.

### The cycle is not the same cycle for every school

The policy is exact about when a school enters the cycle, and the news page does not mention it at all:
*"Schools shall be subject to ADEK inspections in their third year of operations and every 2 years
thereafter."*

A school's inspection history is therefore a function of the year it opened. That has a consequence for
the five rows the news page prints — 13 Outstanding, 51 Very Good, 93 Good, 42 Acceptable, 5 Weak — which
sum to 204 schools. The sum is ours, computed from the five printed rows, and it is stated here as that
and nothing more. Those 204 schools are not standing at the same place in a shared cycle. They are
standing at different points in their own, some inspected last year, some the year before, some in their
third year of operation and being measured for the first time.

A distribution assembled from readings taken at different times is a legitimate thing for a regulator to
publish. It is a different thing from a cohort measured together, and the difference matters precisely
when the question is how much of a year's movement belongs to the schools.

### Four kinds of visit, and only one of them grades

The policy defines four. *"These comprise monitoring visits, inspections, follow-through visits, and
thematic visits."* It then defines three of the four away from rating.

A **monitoring visit** is for *"readiness for ADEK Inspections"* — a visit about whether a school is ready
to be measured, not a measurement. New schools get one in their second year of operation; established
schools get one *"if ADEK believes the visit will support the school in its next ADEK inspection."* A
**thematic visit** is conducted *"at ADEK's discretion to gather data on specific aspects of the education
system and inform policymaking."* A **follow-through visit** is conducted *"at ADEK's discretion, for the
purpose of following up with schools in addressing inspection recommendations."*

Only the fourth produces a grade. An **ADEK Inspection** is *"regular inspections conducted by ADEK to
assess the quality of education provided by a school against the UAE School Inspection Framework,"* and
what follows one is *"a finalized School Performance Report specifying their inspection rating."*

So "my school was visited" and "my school was graded" are different statements in this system, and the
policy is the document that makes them different. Three of the four visit types could happen to a school
in a year without moving it anywhere on the six-point scale.

### What a parent can find out, and what they cannot

Two things in the policy answer a parent's question better than the news page does.

The first is an obligation on the school. *"Schools shall publish their inspection rating on their website
and make these available to parents of all students studying at the school."* A rating is not a figure a
parent has to take on trust from a sector total; by policy, it is on their own school's website. ADEK's own
publication of ratings is weaker and is written as a right rather than a duty: *"ADEK reserves the right to
publish inspection ratings and/or reports on its website and other relevant government websites."*

The second is the shape of the number before it becomes public. A school *"shall receive a draft inspection
report after inspections to check for factual inaccuracies,"* and then receives the finalised report and
*"ha[s] 10 working days to appeal the result."* A published distribution is therefore a post-appeal
artefact. That is ordinary administrative practice and it is worth a reader knowing, because it is one more
reason a year's figures are not a photograph of a year.

What a parent cannot find out, from anything read here, is **why** their school was measured again. If a
school at Acceptable was re-inspected inside a year, the policy's authority for that is the discretion
clause — a reserved right, not a published rule. The brief that commissioned this piece asked the drafter
to look first and hardest for a mandatory follow-up inspection for schools at Acceptable or lower, because
such a rule would dissolve the whole question. It is not in this register. The follow-through visit is
discretionary, is keyed to recommendations rather than to a band, and does not grade.

### Whose ruler it is

Irtiqa'a *"operating under the UAE Unified School Inspection Framework, remains the core regulatory tool to
drive school improvement,"* the news page says. The framework is not one authority's document. ADEK's
policy cites it in its references under four owners jointly: *"Dubai Knowledge and Human Development
Authority (KHDA), Abu Dhabi Centre for Technical and Vocational Education and Training (ACTVET), Abu Dhabi
Department of Education and Knowledge (ADEK), and Ministry of Education (MoE)."* The same policy gives the
instruments under which inspectors apply it — *"Law No. 9 of 2018 Establishing the Department of Education
and Knowledge and Federal Decree-Law No. (18) of 2020 on Private Education and its amendments."*

Unified across the authorities that wrote it is a different claim from national, and it is the one the
register supports. The framework's own document is published by KHDA, and KHDA's origin answers every
client this publication owns with a 200 and a 954-byte block page, measured on 2026-11-22. We have not read
the framework itself. We have read the policy that cites it, defines it and dates it, which is the nearest
first-party register available from here, and that is the limit of what this piece claims about it.

### The bar, and the band with no row

The page states the bar plainly: *"In line with national expectations, Good is the minimum standard for
every school in the UAE."* Against that bar, 47 of the 204 schools in the printed rows are below it. The
page attaches a consequence, and attaches it in the past tense: schools rated Acceptable or lower *"were
subject to restrictions on capacity growth and grade expansion,"* and those restrictions *"have been lifted
only for schools demonstrating verified improvement."* One restriction is not lifted at all: *"Schools
remain restricted from enrolling new Emirati students until further regulatory requirements are met."*

One more thing about the five rows. The page says *"schools are assessed on a 6-point scale: Outstanding,
Very Good, Good, Acceptable, Weak, and Very Weak."* Six bands are named; five have rows. The sixth, Very
Weak, is defined on the page and carries no row in the distribution. That is all this piece says about it.
A band with no row is not a band with a zero in it.

### What the 23 tells a parent

Less than it looks, and the shortfall is not the regulator's fault.

The number is a published claim, dated 1 October 2025, that 23 schools rose from Acceptable to Good. The
page gives it in two forms that mean different things. The cycle that produced it is called typical by its
own policy and is overridden at discretion. The schools in the distribution it sits beside are at different
points in their own cycles. Three of the regulator's four visit types do not grade. Nothing read here says
how the 23 were measured twice — the policy permits it by reserving discretion and reports it nowhere.

What a parent can do is narrower, firmer, and better: read their own school's rating, which their school is
obliged to publish on its own website.
