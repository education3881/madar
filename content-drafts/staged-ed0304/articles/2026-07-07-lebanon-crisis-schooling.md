In the last week of August 2025, Lebanon's ministry of education did the most ordinary thing a ministry can do: it issued the decision opening the school year — preparatory work in the public schools from 1 September, classes thereafter — over the signature of the education minister, Rima Karami. The form is administration anywhere in the world. The substance is stranger, because the system the paper opens has almost no money of its own to open with. "The core problem is that 90 to 95 percent of public schools begin their school year without any operating budget — zero in the cash box — which leaves them unable to secure the most basic supplies," Hussein Jawad, the president of the public primary-school teachers' league, told the Lebanese outlet Hona Loubnan a few weeks into that year (our translation from the Arabic). He is a league president rather than a school operator — a distinction this piece returns to at its end — but his zero is the working assumption of everything that follows. In Lebanon, the school year is declared by the state, and paid for, where it is paid for at all, by somebody else.

The instrument standing where a treasury should be has a name: the Transition and Resilience Education Fund — TREF — launched by the ministry with UNICEF in 2022 as a pooled fund under government leadership, currently financed by the European Union, Germany, France and Switzerland. Its logic was set by the currency: when the pound collapsed, a public teacher's pay collapsed inside it, and the state could not top up salaries in money it could not defend. So the top-up moved outside both the treasury and the currency. In December 2021 the World Bank announced an incentive programme for public-school teachers for the year then under way; by 2022–23 the mechanism had hardened into a productivity allowance of $375 a month, paid directly in US dollars to some 15,000 teachers and school staff, financed by UK aid and repurposed World Bank and UNICEF grants. A donor-funded, dollar-denominated allowance, routed around the state it serves, became the de facto salary system of a public school network. What the state itself still pays has to be read from the unions' side of the table, because no ministry salary scale is on the public record: the teachers' leagues and Education International put a tenured teacher's take-home, allowances included, at roughly $350 to $600 a month, and the contract teachers who now carry much of the timetable at about 150,000 Lebanese pounds an hour.

The same buildings, meanwhile, run the year twice. Lebanese children attend the morning shift; non-Lebanese children attend an afternoon second shift, from kindergarten to Grade 9, with registration through October 2025 — a deadline the UN refugee agency's help pages record being extended mid-month — on presentation of residency papers or UNHCR identification. Those are the mechanics as published, and they are carried here as mechanics: a system that cannot fund its first school day runs a second one, donor-funded, inside the same walls. And the morning shift is filling past its plans. A survey of 1,089 parents by Mohammad Hammoud, a researcher at the Centre for Lebanese Studies, found 46 percent of surveyed pupils moving from private schools into public ones for 2025–26, with a year of private schooling for one child costing near four times an average family's monthly income. It is a survey, not a census — and Jawad's own caution, to the same outlet, was that the picture would not be clear until registration closed. But no one on the record disputes the direction: the system that opens on zero is the one Lebanese families are moving into.

What the pay arithmetic does to the people inside it is also on the record, though it resists counting. The leagues declared an open strike in April 2025; a further general strike across public education was called for the end of November. No primary tally of teaching days lost to strikes exists anywhere on the public record — a gap worth naming in a piece about mechanisms, because it means the cost of the pay collapse is documented in kind rather than in number. The kind is specific enough. The ministry runs a two-month catch-up programme against learning loss; Jawad described working inside it with nothing to work with: "No paper, no ink, not even working photocopiers" (our translation). A remediation programme without paper is the zero in the cash box, met at the photocopier.

The autumn 2024 conflict had already damaged schools and displaced families; the system absorbed that year and kept its calendar. Then came what UNICEF calls the March 2026 escalation, and the mechanism was tested at full scale. Every school in the country closed for more than three weeks; learning was disrupted for roughly 400,000 children. Within the month, the ministry and UNICEF had moved 40,000 public-school teachers and about 390,000 enrolled students onto supported remote learning — the whole public system, pivoted at once. By the end of March, 344 public schools were serving as shelters for some of the more than 1.1 million people displaced, among them over 390,000 children, and the ministry was reopening the rest in phases on a single operational test, school by school: a building being used as a shelter cannot also be a classroom, and its teachers must be available. It is the purest expression of the seam this piece is tracing — a ministry with almost no money of its own deciding, because deciding is the asset it still holds, which of its buildings can be a school this week.

In June 2026, the ministry counted. A nationwide damage assessment — run with technical support financed through TREF, the pooled fund paying even for the state's survey of its own losses — found 340 public schools damaged and 17 destroyed outright. UNICEF's statement of 2 July sets the September line without decoration: without urgent repair, at least 100,000 children risk having no classroom to return to when the year opens. Donor money had already begun moving that way — the ministry, the European Union and UNICEF announced education-continuity support for the south in February. So the summer of 2026 is a repair race run on the now-standard architecture: the ministry administers — it opened the year by decision, scheduled the double shifts, executed the remote pivot, counted the damage — and nearly every act it administers is financed from the pooled fund and its donors. A school year administered by a state and paid for by its guests: that is the mechanism, named.

Two honest limits sit on this account, and this is a story that would be poorer for hiding them. The first is whose voice it carries. The president of the primary teachers' league appears here as a labelled, contextual voice — a serving educator, but a spokesman by office. What the account lacks, because the public record since 2024 lacks it, is a serving principal or a schools-directorate official speaking on the record about what opening a school on an empty cash box actually requires — the very people Jawad credits when he says that without principals who believe in the cause of public education, the system "would have collapsed long ago" (our translation). Until one of them speaks for the record, that testimony is carried at one remove, and we say so. The second limit is arithmetic: the salary figures are the unions' side of a table the ministry has not published, and the strike ledger has no primary count. What remains is the mechanism itself, and its next test has a date. The buildings are counted, the repair bill sits with the donors, and the workforce's patience is the one line in this budget no pooled fund can replenish. The year will very probably open anyway. It has opened every September so far. That is both the achievement and the warning.
