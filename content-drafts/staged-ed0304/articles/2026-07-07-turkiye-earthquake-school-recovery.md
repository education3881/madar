The Ali Sayar Vocational and Technical Anatolian High School reopened in Hatay in October 2024: sixteen classrooms, steel-framed construction, a new building in place of one the earthquakes of 6 February 2023 had left unusable. Reopenings in Türkiye's earthquake provinces follow a script by now, and the script's most interesting line belonged to the man who runs schooling in the hardest-hit province. "Work continues at 113 of our schools in Hatay," Harun Tüysüz, the provincial director of national education, said at the ceremony. "Thirteen of them are again being built by the Ahbap Association. God willing, we expect even more by the end of next year." No ribbon-cutting rhetoric — a work-in-progress count and a delivery forecast. In this recovery, a reopening is a ledger entry, and the ledger, not any single building, is the mechanism worth studying.

Read in order, the ledger's lines run like this. The earthquakes put about 9,500 classrooms out of use across eleven provinces — the education ministry's own count of the loss. By 9 September 2024, the start of the following school year, more than 7,500 destroyed classrooms had been rebuilt and returned to service — a figure the minister, Yusuf Tekin, specified excludes repair and strengthening work. By the third anniversary, 6 February 2026, the count stood at 14,304 classrooms completed and 9,763 under construction. Behind the running totals sits an investment programme of 1,241 new schools and 19,784 new classrooms, with 3,119 more buildings strengthened rather than replaced. And above all of it sits the target that reframes the whole exercise: some 24,000 additional classrooms by September 2026, lifting the region's classroom stock to a projected 137,505 — at least ten percent more than existed on 5 February 2023, in every one of the eleven provinces. That last clause is the design choice this piece exists to point at. The programme's finish line was set above its starting line: not restoration but surplus, poured as insurance against the next shock. A system that rebuilds to a hundred percent has recovered; a system that rebuilds to a hundred and ten has admitted, in concrete, that the hazard has not gone away.

One discipline note belongs beside every figure above: this is a ministry counting its own work. The delivery numbers are ministerial statements — anniversary press events, provincial directorates reporting upward — and no independent audit of the classroom counts has been published. That does not make the numbers wrong; it makes them a particular kind of number, and they are labelled accordingly wherever they appear here. A second labelling matter sits inside the totals themselves: donor-built schools — the thirteen Ahbap Association sites in Tüysüz's Hatay count among them — are financed by charities and philanthropic campaigns but appear within the ministry's completion figures. The state counts what it did not always pay for. Neither caveat is a scandal; both are simply what reading a self-reported ledger honestly requires.

Concrete was the slow mechanism. The fast one — and the more transferable — was assessment: in 2023 the exam calendar held, and the system moved everything else instead. The high-school entrance exam, the LGS, was limited that year to the first semester's curriculum — the semester that had actually been taught when the ground moved; the university entrance exam, the YKS, likewise excluded the final grade's second-semester topics. For students in the affected provinces, the year-passing rule was rewritten so that the higher of their two semester grades stood for the whole year — no one's certification would hinge on the semester spent in a tent or a relative's spare room. Then the exam authority, ÖSYM, published its logistics package in May 2023: displaced candidates assigned exam centres away from their registered residence were moved on state-organised transport and housed in state dormitories for the exam weekend, with the national disaster agency, AFAD, coordinating; exam fees were waived across the eleven provinces and the district of Gürün; and the higher-education council added a 25% placement quota at earthquake-zone state universities. Every line follows the same principle: move the candidate, not the calendar; shrink the tested syllabus to the taught one; hold the machinery still while everything around it is moving.

Hatay is the stress test, because Hatay is where the destruction concentrated. When Tüysüz spoke in October 2024, his province counted 110 schools completed against 113 under construction — the ledger's two columns almost exactly level. By the 2025–26 school year, provincial reporting — carried in the regional press from governorship figures, a secondary source worth naming as such — had the columns moved: 199 new schools in service, 798 repair jobs done, 70 schools still in construction. Between the two snapshots, the under-construction column drained into the in-service column at very nearly the rate the director's ceremony line had forecast. This is what a rebuild ledger is for: it converts an unanswerable question — has Hatay recovered? — into a checkable one: which column did this school move to, and when. The ministry, for its part, said in February 2026 that classroom stock already exceeds pre-quake levels in all eleven provinces, by an average of sixteen percent in its own telling — a claim the September 2026 completion pledge will make checkable within a season.

Then there is what the ledger does not carry, and the piece owes the reader both omissions. The count counts classrooms; it does not count children back into them. Rebuilt rooms and returned students are different quantities — families displaced from the earthquake provinces settled elsewhere, and whether they have come back is not published alongside the classroom totals. The ledger's most important companion number does not yet exist in public. Nor does a second: the wind-down of the temporary stock. Container classrooms carried a real share of the region's teaching after February 2023 — teachers' union statements described heavy container use in Hatay well into 2024 — yet no ministry-published count records how many temporary classrooms remain in service or have been stood down. We flag the absences rather than adjudicate them: a rebuild ledger is credible in what it publishes, and legible in what it leaves out.

What travels from Türkiye's three years is not the tonnage of concrete; it is two design choices and a habit. The choices: set the rebuild target above the loss, so that recovery ends with insurance rather than parity; and hold the assessment calendar by shrinking the tested curriculum to the taught one, so that a cohort's certification never becomes part of the damage. The habit: publish the running count, dated, line by line — and accept that a published ledger invites exactly the scrutiny this piece has applied, including to the lines it still lacks. September 2026 is written into the programme as the completion date; by then the classroom stock, the container wind-down and the returned children will all be countable, whether or not all three are counted in public. The finish line stands above the starting line. The open question is who will be standing at it.
