On the morning of 1 September 2025, Ukraine's education minister, Oksen Lisovyi, published the shape of the school year beginning that day: 3.5 million pupils, sorted into three formats. Around 8,000 schools — some 2.3 million children — opened in person. Roughly 2,300 schools, another 780,000 pupils, opened "mixed," partly in classrooms and partly remote. About 1,200 schools, carrying 396,000 pupils, opened at distance entirely. Eighty-eight percent of pupils, the release underlined, would study fully or partly in person; the announcement itself ran under the claim that most children would learn offline. Two further lines completed the register: 35,361 pupils on temporarily occupied territory, enrolled remotely in Ukrainian schools, and 302,889 pupils abroad, enrolled the same way. The figures are start-of-year numbers — they move with the security situation, which is why the ministry dates them — but the structure they describe does not move. In its fourth September, this is not improvisation held together by adrenaline. It is an operating model: one national curriculum, one enrolment register, three delivery pipes.

The model is governed the way ordinary school years are governed — by late-August paperwork. On 20 August 2025 the Cabinet of Ministers fixed the year by resolution № 1003: 1 September to 30 June, under martial law. Two days later the Ministry of Education and Science issued letter № 1/17526-25, "On the organisation of the 2025/2026 school year," the instrument through which each school gets its format. The assignment rule is infrastructural, not preferential. A school opens in person if it has certified shelter capacity for its enrolment; where shelter covers only part of the roll, the school runs mixed, rotating cohorts between classroom and screen; where no safe format exists, instruction moves to distance — under rules the ministry tightened in July 2025 with order № 1020, which narrowed the conditions for forming a distance class at all. The decision chain runs shelter audit, then format assignment, then schedule, then platform fallback — and the state budget follows the same chain, through a subvention line for school shelters under the policy the ministry calls "School Offline." The reason is carried in the documents as an operational fact, and it needs stating only once: educational infrastructure keeps being hit. By February 2025, UN operational reporting counted more than 3,600 education facilities damaged and around 400 destroyed. Shelter is why in-person schooling exists at all; everything else in the machinery follows from that.

What makes three formats one system, rather than three systems sharing a ministry, is the register underneath them. A pupil in a Kharkiv basement classroom, a pupil studying from occupied territory, and a pupil logging in from Kraków are enrolled in the same national system of record, against the same curriculum. The distance pipe runs on standing infrastructure — the All-Ukrainian Online School, the state platform administered by the Institute of Educational Analytics, a ministry body: permanent plant, not emergency scaffolding. And the state keeps legislating the register's edges. A resolution of June 2025 established the procedure for recognising learning acquired abroad, so that a returning child does not re-enter through a bureaucratic void; the July order narrowing distance classes is, read in context, the same policy from the other side — wherever a shelter can be certified, the format is moved up the ladder toward the classroom. The direction of design is consistent: distance is carried as a right of enrolment, and constrained as a default.

The most concrete artefact of the model is municipal, and it is built, not improvised. On 1 September, Kharkiv opened the year with seven purpose-built underground schools — three of them constructed over the spring and summer of 2025 — and six metro stations converted into classrooms. Each underground school takes at least a thousand pupils in two shifts, by the city education department's count. "It is important that children can study not only online but also offline. Yet we cannot allow them to study above ground," the mayor, Ihor Terekhov, said days before the opening. "We were the first to convert six metro stations, where children will now be able to study." What he is describing is an asset class, not a hiding place: facilities with construction seasons, opening dates and expansion plans — the city intends several underground schools in every district, and its first underground kindergarten is under construction. The increments are countable. When the UN refugee agency and the Lutheran World Federation opened shelter classrooms at the city's Railway Lyceum in February 2025 — five schools rehabilitated and a sixth equipped, roughly 2,500 children of in-person capacity — the agency recorded what the addition bought: Kharkiv's mixed-format cohort reached 7,500 children. In this model, a shelter is not where a school hides. It is how a school counts.

Further north, in the village of Bobryk in Sumy region, the school has moved entirely into its basement and runs in two shifts — a principal's answer to arithmetic, not to symbolism: half the space, all of the enrolment, therefore twice the day. The principal, Oleksii Korenivskyi, compressed the operating logic into three sentences when the Associated Press visited as the year began: "We must do everything so this generation is not lost. Time is the only thing you cannot make up. This is our future, and we must give it everything we have." It is the speech of a man managing a timetable. Time is the one resource his school still fully controls, and the two-shift basement is the machine that spends it deliberately.

The model's costs concentrate exactly where its designers cannot declare victory: in keeping the three formats equivalent. Equivalence is expensive in teacher load — a basement school on two shifts is a staff teaching double; a mixed school is a staff delivering everything twice, in two media. It is expensive in attendance, which must be defined three ways and audited in all of them. And it is expensive in assessment, which has to read the same whether a pupil sat the year in a classroom, in a rotation, or in a bedroom in another country. The system's own numbers say where the strain lives: in frontline territories, UN reporting put the share of children studying only online at 70% in early 2025. The register can guarantee those children a seat in the system — enrolment, curriculum, certification. What nobody can yet publish is parity: whether the same year, delivered down three pipes, arrives as the same learning. The ministry's tightening of distance classes is its tacit answer — the formats are ranked, not equal, and the machinery works to move children up the ranking as fast as shelters can be certified.

That is the exportable finding, and it has nothing to do with the circumstances that forced it. Systems hit by floods, pandemics or mass displacement usually improvise their formats and lose their register — children scatter into arrangements nobody records, and the state discovers afterwards what it stopped knowing. Ukraine's machinery inverts the priority. The format is the variable, adjusted school by school against an audited fact; the register is the constant, holding the pupil in Kharkiv, the pupil on occupied territory and the pupil in Kraków inside one system of record. The numbers to watch next September are the middle band and the third pipe: whether the 780,000 in mixed format drain toward classrooms as shelter certification advances, and whether the 396,000 at distance shrinks for the same reason. The formats are the emergency. The register is the state.
