Most curriculum decisions are made by ministries answerable, at whatever distance, to the parents of the children they school. The one that governs roughly 300,000 children in the camps of Cox's Bazar was made another way. In January 2020 the Government of Bangladesh decided that the Rohingya refugee children on its territory would be taught the national curriculum of Myanmar — the country they came from — in Burmese, by teachers drawn from the camps and the host community, inside learning centres run by UNICEF and its partners. Not the Bangladeshi curriculum, not Bangla, not the schools of the national system. The stated premise was, and remains, return: schooling that would keep a generation ready to re-enter Myanmar's classrooms and hold its place there whenever repatriation happens. This piece does not assess that premise — whether return is near, far or likely is a question for other desks. It follows the mechanism the premise built: how a state chooses another state's curriculum, how the choice scaled, what happened when the money that operates it fell away, and what the arrangement leaves in a student's hand at the end — the question a curriculum always answers last.

Read as system design, the 2020 decision has a shape worth pausing on. A curriculum is a promissory document: it points at an examination, the examination points at a certificate, and the certificate points at a state that will honour it. Choosing Myanmar's curriculum aligned the first link with the stated future — children learning in Burmese, from Myanmar's syllabus, could in principle re-enter Myanmar's system at grade rather than at zero. But the same choice detached the schooling from every institution of the place where the children actually live: a camp classroom in Bangladesh teaching a syllabus no Bangladeshi board examines, in a language no Bangladeshi employer requires. Two links of the chain existed from the first morning. The third — the state that stamps the certificate — was deferred to the future the policy assumed. Keep that structure in view; the story ends with it.

The rollout itself was steady, and by the standards of education in emergencies, fast. A pilot opened in November 2021 with 10,000 children in grades six to nine — the cohort closest to ageing out of schooling altogether. By the 2023/24 school year the remaining grades — three to five, and grade ten — had opened, putting every age of a record enrolment of about 300,000 children under the Myanmar curriculum, across roughly 3,400 learning centres, 2,800 of them UNICEF-supported. The teaching corps reached about 8,000: some 3,900 from the Bangladeshi host community, the remainder Rohingya teachers from the camps themselves. By any operational measure this is a school system the size of a mid-sized city's. What it is not is a school system funded like one: no ministry's recurrent budget stands beneath it — only humanitarian appeals, renewed year to year. The distinction stayed theoretical until June 2025.

On 3 June 2025 classes stopped, with immediate effect, for every child from kindergarten to class four — the suspension reaching some 6,400 NGO-run centres and facilities, a broader counting unit than UNICEF's 3,400 supported centres, and the two counts should not be blended. About 1,100 teachers were dismissed in the first press count; UNICEF confirmed that the contracts of 1,179 host-community volunteer teachers had ended by 30 June. For the children still in class, the year's offer thinned by subtraction: kindergarten to grade two lost English, science and social studies for 2025-26, and no new textbooks or teacher guides were procured. The arithmetic behind the subtraction was published as it happened — the education response required $72 million for 2025 and had received less than $10 million; the whole Joint Response Plan sought $934 million and stood 32% funded in mid-July, OCHA's figures as carried by The Daily Star. A Rohingya teacher in Kutupalong camp, Ehsan Ullah, described to the paper what the interim timetable meant where he stood: classes five to seven meeting four days a month, classes eight to ten five days a week. His account reaches this page as the reporter rendered it — a limit noted below. UNICEF's June appeal put the education of almost a quarter of a million children at risk. A ministry cuts a curriculum by revision, with a gazette notice and a phase-in year; a budget cuts it by subtraction — the youngest first, then the subjects, then the paper.

The grades came back. By the following school year, after emergency fundraising, UNICEF reported all grades learning again — on the thinned offer, with the next funding cliff already flagged for early 2026. And then, in March 2026, the arrangement produced the event its designers had deferred: an examination. On 26 March the Examination Board of Rohingya Refugees — the EBRR, a community-run body, not a state one — held the camps' first matriculation examinations under the Myanmar framework, the papers distributed with the cooperation of the camp authorities and the Armed Police Battalion, as reported by Kaladan Press, a Rohingya community outlet. The candidates sat a matriculation their own teachers built for them, because no ministry would. The certificates they earned are, as of this writing, recognized by no state: not by Bangladesh, whose curriculum it is not; not by Myanmar, whose curriculum it is, but whose institutions have stamped nothing issued in the camps. The third link of the chain — the state that honours the paper — has not arrived, and the community has answered by building the second link itself and holding the results in trust. Credential limbo is no longer a policy-brief hypothetical; it has a graduating class.

Two honest limits sit on this account, and a story about unrecognized paper should not trade on borrowed authority itself. The first is whose voice it carries. The operator closest to the classroom here — Ehsan Ullah, named with role and place in The Daily Star's reporting — reaches the record in paraphrase: the source carries no quotation marks, so none appear here, and we have not dressed his account as speech. The system's day-to-day operators — centre teachers, the board's examiners — are the voices this record still owes its readers, and coverage of the coming school year, or of the EBRR's first results, may yet supply them. The second is arithmetic: the count of learning centres differs by counting unit — roughly 3,400 UNICEF-supported in 2023, some 6,400 NGO-run facilities in the 2025 suspension coverage — and we have reported the two separately rather than average them into a number nobody published.

Read end to end, the mechanism is uncomfortably clean. A host state chose a curriculum for a future it declared; humanitarian budget lines were left to operate that choice in the present; and when the budget shrank, the present overruled the future line by line — first the youngest classes, then the subjects, then the textbooks, then more than a thousand teachers. The choice itself never changed hands: through five years, one suspension and one resumption, the children of the camps have stayed on Myanmar's syllabus, waiting on Myanmar's recognition, schooled at Bangladesh's discretion, on money that belongs to neither. In March a community board gave the arrangement its first graduating class, and gave the question its sharpest form. A curriculum chosen for return has now produced its first certificates. What it has not yet produced is a country that will read them.
