On 4 December 2024, a little over 2.1 million students sat the same exercise on the same day, in 74,229 schools across 781 districts of India — and the most consequential thing about the exercise was the change in what it asked. The PARAKH Rashtriya Sarvekshan, successor to the National Achievement Survey, reads the school system at Classes 3, 6 and 9, the joints where its redesigned stages meet, and its items read less like a syllabus checklist than like small demands on competence: put the numbers up to 99 in order; take a percentage or a fraction and make it work on a problem from ordinary life. Behind the changed question stands a changed questioner. PARAKH — Performance Assessment, Review and Analysis of Knowledge for Holistic Development — is the national assessment regulator India constituted in 2023 under NCERT, the centre its 2020 education policy mandated; its remit runs from the survey at the top of the system down to the report card in a child's schoolbag. In the two years since, it has redrawn both ends.

The survey's scale was announced in advance at a target of about 2.3 million students across some 88,000 schools; the measured sitting came in at 21.15 lakh — 2.1 million — across 74,229. The gap matters less than the habit it reveals: a system beginning to put its planned figures and its achieved ones on the record together. What PARAKH did next with the reading is the design choice the exercise will be remembered for. When the national report was released in July 2025, the regulator did not stop at a country-level headline. It pushed the results down into a public dashboard that now carries a report card for the nation, one for each of 36 states and union territories, and one for each of the 781 districts — the tier of the system close enough to individual schools to act on what its card says. A national average is a news story; a district report card is an assignment.

The cards carry uncomfortable arithmetic, and the discomfort has a shape: it deepens as children move up the school. In Class 3, the national average in language is 64 per cent — a figure the report itself sets two points above the 2021 round of the survey it replaced — and 60 per cent in mathematics. By Class 6, language has slid to 57 and mathematics to 46, with the integrated subject The World Around Us at 49 — below half in everything except language. By Class 9, language stands at 54 and mathematics at 37, the weakest cell in the whole survey. The spine of the dataset is the mathematics line — 60, 46, 37 — a subject shedding more than a third of its national average between a child's third and ninth years of schooling. Beneath the averages sit floors that make them concrete: 55 per cent of Class 3 students could put the numbers up to 99 in order, which means nearly half could not; 28 to 31 per cent of Class 9 students could apply percentages and fractions to real-life problems, which means roughly seven in ten could not. And the survey published its gradients rather than smoothing them: students in state-government schools score lower across subjects, as do Scheduled Caste and Scheduled Tribe students, and the gaps widen by Class 9. The fall, in other words, is not evenly distributed — and the country can now read exactly where it lands.

It is worth being precise about what such an exercise can and cannot do. A survey moves no teacher, funds no classroom, teaches no child fractions. What it changes — all it changes — is what the system sees, and how publicly. On that score the redesign is thorough: the question changed, to competences read at the stage joints; the resolution changed, to 781 district cards where a headline once served; and the audience changed, because a dashboard anyone can open turns a finding from a ministry's property into a public fact. Whether seeing becomes doing is the part no assessment can guarantee, and PARAKH's own instrument will eventually be the judge of it: the next national round is expected on a roughly three-year cadence — which would put the re-measure around 2027 — though no date has been announced. Until then, the 37 stands.

What makes India's case more than a survey story is that the same regulator has been redrawing the ruler at the other end of the system — the one a parent actually holds. The Holistic Progress Card, designed by PARAKH and published in stage versions from the foundational years through secondary, is built to replace the marks-only report card. It is a 360-degree instrument: alongside academic attainment it records a child's progress across cognitive, socio-emotional and psychomotor domains, and it builds self-assessment and peer-assessment into the card itself, so that the child and her classmates are among the instrument's authors. The scope claim should be kept exact — the HPC replaces the school's report card, not the board examination — but within that scope the redefinition is real. A marksheet answers one question: what did this child score? The new card asks a different one: how does this child learn, work and grow — and who has observed it? A marksheet cannot record that a child persists, or collaborates, or works better with her hands than her memory; a card built on three domains and four observers at least has somewhere to write it down.

Prof. Indrani Bhaduri, the CEO and head of PARAKH, set out the theory of the change in an authored piece last July. "Assessment is no longer seen as a terminal act of evaluation but a continuous and inclusive process," she wrote. The HPC, in her words, is "replacing the one-dimensional report card system"; it "tracks the learner's progress across cognitive, socio-emotional, and psychomotor domains." The regulator's theory now meets federal reality, because in India a card is adopted state by state or not at all. By one press count from April 2025, 26 states and union territories had adopted or adapted the HPC for kindergarten through Class 8, and the card is being translated into 26 languages — a figure worth carrying as reported rather than audited. The implementation has the texture of workshops and translations: district teacher-training institutes as far from Delhi as Kargil ran HPC sessions with PARAKH through the second half of 2025. There is no national switch-on; there is a card, and each state is answering it at its own speed.

Read together, the two redrawings are one reform. The survey changed what the state sees when it looks down the system: not a rank but a gradient, not a headline but 781 local assignments. The card changes what a family sees when it looks at one child: not a total but a profile. Both bets share a failure mode, and it is the same one — paper. A district card no district officer acts on is a PDF; a holistic card a teacher fills in from memory at term's end is a marksheet with more boxes. The honest reading of mid-2026 is that the seeing is real, public and uncomfortable, while the doing remains uneven and state-by-state — and that this is a sequence, not a scandal. A survey changes what a system sees before it changes what a system does. India has done the first at full scale and in the open. The second is now a matter of record: the next card, and the next survey, will show whether anyone acted on the last ones.
