The first fact to learn about the Sudan School Certificate Examination, as it has run since the war began in April 2023, is what time it starts: half past two in the afternoon. The hour was not chosen for anyone's comfort — a paper that ends at five releases its candidates into early dark — but for a reason no peacetime timetable ever had to weigh: so that a candidate in a hall in Kassala and a candidate at a consulate school in Aswan, in another country, would open the same paper at the same moment. The examination itself is old machinery: supervised by the General Directorate for Evaluation and Examinations of the Federal Ministry of General Education, its results validated by the Sudan Examination Board, it has gated entry to Sudanese universities and the labour market for generations. Since December 2024, that machinery has run the exam three times without a functioning country around it. The mechanics, not the adversity, are the story.

The first war-time sitting, on 28 December 2024, was an act of backlog administration: it examined the cohort of 2022/23, whose exam had waited two years. The ministry's registration tallies, announced as the sitting opened, sketch the operation's shape. Inside Sudan: 343,644 registered candidates — 120,724 of them from war-affected states — across roughly 2,300 centres, concentrated in the safer states, with exam fees waived and transport, accommodation and meals arranged for displaced candidates. Beyond the border: more than 42,000 candidates in 46 centres across 15 countries, around 28,000 of them in Egypt alone, in 25 centres run through consulates. Registration, in the sector's own account, "was open for all children regardless of their geographical locations" — a single national register, wherever the candidate stood. A catch-up round followed in March 2025 for candidates from areas the December sitting could not reach. All of these are announced figures: a ministry counting what it registers and intends, not yet what happened.

The fullest operator's account of that first sitting comes from Ibrahim Adam Idris, a Sudanese educator and former primary-school teacher who serves as the INEE Country Focal Point for Sudan, writing on the network's site in January 2026. It is Idris who records the clock — "In order to align with students taking the exam outside the country, the Ministry adjusted the exam timings to start at 2:30 PM and end at 5:00 PM" — and records its cost: dark falls soon after five in Sudan, "so travel home was more difficult and dangerous." He records what paid for the margins of the operation: communities mobilised through "Nafeer," the traditional volunteer fundraising practice, covering meals, transport between home and centre, and protection for displaced candidates. And he records what the paper was worth in distance: one candidate travelled 2,662 kilometres, from Abéché in Chad back to River Nile State, to sit it. A machine people will cross a border to reach is a machine that still means something.

What raises the operation from improvisation to system is that each sitting closes its books in public. The second war-time sitting ran from 29 June to 10 July 2025, for the 2024 batch, in 2,018 centres inside and outside Sudan; on 20 October 2025 the ministry announced the ex-post tally: 222,393 registered, 193,809 sat, 132,817 passed — a pass rate of 68.5% — with results posted to an official portal, result.sudani.sd, where a candidate anywhere on earth can query a number. The sitting's top-ranked candidate, at 97%, sat the paper at the Sudanese Consulate School in Aswan: the diaspora machinery producing not only candidates but the year's first name. Two disciplines belong on this ledger. Press summaries of the same sittings circulate slightly different counts, so we have used the ministry's own registered-sat-passed series throughout and left the variants aside. And the ministry's web surfaces are themselves war-degraded — the official announcement page did not render when we fetched it — so the load-bearing counts here are ministry figures as carried by the regional press, anchored to the results portal that does resolve. On a story about a state keeping its registers, the state of the registers belongs on the record too.

The third sitting opened on 13 April 2026, and its announced scale is of a different order: some 564,000 registered candidates across 3,333 centres inside and outside Sudan — 226,684 of them from the war-delayed batches, 222,404 from the class of 2024. Against the same ministry series before the war — 480,519 sat in 2021, 518,712 in 2022 — the number is larger than any peacetime year, and the reason is the point: this is not a cohort, it is a queue. Khartoum state alone registered 148,842 candidates, roughly five times the year before, as the capital's students came back within the system's reach. Abroad, the geography widened: 38,000 examinees in Egypt, around 3,000 in Uganda, and centres in Chad — which had declined to host the December 2024 sitting, leaving some 10,000 refugee candidates waiting; by April 2026 Darfuri candidates were registered to sit there. A door that was closed at the first sitting is open at the third, and both facts belong in the record. Host-state sittings also inherit host-state conditions: in the UAE, the Sharjah centre was postponed to May days before the start, under national restrictions on in-person schooling, with more than 1,200 candidates affected — each of whom had paid a 450-dirham exam fee. The 2026 results, as of this writing, are not yet announced; when they come, they will be the third audited line in the war-time register.

All of this logistics serves one conversion: the certificate is the paper against which Sudanese universities admit, and it competes with nothing. Recognition, not printing, is the system's real product — which is why the one dispute worth reporting here is a recognition dispute. In areas beyond the ministry's reach, a parallel administration has announced school-leaving exams of its own for June 2026; the federal ministry's position is that such results carry no recognition for university entry. We report that as a fact about certification, not a judgement about anyone's politics: a certificate is a promise made by a register, and a register is only a register while it is singular. The system's stated limit sits on the same public record: by the count of the National Initiative to Support Sudanese Exams — a support initiative's estimate, not a ministry figure — roughly 280,000 students in areas the sittings cannot reach have now been unable to sit for a third year. The machine publishes its reach; the gap is the machine's own number for what it has not yet done.

Continuity, in the end, is not quite the right word for what is being protected; synchrony is. Any administration can reschedule an examination. What Sudan's exam directorate has protected, three sittings running, is the property that makes a certificate a certificate: that everyone who holds one sat the same paper, under the same clock, on the same register, wherever the map had moved them. The 2:30 start is the whole philosophy compressed into one administrative detail — a state that could no longer gather its candidates in one place gathered them instead in one hour. Universities do not admit against courage; they admit against a stamp that must go on meaning one thing. Keeping the stamp singular, across 15 countries and half a million queued candidates, is among the quietest functions a state performs — and, on the evidence of the queue, among the most trusted.

— *The Editors*
