Afrah Ali is thirty-three, from the district of Ja'ar in Yemen's southern governorate of Abyan, and she teaches Islamic studies at Al-Thawra School as a volunteer — a word that, in the vocabulary Yemen's long war has built, means a teacher who is not on the public payroll. In August 2024 she told UNICEF what keeps her in the classroom, and her accounting was exact. "The reason I continue to volunteer is my love for the job," she said, "but that would not be enough without the financial support provided by UNICEF. The war has caused too many students to drop out and has cost others valuable school time due to teacher strikes. Our presence has helped mitigate those problems and keep girls in school." The sentence rewards slow reading, because it is the whole machinery of Yemeni schooling in miniature: the devotion is hers; the money is wired from somewhere else; and neither, alone, would hold the door open. For most of a decade, the operative question in Yemen has not been how to improve schools. It has been what, precisely, keeps a school open when the state stops paying the people inside it — and Yemen's answer, the most fully documented anywhere, comes in layers.

The absence beneath the layers is counted and dated. In late 2016 the central bank was relocated from Sanaa to Aden, and the public payroll split with the country's administration: in areas under the Sanaa authorities, public salaries have gone largely unpaid since; in areas under the internationally recognised government, they arrive irregularly, in a currency that keeps giving up its value. UNICEF's standing estimate put roughly 172,000 teachers — two-thirds of the workforce — on irregular pay or none since 2016. The UN's humanitarian needs and response plan for 2026, released at the end of March, sharpened the count: 193,668 teachers, nearly two-thirds of the national total, now receive no salary at all, in a sector where 6.6 million school-age children are already out of education and 2,375 school buildings stand damaged or destroyed. The remarkable fact — the one this piece exists to trace — is that most of those teachers are still teaching.

The first layer above the missing payroll was an audited machine. The Restoring Education and Learning project — REAL, in the World Bank shorthand — was financed by the Bank and the Global Partnership for Education and implemented by UNICEF, the World Food Programme and Save the Children alongside the education ministry, and it was built on the admission most aid design avoids: that the binding constraint on Yemeni schooling is not the textbook or the classroom but the teacher's income. What it paid, it did not call a salary. It paid performance-based payments, conditional on being in the classroom, and salary allowances for rural female teachers — a regular payment that lets a woman go on teaching in a village school no other teacher will reach. By the project's close, its results tables record performance-based payments reaching 39,047 teachers against a target of 12,000, and 2,135 rural female teachers on salary allowances against a target of 2,000; some US$148.4 million had moved through five credits and trust funds; all five of the project's development-objective indicators finished above target. Two things set these figures apart from the ordinary run of aid claims. They live in implementation status reports — the Bank's audited results surface — rather than in press releases. And they attach to named people: the UNICEF component carried incentives for 2,600 volunteer teachers standing in for a depleted profession, and Afrah Ali's "financial support provided by UNICEF" is a line in that ledger.

Beneath the audited layer sit two more, best seen from Mukalla, the southeastern port city from which Al Jazeera reported in April on how the arithmetic works from below. Layer two is the governorate decree. With the national government unable to raise public pay, some governors have approved monthly incentives from local budgets: 25,000 riyals — about $16 — in Hadramout, and between 30,000 and 50,000 riyals elsewhere. "The incentives provided by local authorities vary from one province to another, depending on each governor's priorities and capacity to support teachers in their region," Abdullah al-Khanbashi, who heads the teachers' union in Hadramout, told the network. Layer three is not a policy at all. In Marib, the union head Abdullah al-Bazeli described the province's farmers keeping teachers in front of classrooms with produce: "Farmers support teachers, especially those coming from outside the province, by giving them tomatoes, potatoes and other vegetables for free." And under every layer runs the teacher's own labour, resold by the hour. Mohammed Salem, thirty-one years in the profession, teaches his morning shift at a government school in Mukalla, an afternoon shift at a private one, then works a hotel job into the night; his earnings have fallen from the equivalent of $320 a month before the war to $130. "If I had any spare time for a fourth job, I would take it," he said. Hana al-Rubaki, a part-time teacher and the only earner for her mother and three sisters, gave the ledger its bottom line: "After taxes, my salary is just 70,000 Yemeni riyals [$44] a month. With the high cost of living, it feels more like a token allowance than a real salary." She also named the constraint the job-stacking strategy hides: "While some teachers can find additional work to support their families, it is incredibly difficult for us female teachers to do the same." The riyal explains why every riyal-denominated layer thins between decree and payday — from about 215 to the dollar before the war to about 2,900 by mid-2025, before a partial recovery to around 1,560 in government-area trading. A governor's 25,000-riyal gesture is sixteen dollars, until it is less.

The hinge of the story is a date: 30 June 2025, the day the audited layer switched off. REAL closed with its targets exceeded. Its announced successor — the Sustaining Education and Learning project, SEAL, a US$10 million grant approved on 17 June 2025, again implemented through UNICEF — is roughly one-fifteenth the financial size of what it follows, and its announced scope runs to school rehabilitation, classrooms and water points, community-managed school grants and the ministry's education-data systems. Teacher payments do not appear in it. Whether they will appear — whether any donor instrument picks up the incentive line at its old scale — is an open question the public documents do not yet answer, and this account will not answer it by speculation. What the arithmetic already on the table does say is narrow and hard: the strongest payment layer Yemen's teachers had reached 39,047 of them at its height, about one in five of the 193,668 the UN now counts as unsalaried — and that layer has closed with nothing of its scale announced behind it.

One seam in the record should be shown rather than smoothed. The teachers who personify the machine's success — Afrah Ali; the volunteer physics teacher Fatima Al-Mashriqi, who credits volunteers with cutting her school's dropout rate; the principals who praise them — reach the public record through UNICEF, the machine's own implementer, in its own storytelling. The quotes are real; the selection is the project's. The 2026 voices reached the record the other way, through independent reporting, and they speak in a different key: sit-ins, marches and strikes across government-held areas that have cost months of schooling — the same strikes Afrah Ali counted among the reasons volunteers are needed — and a union head saying protests will continue until pay is better and regular. Both registers are true, and a reader should know in which register each name arrives. One further discipline: reports circulate of stipend schemes stopping and starting in areas under the Sanaa authorities. They do not stand on primary surfaces this publication can check, and they are not carried here.

Read from the bottom, then, the stack that keeps a Yemeni school open is: tomatoes in Marib; a hotel shift in Mukalla; a governor's 25,000 riyals; and a results table in Washington that wired payments to 39,047 teachers and now belongs to a closed project. Yemen's lesson is not that such a stack can be built — it visibly can, and it has held a system upright for years. The lesson is what the stack costs: every layer is discretionary, every layer is smaller than the need, and the largest layer carried a closing date from the day it opened. A payroll is many things a stack of workarounds is not, and the plainest is this: it does not have to be renewed by somebody's decision every June.
