In 2014, Chad's Ministry of National Education and Civic Promotion took a decision that would not be tested at full scale for nine years: refugee students in Chad would be schooled not beside the national system but inside it — attending Chadian schools, following the Chadian curriculum. In 2018 the country's education sector plan went further and converted the camp schools themselves into Chadian public schools; by 2020 the Stratégie Éducation 2030 had written full inclusion into standing sector policy. None of this was drafted against any particular emergency, which is precisely what made it an instrument rather than a response. So when more than 876,000 Sudanese refugees crossed into eastern Chad after April 2023 — an estimated 61% of them children — one of the poorest school systems on earth met one of the largest school-age influxes in its region's history with a policy that already existed. This piece is about the ledger of that absorption: what a host system counts when it decides, in advance, that arriving children are pupils.

Absorption, in the Chadian design, is not a programme layered over the school system; it is enrolment. More than 100 camp schools have been converted into official Chadian public schools across 19 camps and sites. More than 1,700 refugee teachers have been trained to teach the Chadian curriculum — Sudanese adults retrained to deliver the host's syllabus, the quiet inverse of every parallel-system instinct. And more than 163,000 Sudanese refugee children and youth now have access to education in Chad, enrolled in public schools and following the Chadian curriculum, on UNICEF's operational count as of September 2025. The state doing this hosts more than 1.5 million refugees in all, about half of them of school age, and spends 16.5% of government expenditure on education — above the average for the region's low- and middle-income countries — out of one of the tightest budgets anywhere. The commitment is easy to under-see because its form is so ordinary: a desk, a Chadian syllabus, a teacher paid (when the pay comes) by the system rather than the emergency.

The ledger's longest line is an examination line. Since 2015, 12,348 Sudanese refugees have sat the Chadian baccalauréat — the host's own school-leaving examination, on the host's curriculum — and 5,919 have passed it. The pathway predates this war entirely. It means that for a decade there has been a route by which a Sudanese teenager in an eastern Chadian camp finishes school as, in certification terms, a graduate of the Chadian system, holding the paper that opens what that system opens. Twelve thousand candidates is not a footnote; it is a cohort of alumni, and it is the strongest evidence that Chad's inclusion policy is a machine that runs, not a communiqué.

Then, for nine days in late 2025, the machine ran briefly in the other direction. Between 25 August and 2 September, close to 5,000 Sudanese refugee students sat Sudan's own school certificate — the examination Khartoum's directorate has kept running through the war — at seven centres across the provinces of Ouaddaï, Sila and Wadi Fira. The machinery on the host side was a Chadian interministerial ad hoc committee — Higher Education, National Education and Civic Promotion, Social Action — working with the Sudanese Embassy and Consulate, UNICEF, UNHCR and a set of national and international NGOs. The support ran from exam-preparation sessions and learning materials to transport between camps and centres, accommodation, psychosocial support and, critically, coordination with education authorities so that the results would be recognised. These candidates were finalists stranded between systems — students who had fled within two years of their certificate and could not reasonably start again from scratch on another curriculum. "When we left Sudan because of the war, I never thought I would have the chance to continue my studies," said Musdélifah Adam Arbab, 18, sitting the exam at the Abéché centre, in UNICEF's account of the round. The committee's framing carried its own quiet clause: this was a one-time initiative. Whether it recurs is, as of this writing, unresolved.

Hold the two examination lines side by side and the deepest question in refugee schooling comes into focus, sharper here than almost anywhere: whose curriculum — and therefore, whose future. Chad's standing answer is integration: the Chadian syllabus, the Chadian bac, a certificate that works where the child now lives. The August exception answered the other need: the Sudanese certificate, the paper that counts for Sudan's universities and for an eventual return. Over a decade, 12,348 took the host's track; in one round, 5,000 needed the home country's. Neither line refutes the other. A host system can hold its own door open — Chad holds it wider than systems many times richer — but it cannot decide for a displaced family which country's future to enrol a child in. That tension is not a flaw in the design; it is the honest shape of the problem, and Chad's ledger is one of the few places where both answers are written down with numbers attached.

The same ledger keeps its debit column in public view. By UNHCR's operational reckoning, roughly two-thirds of school-age refugee children in Chad remain out of school: inclusion is the policy and coverage is still partial, and both facts are true at once. The corps holding the classrooms is itself the system's soft point: around 38,000 community teachers — locally recruited, modestly paid and often paid months late — make up about 65% of Chad's entire teaching workforce, on the country's own 2025 statistical yearbook. The financing story is where the next chapter will be decided: the World Bank–UNHCR INSPIRE programme is explicitly funding the transition from parallel humanitarian schooling to national-system inclusion, including plans to bring teachers in refugee-hosting areas into the sector's own budgeting, and the government convened its partners in N'Djamena in November 2025 to reinforce the commitment. On all the evidence, the binding constraint is money, not policy — in the landscape of refugee education, the rarer and the more fixable problem.

One voice is missing from this account, and it is better stated than papered over: no serving Chadian school-level operator — a head teacher in a converted camp school, an officer of the examinations machinery, a provincial education delegate — is on the public record for this story since 2024, and the nearest voices are pseudonymised or institutional, used here as labeled texture rather than testimony. A camp headmaster whom the Jesuit Refugee Service called "Saleh," in 2023, said simply: "There are many children in small classrooms." Chad's own education-in-emergencies division, in an unnamed official's words recorded by UNHCR, said the system needs more money and more partners to sustain a sufficient teaching population. The account would be stronger with a named Chadian schoolmaster in it; the public record, so far, has not produced one.

What Chad has built is easy to under-praise because it is defined by an absence: no separate system, no second register, no refugee track with its own diplomas and its own dead ends. A decade ago, a state with almost nothing to spare decided that a child who arrived would be a pupil, full stop — and when the largest displacement on its border arrived, the decision held. The absorption is incomplete, underfunded, and carried on the shoulders of teachers paid late. It is also the most complete answer any system in the region has given to the question the decade keeps asking: not whether the children will come, but what kind of school will be standing when they do.
