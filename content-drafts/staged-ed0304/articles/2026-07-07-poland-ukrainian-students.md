On the first of September 2024, a Ukrainian child living in Poland stopped being someone the school system was open to and became someone it was owed. An amendment to the special act on assistance to citizens of Ukraine, adopted that May and effective with the new school year, extended Poland's school obligation — the obowiązek szkolny that binds every Polish family — to refugee children from Ukraine, and attached a price to staying away: the state child benefit known as 800+, and the Dobry Start school grant with it, would be paid only for a child attending a school in the Polish system. One exception was carved out, for one year, for the final-year cohort finishing Ukrainian school online. Everyone else was expected at a Polish door. For two and a half years before that morning, Poland had run the opposite design — an open door with no duty to walk through it — and the register had begun to say something awkward: more than 152,000 refugee pupils in Polish schools at the end of September 2022, then roughly 146,800 a year later. The count was not growing; it was thinning, while tens of thousands of children sat out the Polish system in Ukrainian online classrooms — some 58,000 as of April 2023, by the Centre for Citizenship Education's count. In September 2024 the state flipped the default. What follows is the arithmetic of the flip — which Poland, unusually, has audited in public.

The ministry braced for a wave it could not size. Asked how many new pupils the schools should expect, the ministry gave the fact-checking service Demagog an estimate of 50,000 to 80,000 — a number resting on the gap between children registered in the country and children registered at school. What arrived was smaller and more instructive. The education information system counted 160,200 pupils fulfilling the school obligation that autumn — 9% more than the year before. The Supreme Audit Office, NIK, whose report of 3 June 2026 is the nearest thing the mechanism has to a final grade, attributes roughly 13,000 additional pupils to the obligation-and-benefit machinery itself; much of the rest of the movement was already under way. Count children rather than pupils and the ledger widens — 195,288 including preschools by the end of September 2024; the two denominators should not be swapped. And the lever had a second output, of a kind ministries rarely publish: withholding the benefit from families whose children did not appear cut state spending by more than 101 million złoty. Two readings of one instrument — thirteen thousand children in, a hundred-odd million złoty not paid out — and the question of the design sits in the space between them.

The absorption machinery was regulation rather than rhetoric. An August 2024 ordinance raised the class-size ceiling in grades one to three to 29 pupils, making legal room for the newcomers; preparatory classes — the oddziały przygotowawcze in which new arrivals learn Polish before joining mainstream lessons — remained the entry chamber; and the intercultural assistant, who works the space between the child, the teacher and the parents, was written into the education law as a formal school role. Free Polish-language lessons were extended from 24 to 36 months, and pupils joining the last primary grade were excused the Polish-language paper of the eighth-grade exam. On the second day of the new regime, the principal of Szkoła Podstawowa nr 61 in Wrocław, Jacek Wildbret-Tuszyński, described what the machinery looks like from inside: "After the outbreak of the war in Ukraine a great many Ukrainian children came to my school, so I very quickly decided to open preparatory classes for them," he told TVN24 — our translation from the Polish. "At the end of last school year we had 156 children, but over the holidays another thirty or so enrolled." One school, thirty new names over one summer: the mechanism's marginal effect, seen from a corridor in Wrocław.

Then the mechanism did the thing worth watching: it moved out of the schools. Formal enforcement of a school obligation in Poland runs through administrative proceedings and a fine of up to 10,000 złoty — a route that exists for refugee families as for Polish ones, and not where this design placed its weight. From 1 June 2025 the verification moved to ZUS, the social-insurance agency: 800+ and Dobry Start are granted to holders of UKR status only where the education register confirms the child is fulfilling the school obligation in a Polish institution — alongside residence and economic-activity checks — with families re-applying each cycle, the first full checkpoint falling in February 2026. Attendance enforcement by benefits ledger, not truant officer. The audit counts the money: more than 101 million złoty withheld. An OKO.press reading of ZUS data counts the children: roughly 20,000 reported to have lost the benefit for non-attendance. The figures are two faces of one design — the state counting what it kept, the investigation counting whom the condition reached — and neither is a malfunction. Withholding is not the failure mode of a conditional benefit; it is the condition, operating — what a system buys, and pays, when it converts hospitality into obligation.

What did the operating buy? By 2025/26 the system had settled into a steady state the audit could measure: 111,726 refugee pupils in primary schools and 54,089 in post-primary — 165,815 in all, slightly above the year before — present in 57.8% of all Polish schools, 13,162 buildings out of 22,754. The bill to 31 March 2025: more than 7.8 billion złoty from the Fundusz Pomocy, the assistance fund that finances the schooling. The audit's central finding reads, at first pass, like a clean bill: the right to education was delivered, and in the schools NIK examined, no child was refused admission. The faults it lists sit one layer down, and they are all of a kind — preparatory-class rules not always followed; Polish-language hours under-delivered; teachers under-trained for the classrooms they now stand in. Read carefully, that is the seam in the design. The mechanism was built to produce enrolment, and enrolment is a compliance product: it can be commanded by statute, verified by a benefits agency, counted to the pupil. What the audit found wanting is the integration product — the language hours, the prepared teachers, the transition classes — which no benefit can be wired to, because no register records it.

The gap between the feared 80,000 and the measured 13,000 deserves its own reading, and the generous one is also the accurate one: the ministry was pricing an unknowable — how many of the children outside the system would surface inside it — and said so at the time. Forecast error of that kind is what planning under uncertainty looks like. The class-size headroom, mostly unneeded, cost little to hold. And the quieter numbers say the machine has settled rather than strained: enrolment in 2025/26 sits slightly above the obligation's first year, the February 2026 re-application checkpoint has passed without a second reckoning, and the ministry's support programmes — Szkoła dla wszystkich, Przyjazna szkoła — are funded through 2027. Poland planned for a flood, absorbed a stream, and kept the machinery.

Host systems with an enrolment gap argue, usually in the abstract, between invitation and obligation — keep the door open and wait, or extend the duty and enforce it. Poland is the rare case that ran the second policy at national scale and then sent its own auditors to read the result. The honest summary is double. The lever worked: thirteen thousand children the open door had not brought in are in school, and the state can show the mechanism that moved them. And the lever is not the school: everything the audit faulted begins after the register is taken — the language hours, the preparatory rules, the training of the person at the front of the room. A benefit can verify a child's presence. It has no way to verify what the presence is worth. Poland built the first machine and audited it; the second is the one the audit says still needs building.
