## Test 2 — Register fit. PASS.

A Gulf subject in analytic fusha, which is the register a careful Arabic editor at a Gulf publication would
use for a statistical reading of a Saudi system. No chamber-of-commerce register, no promotional diction,
and the piece's refusals are stated in Arabic as flatly as in English (*فهذه المادةُ لا تطرح 5 من −18*).

**Corrections 1 to 4, the grammar and terminology ones, were all in this test's territory:**

1. **كلتا الساعتين التي → الساعتين اللتين.** `كلتا` governs a dual, and the relative pronoun must be dual
   too. `التي` for a dual antecedent is a plain agreement error and it was in the piece's **first sentence
   after its first subheading**, which is where an Arabic reader decides whether the edition was composed or
   converted. Rewritten as *الساعتين اللتين تتحدّث عنهما هذه المادة*.
2. **مُلائَم → مُقدَّر**, twice, for a *fitted* regression. `مُلاءَمة` is the act of fitting a curve;
   the standing Arabic term for the resulting estimated line is **مُقدَّر**. This mattered more than a word
   choice usually does, because the whole piece turns on the difference between a line **estimated across
   cycles** and a **difference between two points**, and the imprecise term blurred exactly that.
3. **رقمَ عشرٍ → رقمَ عشر سنواتٍ.** `عشرٍ` alone names no unit. The piece's own standing rule is that every
   figure carries its span, and the Arabic had dropped the noun the span is counted in.
4. **The dek's apposition put in the accusative** — *خمسًا* for *خمسٌ*, in apposition to `تغيّرَين`. Measured
   after the change: **167/200**, inside the cap with the tashkeel counted at +1 each.

## Test 3 — The "originally written" test. PASS, after four corrections.

Read aloud, the Arabic carries its own sentence rhythm; the long analytic sentences break at Arabic joints
(`فـ`, `وـ`, `أمّا`) rather than at English ones. **Four English shadows were found and removed:**

5. **وقارئٌ سيأخذ القائمةَ → والقارئُ يأخذ القائمةَ.** An indefinite subject fronted before a future verb is
   English word order wearing Arabic words; the Arabic generalises with the definite article and the
   imperfect.
6. **تفصل بينهما → تَحكُم بينهما.** The English was *to break the tie*. `تفصل بينهما` says *separates
   them*, which is the literal act and the wrong sense — the missing third statement would **adjudicate**,
   not separate. A calque idiom, caught by reading the sentence for what it claims rather than against the
   English.
7. **A nominal chain lightened** — *وغيرُ قابلةٍ للمقابلة بأيّ تغيّرٍ* → *ولا تُقابَل بأيّ تغيّرٍ*. English
   prefers the nominalisation; Arabic prefers the verb.
8. **يَصوغان أخبارَهما بجافاسكربت → يَعرِضان.** `يَصوغ` is to *form* or *compose*; a site **renders** its
   news, and `يَعرِض` is the verb for it.
