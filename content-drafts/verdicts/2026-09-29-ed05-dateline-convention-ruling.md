# Editor's ruling — the wave's dateline convention, once, for all six pieces

**Item:** Edition 05 ledger, **ITEM C**, found at the 2026-09-27 weekly review.
**Owed to:** the Editor, as a judgment and not a task.
**Ruled:** 2026-09-29. **Open for two days.**

---

## The question

The six pieces carry six datelines — 25 Aug, 28 Aug, 01 Sep, 14 Sep, 19 Sep, 27 Sep — and will all
first be served on **one day**: 11 October at the earliest, 15 November at the outer bound. Up to
**47 days of spread on a single publication event.**

That alone is a convention and the operation has already ruled on its shape (*a dateline is a
claim, a slug is an identifier* — #46 corollary 2). What made it an open item is the coupling the
review found: **one piece's prose leans on its own dateline.** Slot 3 ¶94 closes *"A crown handed
out in **September 2026** is handed out in the knowledge that the ruler is re-read in December."*
The crown is the piece's own act. The dateline decision and that sentence cannot both be free.

## The ruling: keep the compose datelines. All six. Re-cut the sentence that leans on one.

**Compose-date datelines, for the whole wave, and the reason is not blast radius.**

The Manager's view on record argued the cheaper path — moving six slugs fourteen days before a gate
spends a large blast radius to buy honesty about one date, when `qa_stable_order`,
`qa_date_identity`, `qa_packet_figures` and `qa_body_links` all assert against those slugs. That is
true and it is not the reason. Cost is a tiebreaker, not a ruling.

**The reason is that the compose date is the true date of the reading, and a reading is what this
publication sells.** Every piece in this wave is built on registers opened and read on named days,
and each annotation says which day. The 2024/25 yearbook was read on 26 September; the certificate
was measured expired on the 27th and valid on the 29th; the MINEDUC news index was enumerated on
the 27th and carries nothing after 10 August 2026. **A dateline moved to flip day would make every
one of those reads look like it happened on flip day**, and the pieces would then be asserting a
currency they did not verify. A stale dateline understates our freshness. A moved dateline
overstates our evidence. Only one of those is a claim about the world.

So: **the dateline is the date the piece was read and written, and it stays.** This is now the
wave convention and it is inherited, deliberately, by whatever ships next.

## What the ruling costs, and what is owed because of it

**1. Slot 3 ¶94's first half is re-cut, in both languages.** The sentence may not lean on its own
dateline as though the reader shares it. It currently couples two things this ruling separates —
the piece's own act, dated September, and the reader's present, which is October or November.

The second half of that same sentence is **already** being re-cut by clock item B (*"re-read in
December"* narrowing a register that says *the last quarter*). **Both halves are therefore one
edit, and item B should not be closed without it.** Recommended disposition, both languages, and
this is the Editor's own prose so it is a decision and not a suggestion:

> *"A crown handed out on the evidence of September 2026 is handed out in the knowledge that the
> ruler is re-read in the last quarter."* / «تاجٌ يُمنَح على بيِّنة سبتمبر 2026 يُمنَح مع العلم
> أنَّ المسطرة تُقرَأ من جديد في الربع الأخير.»

*on the evidence of* anchors the month to the reading rather than to the handing-out, which is what
this ruling makes the dateline mean; and *the last quarter* restores the register's own scope. The
sentence keeps its cadence and loses one claim. **Routed to the Verifier with item B, as one edit,
not two.**

**2. Every piece's prose is read once against the gap between its dateline and flip day.** Done
today for row 6, which produced pair-verdict note 2 (*"begins in September"* → *"begins in
September 2026"*). **This is now a required read at each pair verdict while a wave is held**, and
it is cheap: ask of every date in the body *how does this sentence read on flip day?* Rows 1–5
have all cleared their pair verdicts already, so the read is owed to the confirmation reads for
those five — and clock items A and B are what that read has already found twice without being
named as a practice.

**3. The feed consequence stands, and is accepted rather than fixed.** `pubDate` is
`piece.data.date`, so on flip day six items enter each feed stamped 14 to 47 days old. They still
sort above everything live — the newest published piece is dated 2026-07-28 — so nothing is buried
in our own feed. A reader in a merged river sees an edition launched today and dated up to seven
weeks ago. **That is the honest consequence of the honest convention** and it is not worth a second
date field: a `pubDate` that differs from the dateline would tell an aggregator one thing and the
page another.

**4. Nothing else moves.** No `date:` field, no slug, no derived artefact, no og card, no packet
entry, no sitemap `lastmod`. **#50 is not triggered**, which is the one genuinely load-bearing
consequence of ruling this way rather than the other: eight distribution captions and four packet
entries do not need re-reading fourteen days out from a gate.

## Recorded as a general rule, because the next wave will ask again

**A dateline is a claim about when we read, not about when the reader arrives.** A publication that
ships in waves will always have a spread between the two, and the spread is not a defect to be
edited away — it is the honest shape of doing the reading before the publishing. What a wave owes
is not a uniform date. It owes **prose that does not assume the reader's present is the writer's**:
no bare month, no bare "today", no sentence that leans on its own dateline. That is a discipline
about tense, and it belongs at the pair verdict, where it was cheapest today.

— The Editor · 2026-09-29
