## What rides, with its page

**1. The headline, verbatim in substance (exec. summary, p. 1):**
> "In TIMSS 2019, South African Grade 9 learners achieved an average of **389 (SE 2.3)** on the mathematics assessment and **370 (SE 3.1)** on the science assessment. This amounts to an increase of **one standard deviation (104 TIMSS points for mathematics and 102 points for science) between the 2003 and 2019 cycles.**"

**2. The Grade 9 trend series (Figure 7, p. 18) — the spine of the climb:**

| Cycle | Grade | Average scale score (SE) | Score difference (5th–95th percentile) |
|---|---|---|---|
| TIMSS 2003 | Gr 9 | **285 (4.2)** | 320 |
| TIMSS 2011 | Gr 9 | **352 (2.5)** | 287 |
| TIMSS 2015 | Gr 9 | **372 (4.5)** | 286 |
| TIMSS 2019 | Gr 9 | **389 (2.3)** | 252 |

389 − 285 = **104**. The arithmetic closes against the report's own headline; no averaging, no mirror.

**3. The decomposition, in the report's words (p. 18):** +67 (2003→2011), +20 (2011→2015), +17 (2015→2019). "The achievement differences between each of the different cycles were **statistically significant**."

**4. The deceleration — mandatory companion, in the owner's own voice (p. 18):** average annual improvement 2003–2019 was **six points**; split at the midpoint, **7.4 points per year** (2003→2011) and **4.6 points per year** (2011→2019). The report's framing: "The implication of the slowing down of the achievement improvement rate is that it would take the country longer to reach the achievement levels to which it aspires." The Principal Investigator says it plainly at launch: *the rate of achievement improvement is decreasing.*

**5. The floor, which the report supplies unprompted (p. 18):** the achievement range (5th–95th percentile) narrowed from **320 points (2003) to 252 (2019)**, −68; "the best achievement gains continued to be at the lower end of the achievement distribution"; and, against it, "since 2011, there has been very little improvement at the top end of the distribution curve."

**6. The ability levels (p. 18, prose):** **11 percent** of learners demonstrated basic mathematical knowledge in 2003; **41 percent** in 2019 — the report's word for the change is "almost fourfold."

## What does not ride, and why — the seam

The report constructs its 24-year line out of **two different grades**, and says so (Ch. 2, p. 17, and Study Design, p. 9):

> "Figure 7 presents the **Grade 8** average mathematics scale scores for the TIMSS 1995, 1999 and 2003 cycles, and the **Grade 9** average mathematics scale scores for the TIMSS 2003, 2011, 2015 and 2019 cycles."

> "The South African Grade 8 average mathematics scale scores were **not significantly different** in the TIMSS 1995, 1999 and 2003 cycles (Reddy et al., 2012). In 2003, we assessed learners in both Grade 8 and Grade 9. On average, **Grade 9 learners scored 21 points more than Grade 8 learners.** This 2003 assessment of both Grade 8 and 9 learners **provides the bridge** for the 1995 to 2019 trend line."

The Grade 8 arm, for the record (Figure 7): **276 (SE 6.7) in 1995 · 275 (SE 6.8) in 1999 · 264 (SE 5.5) in 2003.**

Three consequences, all fatal to the 0.9 SD claim as carried:

1. **The report never prints 0.9.** It prints *one* standard deviation, twice, and both times bounded **2003–2019**. A figure absent from the register does not ride (#29, #33).
2. **A 1995→2015 span crosses the grade seam.** 276 (Gr 8, 1995) → 372 (Gr 9, 2015) is +96, which is very likely where "0.9 SD" came from — but 21 of those points are the report's own measured **grade step**, not system improvement. The bridge is a construction the owner declares; a figure that crosses it and does not name it is reporting a grade difference as a policy result.
3. **The report's own verdict on that arm is null:** "from 1995 to 2003 there was **no significant change** in the average mathematics achievement scores, but from TIMSS 2003 to 2019 there was an increase of 104 points." Starting the clock in 1995 buys eight years of flat line and one grade step, and buys them at the cost of the only window the owner certifies.

**Editor's instruction implied by the read:** the piece carries **2003→2019, Grade 9, +104**, and carries it as *one standard deviation on the TIMSS scale*. If the drafter wants the longer arc, it is a sentence about **two arms and a bridge**, not a bigger number.

## A trap inside the figure that rides — "one standard deviation" is a scale unit

The report defines its metric on p. 14: *"The TIMSS achievement scale is summarised on a 0 to 1 000 scale, with a centrepoint of 500 and a **standard deviation of 100**."*

So "one standard deviation" is **100 points of the international scale, fixed by construction** — it is not an effect size computed on South African learners. South Africa's own spread in 2003 was 320 points between the 5th and 95th percentile. A reader who hears "one standard deviation" will hear *an effect size relative to the population described*; that is not what this is. The piece states it as the report states it — *104 points, which is one standard deviation on the TIMSS scale* — and never as "a one-SD effect."

This is the same shape as **#19 (a multiplier without its base)** and **row 20 (a deficit is a ratio)**: the unit is doing work the sentence is not showing. Third member of the scoped-instrument family to arrive via a ruler.

## Where the report and the launch disagree (#43 — recorded, not papered over)

Both are the owner's. They differ in one place, and the difference matters to a piece whose subject *is* measurement:

- **Benchmarks.** The press release: *"13% of mathematics and 15% of science Grade 9 learners reached the international intermediate benchmark."* The report's Figure 8 for 2019 reads **10 percent in the Intermediate band (475–550) and 3 percent High (>550)** — so 13 is **at or above** intermediate, not *in* it. The release compresses a cumulative share into a band name. **The piece uses neither figure**; the ability-level claim it carries is the 11→41 percent one, which both registers state identically in prose.
- **Country count.** The press release says *"Forty-six countries and entities participated at the grade 8 or 9 level"*; the report body says *"Thirty-nine countries and seven benchmarking participants"* (39 + 7 = 46). Not a contradiction — an aggregation. Per the 09-04 re-verification the count of **countries** is **39**, and the DBE 2023 Highlights' **59** refers to the 2023 cycle, a different year. **Any count in this piece names its cycle.**

**Carried forward, unresolved on purpose:** Figure 8's intermediate-year rows (2011, 2015) extracted from the PDF in scrambled order. The 2003 row (11 percent) and the 2019 row (41 percent) are confirmed against prose and ride; the 2011 and 2015 shares were reconstructed by arithmetic alone and **do not ride** (#41 — a figure is cited as read, never as computed from a pattern).
