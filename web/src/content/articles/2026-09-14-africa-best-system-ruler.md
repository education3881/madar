Somewhere there is a table with African countries in rank order and one column of scores beside them, and it is the least useful document on this subject. The problem is not its arithmetic. The problem is that a rank is an answer with its question deleted. Every ranking of school systems is a ruler someone chose, and the choosing is the whole argument; publishing the order while omitting the ruler is how a measurement becomes an opinion with decimal places.

This piece was commissioned to answer a plain question — which is the best education system in Africa? — and the honest answer research can give is that there are four defensible ways to measure it, that they crown four different countries, and that no two of them can be added together. What follows names each ruler, says what it can and cannot see, and hands out its crown.

## Ruler one: how much learning a child actually ends up with

The World Bank's Human Capital Index takes the years a child is expected to spend in school and discounts them by how much is learned in each one. On that ruler the crown is Mauritius's — handed out on a single country brief read against the regional averages the brief itself prints, not on a continental table. Its 2020 country brief recorded 12.4 expected years of school converting into 9.4 learning-adjusted years, a harmonised test score of 473 on a scale the brief bounds at 300 to 625, 98 of every 100 children surviving to age five, learning poverty of 40 percent on 2006 data against a regional 80, and education spending, in 2018, of 4.8 percent of GDP against a regional 4.0.

Two things must be said in the same breath. The first is the tense. That brief carries its own vintage statement — all its data represent the status of countries before COVID-19 — so every sentence above is past tense, and this piece makes no claim about Mauritian schools today. Pre-COVID is a floor and not a date: the brief prints a year beside each complementary indicator, and the learning-poverty reading is 2006, fourteen years older than the brief that carries it. The second is what the index cannot see. The brief states plainly that a lack of data prevents comparison of the index by gender; the components are a different matter, and there the brief does disaggregate, giving 12.2 expected years for boys and 12.7 for girls, while leaving the test score and the learning-adjusted years undisaggregated. A ruler that cannot be read by sex is not a ruler that has found equality.

## Ruler two: learning relative to what a country can afford

The second ruler asks a different question: not how much a system produces, but how much it produces given its means. It is the one most often invoked and least often named. It exists because of a conversion — the Harmonized Learning Outcome scale, built by Patrinos and Angrist, which puts scores from different assessments onto one axis so they can be plotted against income.

On that plot, the CGD analyst Alexis Le Nestour found that Gabon, Senegal, Benin and Burkina Faso cleared 410 and sat just above the global median, that Chad, Madagascar, Niger, the DRC and Côte d'Ivoire came in under 360 and sat among the bottom thirty countries in the world, and that the francophone results overall were, in his words, not below what we would expect considering their level of development. He spent two years as a technical adviser to PASEC before writing it, which makes him about as informed a secondary as a secondary gets — and a secondary is what he remains. This ruler is his construction, not the assessment's. PASEC's own report runs its own income analysis, against GDP growth rather than GDP per capita, and finds no correlation at all. Those are two different claims, and only the first crowns anybody.

So ruler two crowns all four that cleared 410 — Gabon, Senegal, Benin and Burkina Faso — and it does so on an analyst's axis rather than a measurement. The band is where his register stops: he places the four above the global median, says of the francophone results as a group that they are not below what we would expect, and never separates the four from one another. Naming two of them would be our narrowing, not his. Punching above one's weight is always a residual: it is what is left over after a model has explained what it can, and it belongs to the model.

## Ruler three: how fast a system is improving

Improvement is the ruler that produces the most surprising crowns, because it rewards the systems that started lowest.

On PASEC's own scale, the crown is Niger's, and by a wider margin than most accounts of it allow. Across the ten countries that sat both the 2014 and 2019 rounds, Niger recorded the largest gain in all four trend series — language and mathematics at the start of primary, reading and mathematics at the end. Among pupils tested in French, its start-of-primary language mean rose 76.9 points and its mathematics 89.2; at the end of primary, reading rose 67.5 and mathematics 56.0, and in mathematics at that level Niger and Benin were the only two countries whose gains were statistically significant at all. That start-of-primary gain was not evenly shared: on the same page, Niger's weakest tenth gained 51.6 points in language against 139.3 for its strongest — the shape ruler four finds nearly everywhere. The qualifier about French is not decoration. The trend comparison covers ten countries, not the fourteen in the 2019 cross-section, and it leaves out the Niger pupils tested in Hausa and Zarma along with the Chad pupils tested in Arabic, because in 2014 both countries tested in French alone.

Beside the gain, the report prints what Niger did: it evaluated its contract teachers, who were 70 percent of the primary teaching force, and terminated the contracts of those who did not command the minimum competences, while training those who held the minimum but struggled with it. It reformed initial and continuing teacher training, set a specification document for each inspector, trained every teacher in the first three primary grades in reading and mathematics, made school directors accountable, added supplementary class hours for some pupils, and built a culture of assessment around start-of-year tests and a national evaluation body. Class sizes, meanwhile, did not shrink: Niger's end-of-primary average went from 39.6 pupils to 40.7 while eight of the ten countries fell and Congo rose.

That is a striking list, and it is worth being exact about what it is. It is a list, printed by the report in the same table as the arrows, with a footnote attached to the table saying that the direction of the performance change is given for information and does not indicate a causal link with the action named. Le Nestour's reading — that Niger bought learning with the quality of its teaching rather than with more teachers — is his inference and a reasonable one. It is not the assessment's finding. Nor, in this publication's voice, did Niger sack anyone: the instrument was contractual, and the report's word for it is termination.

On the global instrument, the crown is South Africa's. Its Grade 9 mathematics average on TIMSS went from 285 in 2003 to 389 in 2019 — 104 points, which the national report states is one standard deviation, decomposed as +67 to 2011, +20 to 2015 and +17 to 2019, with every cycle-to-cycle difference statistically significant. One standard deviation here means a fixed unit: the TIMSS scale is defined with a centrepoint of 500 and a standard deviation of 100 by construction, so this is 104 points of an international ruler, not an effect size computed on South African children, whose own spread between the fifth and ninety-fifth percentiles was 320 points in 2003.

The report supplies, unprompted and in the same two pages, both of the things a climb needs beside it. The improvement is decelerating: six points a year across the whole window, but 7.4 a year to 2011 and 4.6 a year after, and the report's own implication is that the country will take longer to reach the levels it aspires to. At the launch the study's principal investigator, Dr Vijay Reddy, put it as a caution rather than a report — she applauds the improvement, the release has her cautioning in the same breath, and the words are hers: the rate of achievement improvement is decreasing. And the floor moved the right way: that 320-point range narrowed to 252, with the best gains at the lower end of the distribution and, since 2011, very little improvement at the top. The share of learners demonstrating basic mathematical knowledge went from 11 percent to 41.

Then there is the seam, which is the reason the longer and more impressive version of this figure does not appear above. The report's trend line runs from 1995, and it is built from two arms: Grade 8 scores for 1995, 1999 and 2003, and Grade 9 scores for 2003 onward. In 2003 South Africa assessed both grades, and Grade 9 learners scored 21 points more than Grade 8 learners; that double assessment is what bridges the two arms, and the report says so. It also says that from 1995 to 2003 there was no significant change. So a span that begins in 1995 buys eight flat years and one measured grade step, and pays for them by abandoning the only window its owner certifies. A trend line can have a seam. Crossing one without naming it turns a grade difference into a policy result.

The currency note belongs here too. In the next cycle, TIMSS 2023, South Africa's Grade 9 mathematics reads 397 against 2019's 389 — and the Department of Basic Education's own report says those improvements were not statistically significant and that achievement levels have remained relatively stable, with a reliability reservation attached: the share of learners whose achievement was too low to estimate exceeds 15 percent, though it does not exceed 25. At Grade 5, South Africa's 362 was the lowest of the participating countries. Counts here belong to their cycles and travel with them: 39 countries and seven benchmarking participants in the 2019 grade-eight-or-nine cycle, 59 countries and six benchmarking entities in the 2023 fourth-grade one.

## Ruler four: whether the gains were shared

The fourth ruler is the one no aggregator can see, because it is not about the average at all. It asks whether a rising mean lifted the children at the bottom.

Applied to francophone Africa, the answer is mostly no. Benin is the report's own illustration: at the start of primary, its language mean rose 66.5 points, but the improvement plateaued at about 25 points for the weakest pupils while reaching nearly 130 for the strongest. Between-school inequality rose on average at every one of the four levels measured, which means that where a child goes to school explains more of what she scores than it used to, and the report explicitly rejects a rural-versus-urban widening as the explanation. Burundi was the only country to go backwards in both subjects at the end of primary.

But there is a crown, and it is the report's own sentence. Senegal, it says, appears as the only country that between 2014 and 2019 manages to reduce inequality of performance. The claim is made of reading, and it is grounded on a standard deviation that fell. Mathematics is a different cell: there the report finds two countries whose variability fell significantly — Senegal and Chad — and separates them. Senegal's weakest pupils gained most; Chad's spread narrowed because its strongest fell. At the end of primary, Senegal's reading standard deviation fell 15.6 points while its tenth percentile gained 54.7 against 5.2 at the ninetieth; in mathematics the standard deviation fell 12.9 and the tenth percentile gained 32.9 while the ninetieth lost 3.2; and the rural–urban gap in mathematics closed by 38.2 points. The report's own limits ride in the same paragraph: this is the end of primary only, and at the start of primary Senegal's spread shows no significant change.

Four rulers, four crowns. They cannot be averaged. A composite would require deciding how many points of Senegalese equity are worth one Mauritian learning-adjusted year, and that is not an arithmetic question.

## The ruler is about to change, and it has already been misquoted

PASEC 2019 is the latest published cycle of the continent's widest comparable measurement. PASEC 2024 has been collected, and according to a Beninese newspaper's report of the national teams' workshop in Cotonou this February, its results are announced for the last quarter of 2026, with a PASEC 2028 cycle named as the next step. The 2019 report anticipated exactly this, writing of the gains that they are a success which will have to be confirmed by the next PASEC study. A crown handed out in September 2026 is handed out in the knowledge that the ruler is re-read in December.

And the instrument has already provided a demonstration of this piece's own argument, at its own expense. An interview with CONFEMEN's secretary-general Abdel Rahamane Baba-Moussa was published under the headline that PASEC 2024 will be the last international evaluation. Read in the body, the sentence continues: the last international evaluation *before 2030*, the end point of SDG 4, with all the 2024 data to be mobilised for the stocktake in that year. The superlative was true and the horizon was in the next clause, and the headline kept the first and dropped the second. That is the same error as a rank without its ruler, performed on a single word.

Hilaire Hounkpodoté of CONFEMEN, which runs the francophone instrument, put it in 2018 — and about the 2014 round rather than this one — that despite progress in access to education, more schooling is not leading to more learning. That sentence is the reason all four of these rulers exist. Which one you reach for is not a technical preference. It is a statement about what you think school is for — and until you say which one you are holding, the word "best" has not yet made a claim.
