Most education technology is built in peacetime capitals and parachuted into emergencies, where it fails for reasons its designers never imagined. Sudan ran the sequence in the other direction. In 2014 — nine years before its own war would shutter nearly every school in the country — Sudan built, trialled and scaled a classroom that needs no school: a tablet, a solar panel, a set of educational games, and a trained adult from the community sitting beside the children. The instrument left home, was carried into other countries' emergencies, and by 2025 was serving roughly 125,000 children in Ukraine, Uganda and Lebanon. Then Sudan's own catastrophe arrived, and the question this piece follows is what happened to the idea in the place that invented it.

That is one question, and honesty requires saying at the outset that it is not answered by brand continuity. No served source connects the digital learning spaces running in wartime Sudan today to the programme Sudan invented a decade ago; they are two true stories, joined by place and by idea, and this piece keeps them joined by nothing more.

## The invention

The idea has a named origin. Kate Radford, who directs the programme it became, credits it to Dr. Aiman Badri of Ahfad University for Women in Khartoum — the programme's initial champion, in the account UNESCO published when it gave the work its prize. The problem Badri's idea addressed was brutally simple: Sudan had millions of out-of-school children whom the formal system was not going to reach on any believable timeline, many of them in villages where the nearest school was hours away on foot. The proposal was to stop waiting for the school and send the classroom instead.

The pilot, called e-Learning Sudan, began in 2014 as a partnership between UNICEF, the Ministry of Education, Ahfad University and the Dutch organisation War Child. Its design choices have aged remarkably well. Learning ran through games, offline, on tablet computers charged by the sun — a resource the country holds in surplus. The first phase, in UNICEF's own account of it, was a numeracy trial — an applied mathematics game delivering certified curriculum-level content — aimed at children aged roughly seven to nine who had never been inside a classroom; Arabic literacy was in the programme by the time the evaluation measured it. A community facilitator — not a certified teacher, but a trained local adult — guided each group. And the game worlds themselves were co-designed by the children, their drawings, Lego constructions and clay figures shaping the characters, villages and vocations on screen, on the theory that a child learns best inside a world she recognises.

What sets the Sudanese origin apart from most pilots, though, is not the design. It is that the country let the design be measured, and let the measurement say everything it said.

## The trial that reported its own bad news

In the evaluation of record — published in the *Journal of Development Effectiveness* in 2020 by Brown and colleagues — 221 out-of-school children aged seven to nine were followed for six months, with the programme measured not against nothing but against the state's own alternative education for out-of-school children. The headline findings favoured the invention: significantly greater improvements in mathematics competency, in Arabic literacy competency, and in psychological wellbeing, corroborated by thirty-three focus-group discussions and interviews.

The same abstract carries two findings that no marketing document repeats. On children's self-reported hope, the programme made no difference. On their self-reported self-esteem, the trial found a significant *negative* effect — the children in the programme reported worse, not better — accompanied by the authors' own caveat that the psychometric properties of those instruments were not adequate. Larger multipliers circulate in the programme's promotional material; they do not appear in the trial's abstract, and this publication has not read the paywalled body, so they do not appear here. What appears here is what the trial says in its own words: real gains in the two subjects and in wellbeing, a null on hope, a negative on self-esteem, and a caveat. An evaluation that publishes its own adverse findings is the reason the favourable ones can be believed — a principle this edition keeps meeting, from Zambia's unreported clauses to Sierra Leone's self-shrunk effect size.

The trial was persuasive where it needed to be. The programme scaled into Kassala state with a target of reaching 35,000 children over three years, run with the National Council for Literacy, and staffed by people like Fatima Abbas Tahir, a community facilitator who from September 2019 guided the tablet sessions in the village of Tamantai — 54 boys and 50 girls, learning to read in a place with no school to attend. In March 2019 UNESCO announced the programme — by then operating in Sudan, Uganda, Lebanon and Jordan under the name Can't Wait to Learn — as one of the 2018 laureates of its King Hamad Bin Isa Al-Khalifa Prize for the use of technology in education. The ambition its director stated to UNESCO in that same account — 1.5 million children by 2023 — should be read as what it was, an aim announced rather than a number reached. But the export itself is documented fact. By War Child's own 2025 accounting, Can't Wait to Learn served roughly 125,000 children that year in Ukraine, Uganda and Lebanon; in Ukraine, the platform was formally handed over to the government. An instrument first sketched from Sudanese children's clay figures had become another state's public infrastructure.

The instrument kept running at home, too, in quieter registers. In late 2022 — pre-war, and the vintage matters — a UNICEF story from Aroma village in Kassala described the model in its settled domestic form: the Badareer e-learning centre, sixty enrolled pupils aged six to fourteen inside a shelter the community built and maintains, tablets and solar power supplied by UNICEF, a twelve-member local committee tracking enrolment and dropouts, two sessions a day, five days a week, a certificate after the two-year cycle. Its facilitator, Tahir Onoor Ali, described children arriving unable to hold a pen and leaving two years later with basic skills across the subjects. The nearest formal school was seven kilometres away. The classroom that needs no school had become, in places like Aroma, simply how education happens.

## The war

In April 2023, Sudan's war began, and the country that had built the classroom for other people's catastrophes acquired its own. The denominators, as the system's own forward-looking register states them today: nearly half of all school buildings nationwide are no longer functioning, and at least eight million children are out of school, with the disruption especially acute in Darfur and Kordofan.

What UNICEF and its partners stood up inside that collapse are called Makannas — from the Arabic for "our space," by UNICEF's own gloss — alongside a wider network of safe learning spaces. By April 2024, more than 870,000 children and caregivers had been reached across 750 such spaces since the war began. These are not, in the main, digital classrooms; they are rooms where displaced children are safe, fed, counselled and taught. At the Al Maimona gathering point in Hantob, Gezira state, a UNICEF story from late 2023 follows a thirteen-year-old named Mehad, displaced from Khartoum, through the anatomy of a day: morning assembly, the national anthem sung as it was at her old school, an Arabic session led by a teacher the story names as Amani, then mathematics, then the cultural hour of songs and plays that Mehad says helps the children forget their pain. More than 600 displaced and host-community children were registered at that one point.

But inside many of these spaces sits something directly recognisable from Aroma and Tamantai: solar-powered tablets, game-based and self-paced content, a facilitating adult. The digital layer of the wartime response runs on the Learning Passport, a UNICEF platform carrying Sudan's national curriculum, which the country's mobile operators have zero-rated — no data charges — and which by April 2024 was supporting more than 35,000 learners in the war's hotspot areas. The same platform crossed the border with the displaced: in Egypt, as of the same vintage, it was supporting 35,000 learners and 6,700 teachers with Sudanese curricular content that would otherwise be inaccessible, an out-of-country continuation this publication covered from another angle when Sudan flew its national examinations to its diaspora. None of this is Can't Wait to Learn by name or by served record — War Child's 2025 report lists Sudan only for a new, separate mental-health response. It is something in one sense larger: the design pattern Sudan proved a decade ago — the classroom that ships to the child — running at national scale, under other names, in the emergency it was invented ahead of.

## The forward build

The next move is on the record and dated. On 4 May 2026, UNICEF and Save the Children, funded by the Global Partnership for Education, launched BRIDGES — Building Resilience to Improve Development and Growth in Education in Sudan — a 2026–2028 programme to return more than 328,000 conflict-affected children to learning. Every figure in it is a plan, not yet a reach, and should be carried as such: approximately 850 schools expected to reopen across seven conflict-affected states; more than 9,000 teachers supported to deliver remedial education against the accumulated learning loss; nearly 1,800 safe learning spaces for children who still cannot reach a formal school; and, less visibly, the rebuilding of the state's own machinery — education data systems and a national workforce strategy for recruiting, deploying and keeping teachers. The consortium structure is itself a register: UNICEF and Save the Children as Grant Agents, with Plan International and the Norwegian Refugee Council, coordinated with GPE, UNESCO and Sudan's Local Education Group. "In the face of an extraordinary crisis, Sudan's educators and communities are keeping hope alive through learning," said Belay Addise, GPE's regional manager for Eastern and Southern Africa, at the launch — and the programme's two-track shape, schools reopened where possible and alternative spaces where not, reads like the lesson of the last decade formalised.

There is a version of this story that would end on the export statistic — the Sudanese invention serving Ukraine's children, handed to Ukraine's government, while Sudan burns. It is true, and it is not the ending. The ending is that the sequence Sudan ran in 2014 — build the alternative before the emergency, measure it against the state's own baseline, publish the measurement whole — left the country with something most systems facing catastrophe do not have: a proven domestic pattern for teaching children the school cannot reach, operators like Tahir Onoor Ali and Fatima Abbas Tahir who have run it for years, and a population of children, like Mehad, who walk into a learning space and recognise it as school. A country that invented the classroom that needs no school is now the world's largest test of whether that idea can hold an education system open until the schools come back. The instrument was named Can't Wait to Learn. The children it was built for, twelve years later, still can't.
