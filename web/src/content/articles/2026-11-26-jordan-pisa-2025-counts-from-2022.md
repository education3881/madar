PISA 2025 was launched globally on Tuesday 8 September 2026. On the same day, Jordan's results were
announced at a press conference of their own, and the notice published by Jordan's National Center for Human
Resources Development records who was there: **"the Minister of Education and Human Development, the
president of the National Center for Human Resources Development, the head of the Higher Council for
Curricula, and the Director of Education and Military Culture."** The notice does not give a venue.

The centre reported three averages rising. Every one of them is measured against 2022.

## The figures, as Jordan's own register gives them

The national PISA coordinator, Dr Imad Ababneh, gave the numbers. Jordan's science average went **from 375
to 407 points**, mathematics **from 361 to 388**, and reading **from 342 to 363**. The centre states that
the improvement **"is statistically significant in the three fields, and indicates a clear recovery after
the decline seen in the 2022 round's results."**

It gives a second set for government schools alone. Across them, science rose **from 365 to
400** points, mathematics **from 353 to 382**, and reading **from 332 to 358**. The centre's president, Dr
Abdullah Ababneh, said that the improvement government schools recorded — **"including the schools of the
Ministry of Education and Human Resources Development and the military culture schools"** — was **"the
highest across the educational authorities in Jordan, compared with UNRWA schools and the private education
sector."**

The sample is stated. **"8,290 students from 276 schools belonging to the Ministry of Education, private
education, UNRWA and military culture took part in the 2025 round, which covered science, mathematics and
reading."** Ninety-one countries and economies took part in PISA 2025 in total, which is the figure the OECD
also prints.

The centre makes two comparative claims of its own, and both are its president's. Dr Abdullah Ababneh said
the 2025 round made Jordan **"among the few participating countries that recorded improvement in the three
fields,"** and that the size of the improvement in Jordanian students' performance was **"the largest among
the participating Arab countries that have data available for comparison."** Neither claim is accompanied by
a count, a list or a named comparison set, and neither is addressed in any of the three OECD registers read
for this piece. They are carried here as what the centre's president said, which is all they are so far.

Two gaps are reported as having narrowed. **"The gap between the performance of boys' schools and girls'
schools, and likewise between rural schools and badia schools, narrowed clearly in the 2025 round."** And
one school category is singled out: the performance of students at the King Abdullah II Schools for
Excellence was, in the centre's words, **"the highest among the educational categories in Jordan, exceeding
the international average and the average of the OECD countries."**

## What the OECD's release says about Jordan

One thing. Jordan appears once in the three registers the OECD published on 8 September — the press release,
the *PISA 2025 Results (Volume I)* full-report page and the Secretary-General's launch speech — and it
appears in a single sentence on the Volume I page: **"Within the OECD, science scores rose in Costa Rica,
the Slovak Republic, Türkiye and the United Kingdom. Among non-OECD members, scores also went up in
Cambodia, El Salvador, Georgia, Jordan, Mongolia, Montenegro, the Philippines, Saudi Arabia, Thailand, the
United Arab Emirates, Uruguay and Uzbekistan."**

That sentence carries no period. The launch speech supplies it: **"Since 2022, science scores went up in
Costa Rica, Slovakia, Türkiye and the United Kingdom – and in 12 other countries and economies."** So the
list is a three-year list, and it is about science.

Jordan is not on the OECD's other two improver lists, both of which run over ten years: the press release's
**"Between 2015 and 2025, some education systems improved performance in contrast to broader trends"**
list, and the list of systems that **"increased their share of top performers while reducing their share of
low performers in science."** Madār set those three lists side by side when this edition opened, because
each runs on its own clock and a claim read off the wrong one credits the wrong years.

At the country scale, the same care does more work. The OECD names Jordan on its three-year science list.
Jordan's own centre reports three-year rises in science, mathematics and reading. The two registers agree
about the interval and about science. On mathematics and reading the OECD's three lists say nothing at all,
because all three are lists about science — and silence about a subject is not a contradiction of it.

## The year both registers count from

The interval is the thing to hold on to, because both sides of this comparison chose it and both say why.

Dr Abdullah Ababneh described the results as **"an important positive indicator"** of the recovery of
Jordanian students' performance compared with the 2022 round, which he said **"came amid the repercussions
of the coronavirus pandemic."** The Minister of Education and Human Resources Development, Dr Azmi Mahafzah, put
the gain down to **"accumulated efforts by the ministry's staff and teachers in the field, particularly
since the period of the coronavirus pandemic, alongside work on developing curricula and implementing
remedial interventions aimed at improving students' level."** Both men are describing a climb out of 2022.

The OECD describes 2022 the same way from the other end. Its Volume I page opens on **"the unprecedented
drop in performance recorded in PISA 2022"**, and adds a warning that belongs beside any recovery reported
against that round: **"The weakening of performance predates the COVID-19 pandemic in many countries,
pointing to deeper and more persistent challenges within education systems."**

So 2022 is a low baseline by both registers' own account. That does not make Jordan's rise smaller than
its own centre reports it: the figures are the centre's, and it calls all three gains statistically
significant. It means the sentence has to carry its years, which is exactly what the centre's own wording
does — *compared with the 2022 round.* A reader who drops those four words is reading a different claim.

## What neither register here can be asked

Three questions came up in reading these four documents, and not one of them can be answered from the
documents themselves.

**Where Jordan stood before 2022.** No register read here prints a Jordanian figure for 2018, or 2015, or
any earlier round. Jordan's absence from the OECD's 2015-to-2025 improver list is a fact about that list and
about science. It is not a statement that Jordan is below its 2015 level, and nothing here supports
saying so in either direction. The OECD's country-level tables and country notes are where that lives, and they
were not read for this piece.

**How Jordan's averages sit against anyone else's.** The Volume I page prints OECD averages for 2025 and
Jordan's centre prints Jordan's. Putting the two in one sentence would produce a gap figure neither register
states, across samples and participation sets whose comparability is not established in either document.
The one comparison in Jordan's notice is the centre's own, about one school category, and it is carried here
as the centre's statement rather than as a measurement of Jordan.

**Which way the two gaps narrowed.** A gap between two groups can narrow because the lower group rose or
because the higher one fell. Jordan's centre reports the boys'/girls' and rural/badia school gaps
narrowing and does not say which. The OECD, reporting its own narrowed gap across OECD countries, does say:
it narrowed **"largely because advantaged students performed less well rather than improvements among
disadvantaged students."** These are two different gaps in two different populations, and the OECD's answer
is not transferable to Jordan's. The question is simply open.

One smaller thing, recorded because it would otherwise be smoothed away: the centre calls PISA 2025
**"the seventh round of the study, in which 91 countries took part."** It does not say seventh of what —
PISA's own rounds, or Jordan's participations — and no register read here settles it, so this piece counts
no rounds.

## Reading is the sentence both men ended on

Reading went from 342 to 363, and it is the subject both named officials put at the top of the next stage
rather than into the good news. PISA reports its three domains on three separate scales, so this piece does
not rank Jordan's three averages against one another. It does not need to. The two men say which domain they
are worried about.

Dr Abdullah Ababneh **"stressed the importance of placing reading at the focus of attention, as the key to
learning."** Dr Azmi Mahafzah said that **"the biggest challenge in the coming stage lies in the field of
reading,"** and that the ministry **"is working on a more comprehensive strategy to advance this aspect and
improve students' skills in it."** The strategy is not described further in the notice, and no document for
it is cited there, so there is nothing yet to read.

That is a ministry naming its own priority on the day its results improved, which is a more useful thing to
put in a brief than a rank. The centre prints no rank at all: Dr Imad Ababneh said the 2025 results
**"showed Jordan's position advancing on the ladder of participating countries, numbering 91"** — an
advance, with no position printed on either side of it.

Dr Mahafzah added one more caution, and it is the one a two-round gain most needs beside it:
**"developing the educational process needs a long time for its effects to appear in improving students'
results."** He said the factors behind the improvement were **"the school environment, teacher training,
and curriculum development."**

## What would settle the open questions

Two documents, and both are promised rather than published. The OECD said on 8 September that **"further
PISA analysis will follow in 2027"**, with the Foreign Language Assessment results in April and the full
Learning in the Digital World results and analysis in May. Jordan's own earlier results are published as
national reports by the same centre, which carries a *PISA* section and a 2022 results document. A reading
of Jordan across rounds belongs in that register, not in a press notice.

Until then, what can be said about Jordan and PISA 2025 is what its own centre said on the day: three
averages up against 2022, statistically significant, government schools rising most among Jordan's
educational authorities, two gaps narrower, and reading named as the work ahead.
