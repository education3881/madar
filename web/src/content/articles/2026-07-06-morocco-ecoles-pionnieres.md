In the first week of December 2025, Morocco's education ministry published a list. It carried 2,415 names — 2,214 primary schools and 201 lower-secondary collèges — each now entitled to the label "établissement pionnier," pioneer establishment, granted by a central commission at what the ministry calls the conformity level. What makes the list worth reading is what it leaves out. That same school year, 4,626 public primary schools were running the pioneer-schools model — the remediation-first overhaul at the operational core of Morocco's 2022–2026 education roadmap — teaching more than two million primary students, roughly 54% of the public primary network, alongside 786 collèges with some 678,000 more; the current-state count comes from the World Bank, which co-finances the programme through a $750 million operation. Two school years earlier, this had been a 626-school pilot. Running the model and holding the label are different counts, kept deliberately apart, and the gate between them — not the growth curve — is the mechanism that makes Morocco's scale-up worth studying.

The model itself is remediation with a timetable. A pioneer school opens the year with a dedicated catch-up block — six weeks in primary, four to eight in collège — built on Teaching at the Right Level, the approach the Moroccan press shorthands as TaRL: children are grouped by what they can actually read and compute rather than by the grade on their enrolment papers, and taught from there. The rest of the year runs on explicit, structured instruction; teachers train for and sit a certification in the approach; inspectors visit weekly. None of this is exotic, and that is the point. The 2023–24 pilot was less an experiment in pedagogy than an experiment in administration: 626 primary schools, 322,000 students, 10,700 teachers who volunteered into the model, 158 inspectors, spread across 82 provinces — a deliberate cross-section of the country rather than a favourable corner of it.

What Morocco added to a known method is the gate. A decree of 8 July 2024 — n°2.24.144 — created "pioneer establishment" as a certified status rather than a programme name. A school does not become pioneer by enrolling. It adopts the remediation block, the structured teaching and the assessment regime; its teachers pass their certification; and then the central commission audits the school against some twenty quality criteria and either grants the label or does not. The December list was the third labelling round, run in November 2025, and the ministry's own list titles state whom it covered: the schools of the 2024–25 intake — on the ministry's plan, 2,626 primary schools and 232 collèges, some 2,858 establishments. Of those, 2,415 passed. If the whole intake stood for audit, roughly one candidate school in six was refused. The money, meanwhile, is chained to the same gate: teachers holding the certification in labelled schools draw an annual bonus — 10,000 dirhams a year in Moroccan press accounts, a figure we could not yet verify against the decree's text in the official bulletin — so the incentive points at certified practice, not at membership of a scheme.

Whether the practice reaches children is — unusually for the region — a measured question, answered by two instruments that must not be blended. The Morocco Innovation and Evaluation Lab, J-PAL's arm at Mohammed VI Polytechnic University, evaluated the pilot's first year and found pioneer-school students outperforming 82% of their peers in comparable schools — an effect of roughly 0.9 of a standard deviation, which Moroccan coverage rendered as "a gain of one to two school years." Those are unusually large numbers, and they carry their own qualifiers: first year, pilot cohort, preliminary; a full research publication has yet to surface. The second instrument reads something else entirely. The CSEFRS — the constitutionally independent education council — published an external review of the pilot in June 2025 with an overall conformity index of 79 out of 100. That is a fidelity reading, not a learning reading: it says how faithfully schools assemble the model, not what the model does to children, and a system that lets the two numbers blur is fooling itself with its own paperwork. An impact lab on outcomes, an independent council on fidelity, a commission on criteria, school by school — the evaluation scaffold is crowded by design, and it is what separates deployment from announcement.

On the ground, the register is neither triumphal nor defensive. It is administrative — which may be the strongest signal in the story. In April 2025, the Moroccan outlet Médias24 reported from Wadi El Makhazine, a rural community school in Ouled Ziane, in the province of Berrechid, rehabilitated and folded into the programme. "We carry out regular evaluations to measure the impact of the strategies put in place," the provincial education director, Hassan El Bilali, told the paper. "The results show tangible progress in mathematics, in Arabic and in French." The school's director, Abdeslam Derrari, accounted for his part in the same key: "The school has been equipped with modern teaching materials, making teaching easier and lessons more attractive." Days earlier, in an agency dispatch carried by the Arabic press, the same provincial directorate had published its year in figures: 42 institutions in the programme across Berrechid, 25,514 students, 862 teachers trained in the remedial approach. A provincial director quoting his evaluation calendar; a head teacher quoting his equipment list; a directorate publishing its ledger. Deployment, at the point of delivery, looks like administration done in public.

The question a system operator would ask is whether any of this survives its own scale. The +0.9 standard deviations was earned in 626 schools staffed by 10,700 volunteers and watched by 158 inspectors — about one inspector to every four schools. The 2025–26 network runs 4,626 primary schools with 75,000 teachers and 960 inspectors — nearer one to five, before counting the collèges — and the announced trajectory is full primary coverage, 8,630 schools, by 2027–28, with the collège tier generalised the school year after. Somewhere along that curve, volunteers become the default workforce, and enthusiasm is the one input a decree cannot certify. The label faces the same arithmetic from the other side. A certification means something while it can be refused, and the November round shows it still can be; but a label designed for full coverage is a ratchet, not a rosette — the day 8,630 schools hold it, "pioneer" will describe the ordinary Moroccan primary school, which is precisely the plan. So the number to watch in each December list is not how many schools passed; it is whether the commission still fails anyone at volume. The refusal rate is the label's pulse. Two seams stay open on the public record: parents have worried that grouping children by level slows the strongest ones — drawing a ministry reassurance, not a design change — and the press has begun asking when the lycées join. What the record does not show, across a change of minister in October 2024 and three annual expansions — the collège tier even ran ahead of its plan, 786 against a programmed 730 — is any move to slow down. The risk story here is dilution, not reversal.

Morocco has, mid-deployment, published its numbers in an unusual order: the impact estimate first, the fidelity index second, the pass list third, and the growth plan in the open throughout — where most systems publish the growth plan and let the rest be inferred. That sequencing, more than the remediation month itself, is the exportable part. But the plainest thing in the record is also the smallest: a provincial director in Berrechid describing regular evaluation as ordinary work. Programmes announce; systems audit. Between 4,626 schools and 8,630, Morocco will find out whether the audit can stay ordinary — and the label refusable — at the speed the plan requires.
