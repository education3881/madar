In a low-income country's education budget, the moment of truth is rarely a classroom. It is a disbursement. In early 2026, Sierra Leone's largest education experiment reached that moment carrying three years of independently measured results — and the results were under target. What happened next is the story: the payments went out exactly as the measurement dictated, reduced where the gains fell short. The evaluator's full report went on the public record. And one of the five organisations being paid published its own shortfall, in its own words, with its own analysis code attached. *"Our results are not what we would have hoped them to be,"* wrote Fergal Turner of Save the Children — a sentence education systems almost never produce, from an instrument built so that someone would have to.

The instrument was the Sierra Leone Education Innovation Challenge, an $18m partnership between the Ministry of Basic and Senior Secondary Education (MBSSE), the Education Outcomes Fund and a syndicate of funders, running from 2022 to 2025 across 325 government primary schools. Five providers — EducAid, Street Child, Rising Academy Network, Save the Children and NYAF — each took a geographic lot, and each was measured against its own randomised control group of schools it never touched. The design register targeted more than 134,000 pupils; the funder register counts more than 130,000 reached; the completion register says more than 100,000. That downward drift between registers is not sloppiness — it is the rare direction. An outcomes contract is one of the few places in education where a reach number gets audited *down*, because someone is paid on it.

The payment mechanics are the architecture. After a first year paid on activities, Years 2 and 3 were paid on outcomes: 95% of performance payments rode on measured learning gains, with the remainder on pupils crossing a minimum-competency bar that MBSSE itself defined. The measurement was a randomised controlled trial run by Oxford Policy Management, whose Year 3 report states plainly that its estimates "form the basis for the outcome-based payments to providers." The ruler was not advisory. It carried money.

## What the ruler found

The pooled Year-3 result: a statistically significant gain of 0.278 standard deviations in maths, and a smaller but significant 0.102 in English, across all lots combined. Maths gains were positive in every lot and for both genders — with one exception, boys in NYAF's Lot 5 — ranging from 0.130 to 0.384 standard deviations. On the second payment metric, the share of pupils meeting the ministry's minimum competency in maths rose in every lot, from 6.2 percentage points in EducAid's Lot 1 to 24.4 in Rising Academy's Lot 3.

Those gains landed against a tide going out. National learning levels were falling over the same period: maths scores declined between Years 2 and 3 in treatment and control schools alike, with treatment schools simply falling less and finishing higher; English scores declined in both groups from baseline onward. The evaluator's careful phrasing is that SLEIC's effect on English was "primarily in mitigating the extent of this decline." Minimum competency in English fell in most lots. The programme register — the Education Outcomes Fund's own six takeaways — carries both halves: gains that outperform roughly 70% of comparable programmes globally, and the caution, in the fund's own words, that the programme "does not definitely prove" outcomes-based financing is a better way to fund education. A funder publishing that second sentence about its own flagship is part of the same architecture as the reduced payments.

## The ruler that shrank its own number

Here is the detail this publication has been enforcing on sources for six editions, practised — for once — by the party being measured. Save the Children's schools gained .359 standard deviations per grade in maths, a result the standard conversion used across low- and middle-income countries would translate as 1.1 to 1.6 additional years of learning. The provider computed it both ways, and published the smaller one: two-thirds of a year, by a ruler derived from its own baseline data. Its stated reasons: the local ruler is numeracy-specific, and specific to the schools it actually worked in. A scoped instrument, scoped by its own user, against its own interest — with the consequence stated in the same post: behind target for girls and boys in Year 3, and therefore "only a portion" of the final payment. The English result is carried in the register it deserves: gains that did not reach statistical significance are no demonstrated gain, not a small one. Three of five providers did better, the post notes, and the endline analysis sits in a public repository under the heading "check our homework."

The provider the results favour is Lot 3, Rising Academy Network. The evaluator's year-on-year figure shows programme impact peaking in Year 2 almost everywhere — except maths in Lots 2 and 3, which rose again in Year 3, and Rising's 0.384 standard deviations is the largest effect in the table. Lot 3 is also the only lot where English gains are significant for both girls and boys. Whatever Rising did — and the evaluator is careful to say the drivers need qualitative investigation, pointing to structured pedagogy and frequent coaching as the practices teachers themselves credit — it is the closest thing the experiment produced to an answer worth copying, and it was found by a ruler that was simultaneously docking a peer's payment.

Two percentile claims from this programme now circulate, and they measure different things. "Outperforms roughly 70% of comparable programmes globally" is the programme register: all five lots, both subjects, pooled. "Somewhere between the 80th and 90th percentile" is Save the Children's own maths-only result, placed on the distribution of effect sizes catalogued by Evans and Yuan. Both are true. Neither substitutes for the other, and a reader will meet them blended.

The gender texture runs both directions, so both get named. Girls led maths in most lots (0.300 against boys' 0.260 pooled, a difference without statistical significance), and the Year-2 gap in Save the Children's schools narrowed by Year 3 partly because boys improved — their Lot 4 maths effect went from a non-significant 0.056 to significant — and partly because girls' gains fell. In English, the pattern inverts twice: girls gained more, and unlike maths, the gains concentrated among the strongest pupils rather than the weakest. In maths it was the bottom quartile that moved most.

## The ministry that let itself be measured

On 15 January 2026, the programme closed where its funding conversations had started: the British High Commissioner's residence in Freetown, with Josephine Gauld putting the fiscal question directly — a celebration, but also a moment to ask where the money comes from and how every pound works for children. The keynote belonged to the education minister, Conrad Sackey, and it is worth noticing what he chose to say. Not the 70th-percentile line. He told the room that expanding access is necessary but no longer sufficient, that the next phase of reform must be decisively focused on learning outcomes — and then he volunteered the finding that made him pause: English literacy did not move at the pace of mathematics. A minister electing to lead with his programme's weakest subject is the honesty architecture reaching the political register, the place it usually dies. *"SLEIC shifted us from inputs to outcomes, from assumptions to evidence,"* he said. His own chief education officer, Edward Kpakra, added the kind of operational fact ministries rarely put on the record: between breaks and extracurricular time, most subjects in a Sierra Leonean school day get less than one hour of actual teaching.

Sierra Leone has been here before, in a different key. The same ministry ran the teacher-deployment matching reform this publication covered in Edition 1 — that story was about who stands in front of the classroom; this one is about what the money buys once they are there. The through-line is a government willing to let an instrument tell it something it did not commission it to say.

What the instrument could not do is its own finding. It could not make English move the way maths moved — a gap the evaluator attributes, without certainty, to some mix of intervention design, language environment and the national decline. It could not immunise Year-3 results against the fade that followed the Year-2 peak in most lots. And it could not answer its own hardest question: whether paying for outcomes *caused* the outcomes, or disciplined everyone into measuring them properly. The programme's designers say only the latter is proven. The same partnership has since taken the architecture earlier in the system, to an outcomes fund for early childhood education that its own programme register now lists as running — a separate programme, inheriting the machinery rather than the results.

The accomplishment, then, is not a miracle number. Sierra Leone finished with real, verified maths gains in 325 schools while the national tide went out — and with something scarcer: a paper trail in which the funder published its caveats, the evaluator published the misses, the provider published its own shortfall and the smaller of its two possible numbers, and the state paid precisely what the evidence said it owed. Education systems produce results every year. Very few produce results anyone has a reason to believe. For three years, one of the poorest countries in the world ran the instrument that makes the difference — and when it reported less than it hoped, that was the proof it worked.
