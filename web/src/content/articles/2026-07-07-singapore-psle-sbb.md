In July 2016, Singapore's Ministry of Education did something ministries of education almost never do: it announced that its most consequential examination would become less precise, on purpose. The Primary School Leaving Examination — the PSLE, the national exam that sorts Singapore's twelve-year-olds into secondary schools — would drop the T-score, an aggregate fine enough to distinguish more than 200 ranks across a cohort, for a scale that allows 29 possible scores. The ministry did not bury the intent; it numbered it. The first stated aim of the change was to "reduce fine differentiation of students," and the press release carried the arithmetic plainly: "students will no longer be as finely differentiated, since there will only be 29 possible PSLE Scores, compared to more than 200 T-score Aggregates." Systems usually announce sharper instruments — more data, finer bands, earlier detection. Here is the rarer document: a state filing, in writing, its intention to blur its best-known ruler. A decade on, its own registers — results releases, parliamentary replies, a posting FAQ — let anyone audit what happened next. The short answer is that the measuring did not stop. It moved.

The instrument, since 2021, works like this. Each of four subjects is graded into eight Achievement Levels, AL1 the best, against fixed standards rather than against the rest of the cohort; the PSLE Score is the plain sum of the four, running from 4 to 32, lower better. The exam is conducted by the Singapore Examinations and Assessment Board; scoring and posting policy is set by the ministry. Where the T-score said where a child stood among all other children, the new score says only how far each subject was mastered — and refuses to say anything finer than twenty-nine steps can carry. The scale of the annual exercise makes the design concrete: in 2025, 37,926 Primary 6 students sat the PSLE, and 98.5% were assessed suitable for secondary school; in 2024, a dragon-year bulge, 40,894 sat, with the same 98.5%. Set thirty-eight thousand children against twenty-nine possible totals and the point is unmissable: at nearly every score stands a crowd. That is not a flaw in the new ruler. It is the ruler's entire argument.

The blunting had a second act. From the 2024 Secondary 1 intake, the streams that organised Singaporean secondary schooling — Express, Normal (Academic), Normal (Technical) — were removed outright. Under Full Subject-Based Banding, a student now takes each subject at one of three levels, G1 to G3, and enters secondary school through one of three Posting Groups; the ministry's measured ceiling on upward movement is that about 65% of students eligible for Posting Groups 1 and 2 can take at least one subject at a more demanding level, a share that held for the 2024 and 2025 cohorts alike. Education Minister Chan Chun Sing, speaking at a ministry lecture in February 2025, described the arc with a candour worth preserving: "it was not really the end of streaming, but refinement of streaming by subject." The label is gone; the per-subject sorting remains. The first cohort never to carry a stream label is now in Secondary 3, and in 2027 it sits the examination redesigned to receive it — the new Singapore-Cambridge Secondary Education Certificate, whose results are to arrive in January 2028 in a single release, where N-Level and O-Level results used to arrive as separate events in December and January. Even the calendar of envelopes is being de-tiered.

At school level, the redesign arrived not as philosophy but as scheduling. Pasir Ris Secondary School joined Full Subject-Based Banding with the final batch of 24 schools in 2024, and its principal, Mrs Grace Chua, described the operation to The Straits Times in January 2025: "One of the main challenges changing from the previous system to the new one was the systematic and logistical change. The complexity of the different and varied schedules due to the different groups also meant the structure of the timetable needs to be altered." The same report records the school rebuilding its timetable on 20-minute blocks and resizing classrooms to fit the new groupings. Her year head for upper secondary, Mr Mohamed Ashiq, stated the intended dividend in one sentence: "The new system also allows students to mix more freely, leading to a better understanding of different backgrounds and strengths." That is the operator's-eye view of de-streaming: before it is a theory of childhood, it is a timetable that must physically work, twenty minutes at a time.

Then there is the part the redesign could not remove: the same number of children still had to be sorted into the same number of secondary school places. Posting still runs on the score, and with twenty-nine possible scores, ties are no longer the exception; they are the condition the system is built around. When candidates tie at a school's last place, the tie is broken in a fixed order: citizenship first, then the order in which each family listed the school among its six choices, then a computerised ballot. The measured outcomes are good, and carefully incomplete. More than 90% of the 2024 Secondary 1 cohort were posted to a school among their six choices — an outcome the ministry describes as "consistent across all posting groups," while declining, in the same parliamentary reply, to publish the per-group figures. The balloting number is older and needs its base stated: in the 2022 exercise, more than 80% of students were posted within their first three choices, and of those at tie-broken margins, around 1 in 10 went to the computerised ballot — a 2022 figure, counted among tie-broken students rather than the cohort; nothing newer has been published.

And the cut-off points — the folk unit of Singaporean school admission — survived the coarsening intact. A school's COP is nothing more than the score of the last student admitted the year before; the ministry notes that COPs "can vary up to a few points from year to year," and its official advice to families now reads like an operating manual for the new margins: include "at least 2 to 3 schools whose previous year's COPs are less stringent" among the six choices. The sharpest measure of where the old precision went came in a parliamentary reply of February 2025. Asked for the data, the ministry reported that the gap between a school's cut-off for applicants from its affiliated primary schools and for open applicants averages about 7 PSLE points in Posting Group 3, about 2 in Posting Group 2 and about 1 in Posting Group 1 — seven points, on a ruler whose whole length runs from 4 to 32. The same reply records that since 2021, about half of affiliated schools' Secondary 1 intake has come from non-affiliated primaries. Both facts are the ministry's own, published side by side: fine differentiation left the score sheet, and reassembled at the school door as affiliation, choice order and ballot.

Whether any of this made twelve-year-olds' lives lighter is a claim the ministry itself declines to make. Chan again, in the same lecture: "If we do not broaden our definitions of success beyond internal relativities, no extent of changes in the PSLE scoring system will ever reduce stress." That sentence is the reform's honest boundary — a coarser ruler changes what the state prints, not, by itself, what families chase. The wager filed in 2016 settles slowly, in measured behaviour: in whether the cut-off gaps narrow, in whether the ballot stays a third-order rarity, in what the 2027 certificate's first cohort does to the queues. What Singapore has already delivered is rarer than the wager: a state that blunted its most famous measure and then kept publishing the numbers — the seven points, the half-intake, the one in ten — by which the blunting can be audited. A system that stops ranking its children has not stopped being asked to rank them. The registers now show, one margin at a time, where the asking goes.

— *The Editors*
