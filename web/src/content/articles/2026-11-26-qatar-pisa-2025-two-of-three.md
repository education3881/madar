Edition 06 opened with a count. *Three lists of risers*, published on 26 November, found that the OECD's
PISA 2025 release names improving systems three times, in three lists on three clocks, and that Qatar is on
two of them. It is on the ten-year science list: **"Between 2015 and
2025, some education systems improved performance in contrast to broader trends. Science performance rose
in Lithuania, the Slovak Republic, Türkiye, the Dominican Republic, Georgia, Macao (China), Montenegro,
Qatar, Thailand, the United Arab Emirates, and Uruguay."** It is on the list that asks whether a system
cut its bottom and grew its top at the same time: **"Türkiye, the Slovak Republic, Montenegro, Qatar and
the United Arab Emirates increased their share of top performers while reducing their share of low
performers in science."** Both of those are ten-year
measurements, and the piece below is the reason that sentence has to be in this paragraph rather than four
sections down. Qatar is not on the third list, the systems whose science average rose between 2022 and 2025.

*Three lists of risers* said, in as many words, that an absence from a list is a question and not an answer,
and that nothing could be said about Qatar's last three years until a register printed them. The register has now
been read. It was one link inside a page this edition had been citing since it opened: Chapter 2 of
*PISA 2025 Results (Volume I)*, the report whose front page carries List A.

## Three years, in the OECD's own table

Chapter 2 prints **Table I.2.6, "Change between 2022 and 2025 in mean performance in science, reading and
mathematics"**, for every system that can be compared across those three years in all three subjects. Its
Qatar row reads **2 in science, −1 in reading, 2 in mathematics**.

The same table's **OECD average-35** row reads **−3 in science, −14 in reading, −9 in mathematics**. *(The
table is printed as three panels side by side and the served text runs across them, so the distance between
two rows is a fact about the page's layout rather than about the table; this piece gives no row distances.)*

So the two statements are consistent, and the table is what reconciles them. Qatar is not among the
systems the OECD counts as having raised their science average since 2022, and the change it printed for
Qatar is two score points. Three years in which Qatar's three averages moved by two points, minus one and
two, against an average that fell by three, fourteen and nine.

**What this piece will not do is call that flat a success or a stall.** Table I.2.6 marks which changes
are statistically significant by the colour of the cell, and a colour does not survive into served text.
What can be said is what the registers say: the OECD's own improver list for these three years does not
name Qatar, and the OECD's own table gives Qatar's change in each subject as a single-digit number.

Table I.2.8 covers the same three years and counts students rather than score points. Its two columns are
*"Change in percentage of low performers"* and *"Change in percentage of top performers"*, and Qatar's row
reads **−0.8** and **−0.1** — both in percentage points, both over 2022 to 2025. They are not comparable with
the score-point changes above, and this piece does not rank the two kinds of figure against each other.

Those two numbers are worth reading beside Qatar's membership of the both-ends group, because they are the
same two quantities on a different clock. The decade claim is that Qatar reduced its share of low performers
**and** increased its share of top performers. Over these three years the first still holds, by 0.8
percentage points, and the second does not: the share of top performers in science is down by 0.1 percentage
points. Neither register contradicts the other. One is fitted across a decade and the other is the difference
between two rounds, and the OECD prints both.

## The decade, which is a different measurement

The chapter's Table I.2.9, **"Trends in mean performance in science, reading and mathematics since 2015"**,
is explicitly labelled *"Based on average decennial trend"* — a trend fitted across the cycles, not the
arithmetic difference between two of them. On that measure Qatar reads **19 in science, 18 in reading and
12 in mathematics**, all three positive, which is why Qatar is on the release's ten-year list and why
*Three lists of risers* found it there. The OECD average-35 row of the same table, on the same fitted
decennial measure, reads **−7, −27 and −24**. The front page of the report puts the science half of that in
score points: *"OECD average science scores fell from 489 to 482 points between 2015 and 2025"* — the register's sentence continues past that comma, and the rest of it is about non-OECD participants.

The decade's other Qatari fact is a membership of five. The chapter names them — **"Only five
countries/economies, Montenegro, Qatar, the Slovak Republic, Türkiye and the United Arab Emirates"** —
and says what they did: they **"were able to simultaneously reduce their shares of low-performing
students and increase their shares of top-performing students over the past decade."** *(The register
prints a doubled comma between the two halves of that sentence; the quotation is split here rather than
reproducing a typographical artefact as if it were ours.)* The press release's version of that list carries no period at all, and the clock on it comes from one
register and one speaker: **Mathias Cormann**, the OECD's Secretary-General, in remarks delivered under the
register's own title, "International launch of PISA 2025", on 8 September 2026. **"Over the past decade, Türkiye, the United Arab Emirates, Montenegro, Qatar and
Slovakia all cut their share of low performers and grew their share of top performers at the same time."**
The page carries *"Check against delivery"*, so that is the text as prepared rather than a transcript, and it
is cited as that. Without it the both-ends list in the press release is a group of five systems with no span
attached, which is the shape this edition's first piece was written about.

Set the two measurements side by side and the shape of Qatar's PISA 2025 is a decade of movement and a
round without much of it. Neither cancels the other. A ten-year trend is not a three-year difference, and
a system can be on a decade list and absent from a three-year one without either register being wrong —
which is the finding Edition 06 opened with, now carrying a number.

## The same table, the same clock, four systems

Table I.2.6 is one list answering one question, which makes the systems inside it comparable with each
other in a way that systems taken from two different lists are not. Four of the participants this
publication covers are in it, and their rows run in this order:

| System | Science | Reading | Mathematics |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jordan | +32 | +20 | +26 |
| United Arab Emirates | +26 | +15 | +21 |
| Saudi Arabia | +23 | +5 | +1 |
| **Qatar** | **+2** | **−1** | **+2** |

Those are four Gulf and Levant systems measured by one instrument across one three-year span, and the
spread between the top row and the bottom is thirty score points in science. Nothing in the register
explains the spread, and this piece does not offer one.

## Where Qatar stands, not how far it has come

Table I.2.1 gives Qatar's science mean for 2025 as **434**. It does not print a number for the OECD
average; it sorts systems into three bands around it. The number is in the chapter's Table I.2.3, whose first
column is the same overall science mean — **Qatar 434, OECD average 482** — and in the report's own front-page
note, quoted above.

Table I.2.1's other column is the company the measurement puts each system in. For Qatar it lists seven
systems whose mean is not statistically different: **Mauritius, Uzbekistan, Montenegro, Albania\*, Greece,
Thailand and Serbia**. The asterisk on Albania is the register's own marker, and the chapter does not define
it — it points to an annex this piece did not read, so the mark is reproduced and left uninterpreted. That
list is a statement about one subject in one year, and about where Qatar sits rather than about where it has
been going.

## The context the chapter insists on

Chapter 2 does not let a reader take Qatar's figures bare. It reports that in **Luxembourg, Macao (China),
Qatar and the United Arab Emirates, more than half of students have an immigrant background** — the only
four participating systems where that is true — and it says of those four that **"Each should therefore be
examined in its own context"**, naming the social and demographic profile of the immigrant population,
immigration policies, and educational policies towards immigrant students.

That instruction is the OECD's, it is printed beside the figures, and this piece carries it for the same
reason it carries the hedges: a register's qualifier travels with the register's number.

## What is still owed

No Qatari first-party register on PISA 2025 has been read for this piece. `edu.gov.qa` does not answer
this runner at all — not a refusal, no status code — and it was re-tested on 2026-11-27 before this
sentence was written. Everything above is the OECD's, read from the OECD's own report. When Qatar's own
authority publishes its reading of PISA 2025, it will be a different piece, and this one will say so.
