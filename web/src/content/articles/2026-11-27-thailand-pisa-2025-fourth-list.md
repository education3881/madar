PISA 2025 was launched globally on Tuesday 8 September 2026. The same afternoon, in a conference room at a
Bangkok hotel, Thailand's results were announced by the Institute for the Promotion of Teaching Science and
Technology — IPST, the body that runs PISA in Thailand as the national centre. The notice IPST published
carries a sentence that none of the three OECD registers this edition opened with carries.

In the institute's own words, translated: **"there are 8 countries and economies whose average scores in all
three domains rose from PISA 2022 with statistical significance, namely: Georgia, Cambodia, Jordan,
Montenegro, the United Arab Emirates, Thailand, Türkiye and the Philippines."**

## The OECD counts the same eight and does not name them

That number is not the institute's own finding, and this piece said so before it went looking: the OECD
reaches eight as well. It does it in Chapter 2 of *PISA 2025 Results (Volume I)*, a chapter page of the
full report rather than anything in the launch — **"as shown in Table I.2.6, it is equally relevant to note
that eight countries and economies improved their performance in all three subjects."** The chapter does
not list them there. What it prints is the table: every system that can be compared between PISA 2022 and
PISA 2025 in all three subjects, with its three changes, ranked by the change in science, and with the
significant rises, the significant falls and the non-significant moves distinguished by the colour of the
cell rather than by any word a reader can quote.

So the two registers divide the work. **The OECD counts the eight and colours them; the institute names
them.** Read the table against the institute's sentence and the eight are the same eight, in the same
order the table ranks them:

| | Science | Reading | Mathematics |
|---|---|---|---|
| Georgia | +38 | +10 | +26 |
| Cambodia | +35 | +18 | +29 |
| Jordan | +32 | +20 | +26 |
| Montenegro | +32 | +13 | +5 |
| United Arab Emirates | +26 | +15 | +21 |
| **Thailand** | **+23** | **+13** | **+13** |
| Türkiye | +18 | +16 | +8 |
| The Philippines | +17 | +20 | +16 |

The row immediately above Thailand's in the OECD's own ranking is **Saudi Arabia, at +23, +5 and +1** — the
same rise in science as Thailand, and not one of the eight. That is the whole of what the eight-name list
tests, visible in one row: all three subjects, and each rise large enough to be called a rise.

Thailand's three figures in that table are the three the institute states in its own prose. **Two registers,
one in Bangkok and one in Paris, print the same three numbers for Thailand**, which is the strongest form
a figure in this publication can take and is rarer than it should be.

## A fourth list, and where it sits

This edition's first piece set out the three lists of improving systems in the OECD's PISA 2025 release and
showed that each runs on its own clock. All three are about **science alone**: whose science average rose
since 2022, whose rose since 2015, and who over ten years cut the bottom and grew the top at once. The
institute's sentence is a fourth list, on the first list's three-year clock, and across all three subjects
rather than one.

It is not a rival to the other three. It sits **inside** one of them. The OECD's since-2022 list, in the
Volume I full-report page, names sixteen systems: **"Within the OECD, science scores rose in Costa Rica, the
Slovak Republic, Türkiye and the United Kingdom. Among non-OECD members, scores also went up in Cambodia, El
Salvador, Georgia, Jordan, Mongolia, Montenegro, the Philippines, Saudi Arabia, Thailand, the United Arab
Emirates, Uruguay and Uzbekistan."** Four OECD members and twelve others.

That sentence carries no period of its own. Its clock comes from somewhere else in the launch: the speech of
the OECD's Secretary-General, Mathias Cormann, which the page marks **"Check against delivery"** and which
counts the same list. **"Since 2022, science scores went up in Costa Rica, Slovakia, Türkiye and the United
Kingdom – and in 12 other countries and economies."** Four and twelve, over three years. Without that
sentence the sixteen have no span, and the comparison this piece makes would not be available at all.

Every one of the institute's eight names is on that list of sixteen. Set the two side by side and the
arithmetic is the finding: of the sixteen systems whose **science** average rose in three years, **eight**
rose in science, mathematics and reading together — exactly half. The other eight — Costa Rica, the Slovak
Republic, the United Kingdom, El Salvador, Mongolia, Saudi Arabia, Uruguay and Uzbekistan — are on the
science list and not on this one.

The OECD did not omit this list, and the record now shows exactly where it lives. The release, the Volume I
full-report page and the launch speech ask three questions about science and answer them; the count of
eight is one level in, in a chapter of the report, and the names are in neither. **A reader who wants the
all-three-subject reading of PISA 2025 can get the number from the OECD and the names from a national
centre**, and until today this edition had only the first three registers and therefore only three lists.
That is a fact about what we had read, which is why this piece names all five registers it stands on.

One of the eight is Jordan. On the same day, in Arabic, Jordan's National Center for Human Resources
Development reported its own three averages up against 2022 and called the improvement **"statistically
significant in the three fields"**. Two national centres, in two languages, neither citing the other, state
the same fact about Jordan. That is what corroboration between first-party registers looks like, and it is
rarer than it sounds.

## Thailand's own four numbers

IPST gives Thailand four averages for 2025. Science **432**, mathematics **407**, reading **392**, and
computational problem solving **476**. All four are below the OECD averages the institute reports for the
same round — **482**, **463**, **461** and **500**. Girls scored higher than boys in science, mathematics and
reading; boys scored higher in computational problem solving. That is the only domain in which they did.

Measured against PISA 2022, all three main averages rose. Science by **23** points, mathematics by **13**,
reading by **13**. The institute's three-subject list is the test those rises passed: statistical
significance in all three at once.

## Nine rounds, counted

Thailand has sat PISA nine times since 2000, and the institute publishes the whole series itself, as a
spreadsheet beside the notice. It is the only register read here that holds Thailand's earlier rounds, and
it is worth reading before the word "recovery" is used.

| Round | Reading | Mathematics | Science |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2000 | 431 | 432 | 436 |
| 2003 | 420 | 417 | 429 |
| 2006 | 417 | 417 | 421 |
| 2009 | 421 | 419 | 425 |
| 2012 | 441 | 427 | 444 |
| 2015 | 409 | 415 | 421 |
| 2018 | 393 | 419 | 426 |
| 2022 | 379 | 394 | 409 |
| 2025 | 392 | 407 | 432 |

Counted against those nine rows, Thailand's 2025 science average of 432 is its **third-highest**, behind
2012's 444 and 2000's 436. Its mathematics average of 407 and its reading average of 392 are each its
**second-lowest**, above only 2022. One round, three rises, and two of them measured from the lowest floor
the country has recorded.

The institute says as much in its own summary of the trend: across PISA 2000 to PISA 2025, mathematics and
reading **trend downward**, while science is **statistically unchanged**. A rise of 23 points inside a series
that has not moved is the shape of this result, and the register prints both halves of it.

## How many students clear the bar

PISA also reports proficiency levels, six of them, and for science, mathematics and reading **level 2** is
the one the institute
describes as the baseline level, where a student can use the skill and the knowledge in real life. **59%** of Thai students reached level 2
or above in science, against **74%** across OECD countries. In mathematics and in reading the Thai share is
**41%** in each, against **65%** and **69%**. In the highest-scoring systems — the institute names the four
Chinese provinces, Singapore, Estonia, Japan, Chinese Taipei and Korea — more than **85%** clear it in
science.

Those shares rose. Against PISA 2022, the proportion of Thai students at level 2 or above went up by **12**
in science, **9** in mathematics and **6** in reading. The register writes these three with a percent sign;
they are changes in a share of students, not in a score.

In computational problem solving the reference level is **3**, which the institute describes as the point
where a student has enough to build on quickly. **55%** of Thai students reached it, against **64%** across
the OECD, and **93%** in Macao.

## Two sentences about one gap, on two clocks

This edition's first piece was about clocks, and the clearest instance in the two registers is a sentence
each about the same gap.

The OECD's press release: **"Across OECD countries, advantaged students scored 85 points higher in science
than disadvantaged students. The gap narrowed between 2022 and 2025, but largely because advantaged students
performed less well rather than improvements among disadvantaged students."**

The institute, writing about PISA 2015 to PISA 2025: Thailand's own gap between its top socio-economic
quarter and its bottom one did not change, while the OECD's **narrowed because the disadvantaged group's
average rose and the advantaged group held its level**.

Those two mechanisms are opposite, and the registers are not in conflict. One is measuring three years and
the other ten. A reader who takes either sentence as the general case gets the direction of the last decade
backwards, which is precisely the error this edition opened by naming.

Thailand's own figures in the same passage: the science gap between its advantaged and disadvantaged
students is **55** points, narrower than the OECD's **85**. **17%** of Thai students are academically
resilient in the institute's sense — in the bottom socio-economic group nationally and the top quarter of
national science scores — against **12%** across OECD countries. And the distance between Thailand's 90th
and 10th percentiles is about **200** points in each subject, unchanged since 2022.

## Which schools moved

The institute reports by school group, and the groups are administrative authorities rather than quality
tiers. Science-focused schools scored above the OECD average in every domain. University demonstration
schools were above it in science and mathematics and close to it in reading. Every other group remained
below the OECD average in all three.

Nearly all groups moved up against 2022, and the institute names which rises met the significance test:
the opportunity-expansion schools under the Office of the Basic Education Commission in all three subjects;
the Bangkok Metropolitan Administration's schools in science and mathematics; and both the commission's
secondary schools and the local-administration schools in science.

## What the register asks for, and what is still owed

IPST's notice ends with recommendations, not causes. It calls the overall picture a positive development in
the quality of Thai students' learning, names the gap between its high and low scorers as educational
inequality still to be addressed, and puts reading first among the things needing continued work —
specifically reading for meaning, analysing and evaluating information from varied sources, and more
practice at reading and critical thought — alongside teacher development, competency-based teaching, and
resourcing schools fairly across every authority.

The sample is stated. PISA 2025 was administered in Thailand in **August 2025** to **8,547** students in
**273** schools across every education authority, part of a round of more than **760,000** students
representing around **33 million** 15-year-olds in **91** countries and economies. The second half of the new
digital-learning assessment, on how students regulate their own learning, is promised for **2027**.
