On 6 March 2026, a few days before Chile's school year opened, the Agencia de Calidad de la Educación — the country's education quality agency — handed schools the results of the national tests their students had sat the previous November. Simce is a census, not a sample: when it runs, it reads every school in the measured grades. For the third year in a row, the reading arrived at the start of the school year it is meant to serve, early enough for a school to do something about it before the year hardens into its habits. And for the third year in a row, the folders held more than scores: alongside reading and mathematics came the Indicadores de Desarrollo Personal y Social — the census's wellbeing indicators — and the findings of the context questionnaires filled in around the tests. The instrument that came back from the pandemic is not quite the instrument that went in. It returns earlier in the year, and it returns carrying more.

The going-in is worth dating. Simce — the Sistema de Medición de la Calidad de la Educación, administered under the ministry's national and international evaluations plan for 2021–2026 — was suspended in 2020, when the pandemic closed the country's classrooms; the census application resumed in November 2022. Since the 2023 cycle, delivery has moved to the first days of March, and that scheduling decision is easy to underrate. A measurement that reaches schools in March describes students the school still has; the teachers reading it can regroup a class, re-teach a unit, flag a cohort, in the same year the data describes. The agency states the purpose in exactly those terms — results delivered at the start of the school year to support schools' pedagogical decisions — and the same delivery carries the wellbeing indicators and the questionnaires, so that what lands on a principal's desk is not a verdict on last year but a reading of the students walking in the door. Whether the instrument's fiercest critics accept that framing is a separate matter, and this piece will come to them; the cadence itself, though, is now a fact three years deep.

What did the returned census show? First a recovery, then the harder thing to narrate that follows one. In 2024, fourth grade — 4° básico — reached 278 in reading and 264 in mathematics, up six and five points, the highest marks of the whole series since 2002; the ministry's release called the advance historic, which is a government's word for its own good news and is attributed here as such. Sixth grade, measured for the first time since 2018, came back at 249 and 245 — one and six points below its own pre-pandemic self. And across the two post-return measurements, the lowest socioeconomic groups gained around fifteen points in both subjects — the recovery reached furthest where the fall had cut deepest. Then 2025: II medio — the tenth-grade cohort — reached 250 in reading and 263 in mathematics, back at pre-pandemic levels with its trajectory still rising. Fourth grade slipped two points in each subject, to 276 and 262, a dip the agency read as statistically insignificant and named with the drier word: stabilisation. Eighth grade, measured for the first time since the pandemic, landed at 238 in reading, three points under 2019, and 258 in mathematics, five under — neither fall statistically significant, by the agency's own reading; history and geography held at 250. A recovery, a plateau after a series high, and a first sounding of a grade nobody had measured in six years: the full vocabulary of a ruler in use.

Gino Cortez, the agency's secretario ejecutivo — the operator who signs the delivery, not the politician who inherits it — put the return in one sentence at the March handover. "For the third consecutive year, we deliver the Simce and IDPS results at the start of the school year, with the purpose of supporting pedagogical decision-making in schools," he said — the Spanish is his; the translation is ours. "The results of Simce 2025 show that the education system has managed to recover pre-pandemic learning levels, especially in II medio." He attached the caveat in the same breath: "Mathematics continues to present challenges at the national level across all grades, and requires education policies that foster the development of skills in this subject." The census gives his caveat its edge. In fourth-grade mathematics the gender gap now runs to fifteen points in favour of boys — 269 against 254 — and it is widening. A ruler that reads every school cannot help reading that too; what is done with the reading is the one thing no census can deliver.

The broadened cargo is the part of the return most easily missed. The wellbeing indicators put numbers to things score tables never held — how students experience their schools — and the context questionnaires reach further still. In this delivery, the agency reported that schools with strategies for managing academic anxiety associate with up to 23 additional points in fourth-grade mathematics: an association, not a proven cause, but a census-scale hint that the wellbeing strand and the score strand are not separate subjects. The instrument now even measures its own uptake — 83% of II medio teachers say they use Simce results for academic improvement. A census that asks classrooms how they feel, and asks teachers whether the census is useful, is doing something categorically different from producing a ranking: it is reading classrooms for more than answers, and delivering the reading while there is still time to act on it.

None of which settled how much of it the country should buy. In May 2026, the incoming administration's education ministry proposed shrinking the 2026 test plan to fourth grade and II medio, plus an exploratory reading test in second grade, citing fiscal restrictions. The Consejo Nacional de Educación — the independent council whose approval the plan requires — rejected the proposal, warning of a "critical gap" in the monitoring of learning trajectories and questioning the loss of sixth grade, and of eighth-grade English and natural sciences. The ministry returned with a revised plan and the council approved it, as La Tercera reported: sixth grade restored, reading and mathematics to run in 4° básico, 6° básico and II medio, the second-grade reading probe alongside. The episode reads small and is not. A census that a virus once suspended was nearly narrowed by a budget, and the counterweight the system reached for was an independent council with the standing to say: measure more. Chile's ruler has now been stress-tested twice in four years — and both times the state, in the end, chose to keep measuring.

The oldest dispute rides alongside, unsettled. Alto al SIMCE — the campaign whose name is its entire argument: stop the Simce — remains active; days before the March delivery, a column in El Mostrador argued that the country's debate over the instrument is still pending, and an academic at the Universidad de Santiago pressed the case that standardized testing is over-valued in how Chile reads its schools. The critique deserves carrying, not adjudicating: a census this consequential shapes what schools attend to, and a country is entitled to keep asking whether the instrument distorts more than it reveals. What the past three years add to that argument is not a verdict but a set of facts about the instrument itself: it comes back when interrupted; it arrives in time to be used; it reads wellbeing beside scores; and when a ministry proposed less of it, an independent council forced more of it back. The argument over how much ruler Chile can afford will run on — annually, by the look of the 2026 episode. The ruler, meanwhile, keeps arriving in the first days of the school year, carrying more than it did when it left.
