*Three lists of risers* opened this edition by counting: the OECD's PISA 2025 release names improving
systems three times, in three lists, on three clocks, and the United Arab Emirates is on all three of them.
That was a membership, and a membership is where a piece starts rather than where it ends.

All three lists were re-opened in served text for this piece rather than carried across from that one. They
hold. The press release's decade list of eleven: **"Science performance rose in Lithuania, the Slovak
Republic, Türkiye, the Dominican Republic, Georgia, Macao (China), Montenegro, Qatar, Thailand, the United
Arab Emirates, and Uruguay."** The both-ends list of five, below. And the front page's list of systems
**"bucking the trend"**. Four systems are on all three — Türkiye, the Slovak Republic, Montenegro and the
UAE — and the UAE is the only one of the four in this publication's own region.

Read in the release's own performance chapter, the UAE is on every improver list a system can be on — and one
number in the same chapter does not join.

## What the three years say

**Table I.2.6, "Change between 2022 and 2025 in mean performance in science, reading and mathematics"**,
covers every system comparable across those three years in all three subjects. The UAE's row reads **26 in
science, 15 in reading, 21 in mathematics**. The same table's **OECD average-35** row reads **−3, −14 and
−9**.

Three positive figures where the OECD's own average is negative in all three. The chapter counts how
unusual that is: it writes that **"it is equally relevant to note that eight countries and economies
improved their performance in all three subjects."** This publication counted the rows of that table in the
chapter's served bytes — **twelve** systems carry a positive figure in all three columns, the UAE among
them. Twelve is our count of the rows; eight is the chapter's word, and the difference is left
open for the reason below.

## Both ends, on both clocks — which is the part that is not a membership

Table I.2.8 covers the same three years and counts students rather than score points. Its columns are
**"Change in percentage of low performers"** and **"Change in percentage of top performers"**, both in
percentage points, and the UAE's row reads **−10.0** and **+1.7**. The OECD average-35 row reads **1.2** and
**−0.3**. Percentage points are not score points, and no figure from Table I.2.8 is set beside one from
Table I.2.6 here.

What makes those two numbers more than a row is that the chapter names the systems in prose, in a passage itself about statistical significance: **"The share of top performers in science did not change
significantly in the majority of systems (61), increased in 7 systems and decreased in 7 systems."** Then it
names the group that moved at both ends over the three years: **"By contrast, in Jordan, Uzbekistan,
Montenegro, Saudi Arabia, Türkiye, the United Arab Emirates and the United Kingdom, the share of low
performers decreased while the share of top performers increased"**. *(The register's sentence ends in a
comma and runs on; the quotation stops where the clause does.)*

The same chapter asks the same question of the decade and names a different group: **"Only five
countries/economies, Montenegro, Qatar, the Slovak Republic, Türkiye and the United Arab Emirates"** — the
register prints a doubled comma here, so the sentence is quoted split rather than with the artefact —
**"were able to simultaneously reduce their shares of low-performing students and increase their shares of
top-performing students over the past decade."**

The release says that second thing three times, in three registers, and not identically. The press release
prints the list with **no period attached to its own sentence**: **"Türkiye, the Slovak Republic, Montenegro,
Qatar and the United Arab Emirates increased their share of top performers while reducing their share of low
performers in science."** What dates it is the launch speech of **Mathias Cormann**, the OECD's
Secretary-General, given on 8 September 2026 on a page that carries *"Check against delivery"*: **"Over the
past decade, Türkiye, the United Arab Emirates, Montenegro, Qatar and Slovakia all cut their share of low
performers and grew their share of top performers at the same time."** The same five systems — and the
speech names the fifth **Slovakia** where the chapter and the press release both name it the Slovak Republic.

**Seven systems over three years, five over the decade, and the UAE is in both.** That is the finding this
piece exists for, and it is not what the brief commissioned. Being on two lists is ordinary; being on the
*same* list on *two different clocks* is not, because this edition has spent a week establishing that those
clocks disagree. *Qatar's decade and its three years* is on the decade's five and absent from the three
years' seven. *Saudi Arabia's reading, up five and down eighteen* is on the three years' seven and absent
from the decade's five. The UAE is on both.

It is not alone in that, and the piece says so because the number is better than the exception would be:
**Montenegro and Türkiye are on both lists too.** Three of the seven hold on the longer clock and four do
not — and the four fail in two different ways, which is the next paragraph. Three of seven is the clocks'
disagreement counted rather than asserted, and it is a larger claim than one system's membership.

And the two lists are not merely two clocks over one population. **Two of the seven — Jordan and Uzbekistan
— do not appear in the decade's table at all**, because that table shows only systems with
**"at least two previous assessments among PISA 2015, PISA 2018 and PISA 2022"**. So the decade's list could
not have named them whatever their schools did. A system's absence from a list is sometimes a measurement
and sometimes a missing instrument, and the register says which.

## The one line that declines

**Table I.2.9, "Trends in mean performance in science, reading and mathematics since 2015"**, carries the
other clock, and its subtitle says which: **"Based on average decennial trend"**. The chapter defines it —
**"The average decennial trend is the average change, per 10-year period, between PISA 2015 and PISA 2025,
calculated by a linear regression."**

The UAE's row reads **18 in science, −6 in reading, 20 in mathematics**. The OECD average-35 row of the same
table reads **−7, −27 and −24**.

**Before that pair is set beside the three-year pair, the thing that must be said is that it cannot be.** 18
is a line fitted through at least three assessments; 26 is the arithmetic difference between two of them.
This piece does not set 18 against 26, does not set −6 against +15, and does not describe the three-year
reading figure as a recovery of any part of the decade's. They are two instruments, and the register keeps
them in two tables.

What can be said, inside one table, is where the UAE's decade sits among the systems it was just listed
with. Of the five the chapter credits with moving both ends over the decade, the decennial reading trends
are: Montenegro **−13**, Qatar **+18**, the Slovak Republic **−8**, Türkiye **+34**, the UAE **−6**. Two of
the five are positive in all three subjects on this clock and two are negative in two subjects of three.
**The UAE is the only one of the five that is positive in two subjects and negative in exactly one** — and
the one is reading.

That is the whole of the exception, stated at its real size. It is the smallest negative reading trend of
the three negatives in that group of five. It is also the only change in this chapter, on either clock, at
either end of the distribution, that does not go the UAE's way.

## Where the system stands, which is a different question

Every figure above is a change. **Table I.2.1, "Comparing countries' and economies' performance in
science"**, gives the UAE's 2025 science mean as **458**; the chapter's Table I.2.3, whose first column is
that same overall mean, prints it beside the **OECD average of 482**.

Table I.2.1's other column is the company the measurement puts a system in — the systems whose mean is not
statistically different from it. **For the UAE that column holds one system: Viet Nam**, at 457. A band of one
is a narrow place to stand, and it is a statement about one subject in one year rather than a
direction of travel.

## What the register will not let this piece say

**No claim here says that any UAE change is or is not statistically significant on this chapter's
authority.** Table I.2.6 sorts every cell into one of three categories and the legend names them in words —
**"Positive change"**, **"Non-significant change"**, **"Negative change"** — while the assignment of a cell
to one of the three is carried by **the colour of the cell**, and a colour does not survive into served
text. That is why the twelve-against-eight gap above is left open: eight of the twelve improved in all three
subjects on the OECD's test, four did not, and which four is not readable from what this register serves.

## The context the chapter attaches

Chapter 2 does not let a reader take these figures bare. It reports that in **"Luxembourg, Macao (China),
Qatar and the United Arab Emirates, more than half of students have an immigrant background"** — four
participating systems, of which the UAE is one — and it says of them that **"Each should therefore be
examined in its own context"**, naming the profile of the immigrant population, immigration policy, and
educational policy towards immigrant students.

That instruction is the OECD's, it is printed beside the figures, and this piece carries it for the same
reason it carries every hedge: a register's qualifier travels with the register's number.

This is also strictly the national reading. School inspection, the Irtiqaa framework and any single
