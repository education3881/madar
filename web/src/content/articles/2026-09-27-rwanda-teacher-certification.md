A government that expands schooling faster than it can staff it has few options and only one of them is honest. It can lower the standard quietly and go on calling the new hires teachers. It can leave the rooms empty and call it phasing. Or it can put unqualified adults in front of children deliberately, write down in its own records that this is what it has done, and attach a plan to the entry.

Rwanda took the third option, and then did the rarer thing, which is what this piece is about: it kept counting, and published the second count as plainly as the first.

The programme's narrative of record is a joint account by the World Bank and the Rwanda Basic Education Board, published on 27 March 2026 and bylined by a senior education specialist at the Bank and the head of teacher development at REB — the funder and the delivering agency, writing together. It is detailed, and it is not an independent evaluation; nothing below treats it as one. The counting is a different kind of document, and it is the ministry's own.

## The debt, and how it was incurred

Under the Quality Basic Education for Human Capital Development project, Rwanda built classrooms at a rate that outran its teacher pipeline. The scale takes two figures rather than one, because they are figures for two different things. The project financed and built over 11,000 classrooms and 14,680 latrines, per the ministry's own progress note with the World Bank — a page that carried no publication date when it was read, and whose date of 5 November 2025 this piece takes from the ministry's news index rather than from the page. The wider national construction programme, of which that project was one part alongside the government's own money, reached roughly 22,000 classrooms. The funder's feature carries the national total under a project heading; this piece does not.

Rooms need teachers at once. Hiring criteria were relaxed, and in 2021 roughly 25,000 secondary-school graduates — people with subject knowledge and no Teacher Training College qualification — entered classrooms and were carried in the system as uncertified, the training obligation recorded beside the job. That figure is the World Bank and REB's, in the March 2026 account, counting entrants under relaxed criteria in 2021.

## What the ministry counted about itself

The second document is not an account of a programme. It is a school census. MINEDUC's Education Statistical Yearbook is the annual the ministry publishes anyway, and it sorts teaching staff into trained and untrained.

For the school year that ended in July 2024, it counted 10,898 pre-primary and 68,326 primary teaching staff, 79,224 together. Of those, 5,162 in pre-primary were untrained, a 47.4 per cent share, and 22,160 in primary were untrained, a 32.4 per cent share: 27,322 in all. And in explaining why the trained count had moved as it did, the register named the cause in its own voice — newly recruited primary teachers "who may not have received training."

Read the hedge. It is the ministry's, in its own annual, about its own workforce, and it says *may not have* rather than *did not* — the most a census can honestly say about training it did not observe.

Two things are worth separating, because these figures are easy to press into claims they cannot carry. This is not a count of the certification programme's intake, and it is not derived from the funder's account: it is the ministry's own census, on a definition the ministry chose.

The timing is what makes it useful. The programme opened in April 2024; the 2023/24 school year ended in July 2024. So this is the debt as it stood essentially at the programme's opening — the first of two observations from the same table, not the only one.

## The discharge, at scale

The instrument was a Special Programme for the training and certification of uncertified pre-primary and primary teachers, opened by REB in April 2024 and delivered on weekends and public holidays so that no classroom time was lost. Four condensed modules out of the standard college curriculum: Educational Psychology, Pedagogy and Instruction, Inclusive Education, Subject Matter Education. Thirty-six experienced facilitators trained 600 trainers, including college instructors and education specialists, who ran sessions across all thirty districts. The method was micro-teaching: trainees studied on their own, then taught short lessons to peers who watched against structured observation checklists. Childcare was provided at the venues — the kind of design detail that decides who finishes. At the end was an examination — written tests, a comprehensive final, and a practical micro-teaching evaluation run by the National Examination and School Inspection Authority — and passing it earned a Professional Teaching Certificate and official status in the workforce.

By March 2026 the World Bank and REB reported 24,000 teachers trained and certified.

The programme's clock belongs in the same sentence as its duration. The headline account says "in less than one year"; the body of that same account says the programme *aimed* to certify every uncertified pre-primary and primary teacher within one year — a design target, and one that may describe a single teacher's passage through four modules rather than the programme's own span. What is on the record is an opening in April 2024 and a certificate count reported in March 2026, and that is the spread any sentence about duration should carry.

One thing is not on the record at all. How many enrolled in each wave, how many sat the assessment, how many passed and how many remain uncertified are unpublished, and this publication looked and did not find them. The 24,000 is a count of certificates reported by the programme's funder and its delivering agency. It is not a pass rate, and there is no pass rate to read.

## The second count

The yearbook for the school year that ended in July 2025 is cover-dated February 2026 and has been on the ministry's own publications listing since 21 April 2026. It carries the same tables with a new column, and the new column is the finding.

Pre-primary and primary teaching staff: 84,763, against 79,224 the year before. Untrained in primary: 21,717, a 30.2 per cent share, against 22,160 and 32.4 per cent in 2023/24 — a fall of 443. Untrained in pre-primary: 5,692, a 44.2 per cent share, against 5,162 and 47.4 per cent — a rise of 530. Combined, across the two levels, 27,409 in 2024/25 against 27,322 in 2023/24. The stock went up.

The ministry's own words for the primary column, in the register's own prose: the number of untrained teachers "declined only marginally"; and "the persistence of a relatively high number of untrained teachers highlights the urgent need to scale up targeted training and induction programs, particularly for newly recruited primary school teachers."

Now the part that has to be read slowly, because two true numbers sit next to each other and invite an arithmetic that neither will support. The 24,000 is a **flow**: certifications issued over a period ending in March 2026, counted by the programme. The 27,322 and the 27,409 are **stocks**: the untrained population standing in the workforce on two census dates, July 2024 and July 2025, counted by the ministry. They are different kinds of quantity, produced by different bodies for different purposes, and no subtraction, share or residual between them means anything in either direction.

A flow does not reduce a stock if arrivals continue, and the ministry says in its own voice that they did. The pre-primary and primary workforce grew by 5,539 staff in that single year, and the register attributes the persistence of the untrained count to newly recruited primary teachers. Rwanda was certifying faster than it ever had and hiring untrained teachers at least as fast. The trained side of the same table moved too, and the register states it in prose: trained teaching staff in primary rose from 46,166 in 2023/24 to 50,162 in 2024/25.

The two registers are not in disagreement, and nothing here is a catch. They answer different questions, and the same ministry published both — which is the point. This publication has credited the same virtue before, in Brazil, where a literacy programme was worth the attention mainly because the state printed the unkind number beside the kind one.

## The bar moves, and the word is announced

Then the permanent correction. On 10 August 2026 MINEDUC published a release stating that "Beginning in September 2026, learners enrolling in Teacher Training Colleges (TTCs) will undertake a five-year programme instead of the current three-year programme." The release gives its reason in its own terms: the longer programme "will also help bring Rwanda's qualification requirements for primary teachers closer to regional and international standards, where a diploma or bachelor's degree is increasingly required to enter the profession."

That is what the ministry says. What it does not say is worth as much. It names no qualification tier. It attributes the diploma-or-degree standard to the region rather than claiming it for Rwanda. And it says nothing whatever about teachers already in service. Nor does anything read for this piece establish that the first five-year cohort enrolled: what is published is an announcement, dated 10 August 2026, that the programme begins in September 2026 — so *announced* is the word this piece uses, in this sentence and every other one about it.

Rwanda's teaching qualifications do have tiers, and they are the ministry's own. Its salary communiqué of 1 August 2022 uses A2, A1 and A0 and attaches a headcount to each: 68,207 A2 certificate holders, 12,214 A1 diploma holders and 17,547 A0 degree holders — the population of that pay award, across primary and secondary, as it stood in August 2022.

What connects those tiers to the 2026 reform reaches us through one newspaper. The New Times of Kigali reported on 24 August 2026 that the two extra years will let college graduates qualify at A1, and that State Minister for Education Claudette Irere, speaking on 20 August at the release of the 2025/26 national examination results, announced a five-year window for serving A2 holders to upgrade: "We have put in place a five-year period to give teachers an opportunity to upgrade their qualifications." The paper cites no ministry document and states no number of teachers affected.

No register in the state's own voice was found for that window. The ministry's news index was enumerated for this piece on 27 September 2026 and read again on 4 October — thirty-five pages, reverse-chronological, back to 2018 — and on both readings its newest item was the 10 August release: nothing after it, including nothing for the 20 August ceremony itself. The speeches and press-release libraries on the same domain ended in 2024 on both readings. The announcements library ended in January 2026 when it was read on 27 September; its route did not resolve from here on 4 October, which is a different thing from having changed. Four channels on one domain are not the whole of a state's voice, so this is an enumeration and not a verdict. But a reader who went looking on those dates for the upgrade window in Rwanda's own voice did not find it, which is why it appears here as remarks by a named minister on a named date in one newspaper, and not as the argument this piece closes on.

It also cannot be read against the untrained counts, however tempting the shapes are. The window applies to holders of an A2; the 27,409 are counted as untrained. Those are different sets, and nothing read establishes their overlap.

## What cannot be read

One absence is structural and belongs in print rather than in a footnote. Every voice in the programme's record reaches us through the funder's channel or the ministry's own. The most useful is Pelagie Abayikunda, a primary French teacher in Kigali who began teaching in 2021 as a secondary-school graduate: "Through this training, I have learned how to properly manage my classroom… I can now prepare lessons and assessments effectively. I know how to support my students." On what the certificate buys her: "With this certification, I will have stability in my profession. I will be able to request a transfer to another school if needed, qualify for scholarships to continue my studies, and access services from Umwalimu SACCO when I need financial support."

That is a teacher describing her own working life, inside the funder's own feature. There is no independent evaluator in the corpus as read, no teachers' union, no critical academic; the pass rate is unpublished. Both are stated here rather than balanced away, because a manufactured counterweight would be worth less than the admission.

And the bill arrives. The same 10 August release announces that from January 2027 parental contributions rise: pre-primary and primary from 975 to 2,000 francs, day secondary from 19,500 to 24,000, boarding from 85,000 to 110,000.

## Four moments, not three

The sequence reads as one instrument with four moments. Rwanda expanded access and incurred a quality debt on purpose. It disclosed the debt in a document it publishes anyway, on a definition it chose, with the cause attributed to its own recruitment. It discharged at scale — 24,000 certificates, weekends and holidays, thirty districts, an examination a teacher could fail. And then it disclosed again, in the next column of the same table, that the stock had not moved.

The fourth moment is the one worth borrowing. Any ministry can announce a training programme, and most that do never publish a figure against it. What is rare is a ministry that prints, in its own statistical annual, the number that undercuts its own funder's headline, and names its own hiring as the reason. The accomplishment here was never the discharge, which is a large and genuine piece of work a stock count cannot see. It is the bookkeeping — legible because the category has a name, each count carries a school year beside it, and the second count was published as plainly as the first.
