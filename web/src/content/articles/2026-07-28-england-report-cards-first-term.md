For a generation, an English school's inspection ended in a single word. *Outstanding*, *good*, *requires improvement*, *inadequate* — one of four, printed at the top of the report and, in practice, printed over everything the school did afterward: on the gate sign, in the estate-agent listing, in the parent's decision. On 10 November 2025 the word came down. In its place England's inspectorate, Ofsted — whose writ runs to England alone, the other three UK nations keeping their own inspectorates — began issuing a report card: a grid. Not one verdict but a set of them, one for each of a minimum of several evaluation areas, each area coloured on a five-point scale that runs *urgent improvement, needs attention, expected standard, strong standard, exceptional*, with safeguarding reported separately as simply met or not met and inclusion promoted to an evaluation area of its own. The single word had the vice of its virtue: it was legible precisely because it flattened. What replaced it is a page that refuses to flatten — and the first full term of that refusal is now on the record, along with the first attempts to measure whether the refusal was an improvement.

That the word came down at all is owed to a death: a coroner ruled in 2023 that an Ofsted inspection had contributed to the suicide of the head teacher Ruth Perry, and the grid is, among other things, the state's answer to that finding. (Oman, which adopted England's older single-verdict model at almost the exact moment England was dismantling it, is the frame's mirror image; this piece lives entirely in the replacement.) What the grid changed, mechanically, is the unit of judgment. A single word is a weighted average a school can dispute only as a whole; a grid of graded areas is a set of separable findings, each defensible or contestable on its own — but each also governed by a rule Ofsted calls "secure fit," under which an area earns a grade only if *every* standard in that band is met, so that one shortfall pulls the whole area down to the band below. Legibility was not abolished so much as multiplied: where there was one verdict to carry, there are now several, and a school "strong" in five areas and "needs attention" in a sixth wears both at once.

The first cards let you see the shape of the thing. By mid-January 2026, Ofsted had published cards for 22 volunteer schools — 13 primary, 9 secondary — the earliest adopters, inspected first because they put themselves forward. Across those cards, 150 individual grades were awarded, and they clustered where a cautious reader would expect the earliest and most willing schools to cluster: 79 at *expected standard*, 58 at *strong standard*, 9 *exceptional* (spread across two primaries and one secondary), just 4 at *needs attention*, and not a single *urgent improvement*. The distribution is real and it is also an artefact — volunteering selects for confidence, and a grid built to spread schools out will look bunched when only the bunched-in-the-middle step forward. The truer test is scale, and scale came fast: by the end of May 2026, Ofsted had inspected 1,360 state schools under the new framework. The instrument was no longer a pilot. It was the weather.

And here the edition's question arrives in its English form. A measuring system had been rebuilt to be fairer; was it? Within the first eight months two rival instruments answered, and they disagreed — because they measured differently. The first was an independent survey published in June 2026 by a group of academics and former senior inspectors — Frank Coffield and Peter Tymms, Christine Pascal, and the former His Majesty's Inspectors Frank Norris and Colin Richards — who gathered the accounts of 100 heads whose schools had been inspected under the new cards. Its readings were bleak: 70 percent negative about the inspection's effect on their wellbeing, 64 percent saying the framework was not an improvement on the discredited one it replaced, eight declaring they would leave before facing it again, and only 53 percent feeling the grades their schools received were fair. The authors called the wellbeing findings "deeply disturbing," and noted the sharpest possible irony — that a framework prompted by a head's death was found, by these heads, to be harming heads' wellbeing still. The survey's own limits are part of its honest reading, and the authors report them: 100 self-selected respondents, and a response rate that itself skewed — 7.9 percent from schools in trusts against 24.5 percent from council-run schools — so that the sample leans toward those with something to say.

The second instrument had not reported when this piece was written, and it has since reported in part. Ofsted's answer to the survey was that it had commissioned a rigorous, independent evaluation of the reforms, carried out by IFF Research, to which every inspected provider would be invited, the terms of reference putting the full report in 2027 — and that its own post-inspection surveys, one covering the new framework expected later in 2026, would add a further reading. On 9 October 2026 Ofsted published that evaluation's summary of interim findings, drawn from the first four waves of surveys and covering inspections published before the end of May 2026; what it found is in the October reading at the foot of this piece. Each instrument's design shapes what it can find: a self-selected letter survey captures the intensity of the aggrieved and the grateful and cannot weight them; a commissioned universal survey reaches everyone and is administered by the body under examination, which has its own gravity. The watchdog notes that its published post-inspection data carry no narrative responses and that schools with positive experiences are more likely to take part. Neither instrument is the neutral one. The edition's recurring finding holds here as it held for the American proficiency maps: the number you get depends on the ruler you pick up, and the honest move is to name the ruler.

The courts were asked to referee and declined. The National Association of Head Teachers brought a judicial review of the reforms; in November 2025 Mr Justice Saini refused it, holding that the merits of the grading system and its approach to wellbeing "are not matters for this court" and that the true complaint concerned "policy choice and system design" — the business of ministers and the inspectorate, not judges. The union's appeal failed, and its general secretary Paul Whiteman said the NAHT would consult members on industrial action. The grid, in other words, is not going to be adjudicated into fairness from the bench; whatever recalibration it gets, it will get from within.

Some has already begun, which is the surest sign the ruler is still being cut. Ofsted's own 12 June 2026 update put the change from September 2026: a new statistical model, built with the Department for Education, that groups schools with similar intakes so that inspectors can read a disadvantaged school's attainment against comparable schools rather than the national average alone — a direct response to the survey's loudest complaint, that context was not being counted, voiced by more than a quarter of the heads unprompted and sharpest from those in the hardest circumstances. The correction comes with a caveat of its own: no early-cohort school will be reinspected because the measure has changed, so the schools that raised the objection carry the grade issued before it was heard. Ofsted's national director framed the same update in the language the whole apparatus turns on — context, he wrote, "is part of inspection, but never pre-determines the grades we give." That is the promise a grid makes that a single word could not even attempt: that the judgment will be many-sided, and that each side can be read, disputed, and, where it was wrong, revised. Whether the promise is kept is now itself a measured quantity — read one way by the heads who lived the first term, another by an evaluation two years out, and by neither yet as settled. England took the verdict apart to see it better. The first thing it saw was how hard the seeing is.

**The September 2026 date has arrived, and this is the reading of it, taken on 30 October 2026.** The model exists and is in force. From September 2026 Ofsted includes similar schools information in the inspection data summary report an inspector reads before the visit, and the Department for Education's model gives every mainstream primary and secondary a list of 50 similar schools. Its variables are published: prior attainment; disadvantage, measured by pupil premium eligibility, IDACI and POLAR4; pupil numbers and cohort stability; and the proportions of pupils on special educational needs support, with an education, health and care plan, and with English as an additional language. Nine entries in four groups, counted off the register's own list — and not one of them is where the school is. The register also carries a limit the July sentence above could not: "The data will not be used to make grading decisions." The measure the heads asked for arrives as context in front of the inspector, not as an input to the grade.

Ofsted's own statistical commentary, updated 16 October 2026, then puts a number under the caveat this piece already carried. The similar schools measure "was not available during our 2025/26 inspections" — a year in which Ofsted carried out nearly 3,300 inspections, "just 15% of state-funded schools" in its own words, nearly 3,200 of them full inspections that graded achievement. The first cohort was graded before the ruler was cut, and it is not being reinspected. The commentary checks those grades against the new measure after the fact: 73% of primary schools and 58% of secondaries graded strong standard or exceptional for achievement were above their similar schools average, and among schools with high free school meal proportions the figures are higher rather than lower — 88% and 68%. Ofsted reads this as evidence that "similar schools data supports conclusions that inspectors were already reaching", and states its own bound in the same document: "the outcomes of inspections carried out in a single year are not representative of the school population as a whole", because schools are selected for inspection by their previous outcomes. The same document prints a gradient it does not resolve. Only 17% of primary schools in the North East, Yorkshire and the Humber were graded strong standard or exceptional for achievement against 44% in London; in the North West, 18% of secondaries against 57% in London, with 41% of the 119 North West secondaries inspected below the expected standard, more than three times London's rate — and both regions, Ofsted notes, have fairly similar pupil numbers and free school meal proportions. From the interim evaluation, the figures Ofsted quotes are a pair: two thirds (65%) of schools said inspection teams sought to understand their context and took account of it, while less than half (47%) of secondary schools agreed their inspection was tailored to theirs. Ofsted's own gloss on the pair is the plainest sentence in the document: "This shows that Ofsted still has work to do." Those two figures are cited as Ofsted quotes them and not as the evaluation prints them: the evaluation's summary is a ten-page PDF no client available here could open, which is a fact about our reading and not about the document.
