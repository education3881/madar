The usual order of an examination reform is announce, legislate, examine, and then — once a cohort is holding results nobody has seen before — tell families what those results are actually worth. The last step is the one that slips. A new certificate exists on paper long before anyone can say which university door it opens, and the families asked to choose it are asked to choose in the dark.

Egypt ran the sequence in the other direction. On 25 July 2025, a full year before any student would sit a baccalaureate examination, the Ministry of Education and Technical Education and the Ministry of Higher Education and Scientific Research jointly published which university faculties and majors each track of the new certificate opens — and said, in the release itself, why: "Parity between the baccalaureate tracks and their corresponding secondary school streams has been taken into account to ensure fair selection."

That is the subject of this piece. Not whether Egypt's new certificate works — nothing served can answer that yet — but the narrower and more checkable thing the Egyptian state has done, which is to write down the consequences of the choice before asking anyone to make it.

## What the law actually says

The instrument is Law 169 of 2025, amending the Education Law issued by Law 139 of 1981, and it was issued at the Presidency on 19 Safar 1447, corresponding to 13 August 2025. Its baccalaureate chapter is short and unusually legible, and the operative sentence is the first one: the baccalaureate is **an optional, free system** — «يكون نظام البكالوريا نظامًا اختياريًا مجانيًا» — open to anyone holding the basic-education completion certificate. Study runs three years. On completion the student is granted a certificate equivalent to the Thanaweya Amma certificate, the examination it now stands beside rather than on top of.

Two further provisions matter more than they look. The first is the commencement clause, which does not name a date at all: the law is published in the Official Gazette and comes into force "from the academic year following the date of its publication." The second is the clause that gets least attention and does the most work — **no transfer into the system or out of it from the other secondary systems during the years of study.** The statute grants a choice and, in the same chapter, closes the exit behind it.

The fees are in the statute too, which is itself worth noting: first sitting free, and a retake bounded at two hundred Egyptian pounds per attempt and not exceeding four hundred per subject. These figures were read on three separate Arabic reproductions of the law today, and the reproductions disagree about which numbered sub-article carries them — so they are reported here by what they say and never by article number.

## What the doors release did and did not publish

The July 2025 release is the strongest evidence that this is an implemented instrument rather than an announced one, and it should be described exactly. Its prose states that the tracks, the corresponding secondary streams and the faculties and majors for each "are detailed in the images attached to this statement." The mapping is published as pictures. The parity sentence is text; the table is a photograph of a table.

That limit is worth stating plainly rather than stepping around, because it bounds what can honestly be said. What is established is that two ministries committed in public, a year ahead of the first examinations, to a published track-to-faculty map and to parity with the existing streams. What is not established from this register is any particular faculty's entry for any particular track, and none is claimed here.

## The objection, on the bill as it stood

A reform is not described by its supporters alone, and Egypt's own record supplies the other half. On 21 July 2025 — while the bill was in parliament, weeks before it became law — the Egyptian Initiative for Personal Rights published its objection to the amendments as submitted. It is worth reading for what it is: a critique of a draft, not of the enacted chapter.

EIPR objected that the cabinet-drafted amendments were "referred to parliament just days before the end of the current parliamentary term, without serious societal dialogue", and that the introduction of fee-bearing programmes would widen an existing gap. Its evidence is the state's own: according to the income and expenditure survey issued by the Central Agency for Public Mobilization and Statistics, "the share of the top 10% of students in highest-income households from spending on education in 2019-2020 was more than 22 times the share of the bottom 10%." It notes that the constitution allocates 4% of GDP to pre-university and 2% to university education, and that "the state has not adhered to these percentages to date."

One of its objections concerns retake fees, and it is a different provision from the baccalaureate's. EIPR was writing about the bill's general amendment on retakes, which set fees "not less than two hundred pounds and not more than two thousand pounds" where "the fees prescribed in the old law were not less than 10 pounds and not more than 20 pounds." That is a separate clause from the baccalaureate chapter's own bounded fee, and the two should not be narrated as one number. EIPR also records, in the same statement, that the amendments extend compulsory education to the secondary stage in compliance with Article 19 of the constitution — a fact reported here because the register reports it, and because a critic's own concession is evidence.

## A choice is only a choice against its alternative

The optionality is guaranteed in the statute. Whether it survived contact with a ministry that wanted the new system to succeed is contested, in Egypt, in public, and the piece carries all three voices in one breath because no reading from outside Cairo can settle it.

After the Minister's 31 August 2025 meeting with approximately 4,000 secondary school administrators, MP Freddy Elbaiady claimed the Minister had signalled his intent to personally monitor daily enrolment numbers. "This is not persuasion," he said. "This is coercion masquerading as awareness-raising." The Egyptian Social Democratic Party warned that "we're seeing students who prefer the old system pushed out—transferred to distant schools or less-equipped facilities." The Ministry answered by characterising the baccalaureate as "a student-friendly model with fewer subjects, multiple assessment opportunities, and less pressure than the high-stakes traditional Thanaweya Amma exam", and officials said the Minister had merely encouraged administrators to inform parents of advantages and provide counselling.

Those are three claims, not one finding, and they sit alongside the statutory fact established above: a family choosing this certificate is choosing something it cannot leave mid-course. That is not an allegation. It is the law, and it is the reason the optionality guarantee is load-bearing rather than decorative.

## The rules kept arriving

The publishing did not stop at the doors. Ministerial Decree 213 of 2025 is named as the instrument that defines the four tracks. On 17 August 2026 the ministry announced further detail following two ministerial decrees, one of which allows third-year students in the certificate to change track to any of the four, on condition that the student studies the new track's specialised subjects. That is a door **inside** the system; the statute's bar on transfer **between** systems is a different clause and is untouched by it. The register that reported the decree does not draw that distinction. It is drawn here, on the statute's authority.

Two weeks earlier, on 3 August 2026, the admissions arithmetic was set out: admission for baccalaureate graduates runs through the same coordination system as Thanaweya Amma, with the Supreme Council of Universities and the Ministry of Higher Education, and "the proportion of university seats allocated to Egyptian Baccalaureate students will correspond to their share based on their overall scores" — published with a worked example, to the effect that where a score of 90% qualifies for a programme, a baccalaureate student at the equivalent overall score is eligible on the same criterion, with no increase or decrease. This reaches us through a government portal that names its own source as a newspaper, so it is reported here as that report and not as the ministry speaking.

Still, the shape is the same act twice: the consequence published before the event.

## The Baccalaureate that is not that Baccalaureate

One clarification, because the shared word invites a false claim in both directions. The Egyptian Baccalaureate is a national certificate created by Egyptian statute. The International Baccalaureate is a different certificate offered by a different organisation. There is now a relationship between them, and its served description is narrow: Daily News Egypt reported on 16 August 2026 that ministerial decrees would follow the International Baccalaureate Organization's approval of the second-year curricula, and the August 2026 ministry account describes a cooperation agreement with the organisation "to establish a framework for reviewing curricula, implementing quality standards, and aligning the Egyptian Baccalaureate with international educational benchmarks."

Approval of curricula, a framework for reviewing, a cooperation agreement. None of those verbs is accreditation and none is equivalence, and this piece does not upgrade them. One further limit belongs in the same paragraph: the organisation's own register could not be read from here — its site refused this reader and no archive holds the page — so everything above is Egypt's account of an agreement with a second party we have not heard from.

## The one thing not published

There is a number this piece does not contain, and its absence is deliberate rather than accidental.

Egypt has published, in its own voice, this certificate's tracks, its subject structure, its examination design, its duration, its fees, its faculty map, its admissions arithmetic and an international cooperation agreement — much of it before the first cohort sat anything. It has not published, anywhere this publication could find across two hunts a month apart, in English and in Arabic, against ministry vocabulary and against statistical-agency vocabulary, **how many students are in it.** Figures circulate; none of them is the state's. So this piece reports what the certificate is, and not how many have taken it, and the sentence you would most like to read here is the one that cannot yet be written.

## What can be checked

Speaking in London on 18 May 2026, in a system of 25 million pre-university students, Minister Mohamed Abdellatif described the certificate as "a structural and philosophical shift aimed at moving learning away from exam pressure towards mastery, applied knowledge, research, critical thinking, and student choice", and put the argument for it in one line: "If we evaluate memorisation only, we teach memorisation."

Whether Egypt's second certificate changes what an Egyptian classroom teaches is a question only measurement will answer, and that measurement does not exist yet. What can be checked today is narrower and not nothing. Before the examinations existed, the doors were listed. Before the first cohort chose, the price was in the statute. Before the first results were issued, the arithmetic that converts them into a university place was in public.

That is not a result. It is the condition under which a result could later be believed — and it is the part of an education reform that is easiest to skip and hardest to retrofit.
