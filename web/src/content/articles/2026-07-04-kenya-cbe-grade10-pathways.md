In the second week of January 2026, 1.13 million Kenyan fourteen-year-olds walked into senior schools they had been assigned to by a single national exercise. Each of them had sat the first Kenya Junior School Education Assessment — the KJSEA, the first exit examination of the competency-based reform that replaced the old 8-4-4 system — and each had then been read by a placement instrument that weighed three assessments together: 60% from the KJSEA itself, 20% from school-based assessments scored across Grades 7 and 8, and 20% from the primary-school assessment sat back in Grade 6. The instrument weighed merit against school capacity against the choices each family had registered on a ministry platform, and placed every child into a school and into one of three pathways — STEM, Social Sciences, or Arts and Sports Science. Kenya has, in one pass and at national scale, run the exercise that every tracking debate in education is about: it decided, at fourteen, which direction a child's schooling points.

The newness is worth pausing on, because almost nothing about the exercise had been done before. The cohort — 578,630 boys and 551,829 girls — is the first to complete the competency-based curriculum from start of primary school to the junior-school exit. The examination was graded on a new scale: nine subjects, eight performance levels, a maximum of 72 points. And the results were published without ranking — no league table of schools, no top candidate on the front pages, a deliberate break with half a century of examination culture. What replaced the ranking is more interesting than what it removed: a set of national figures about where the cohort could go, and then a second set about where it asked to go, and a third about where it went.

Which brings the piece to the STEM numbers, of which Kenya now has four. The standing policy target: 60% of senior-school learners into STEM. The examination council's reading of the cohort: 59.09% "demonstrated potential to pursue" the STEM pathway. The families' answer, registered on the selection platform: more than 600,000 chose STEM first — roughly 53% — against 437,000 for Social Sciences and 124,000 for Arts and Sports. And the placement machine's final word, once the reviews had closed: 51% placed in STEM, 38% in Social Sciences, 11% in Arts and Sports. Four numbers — the target, the potential, the choice, the placement — and they never quite meet. It would be easy to collapse them into a single "Kenya STEM rate," and each of the four bodies involved has an interest in the number nearest its own work. But they are answers to four different questions: what the state wants, what the instrument measured, what households asked for, and what the seats could hold. One more discipline belongs beside them: the council's potential figures — 59.09% STEM, 46.52% Social Sciences, 48.73% Arts and Sports — sum far past one hundred, because a learner can demonstrate potential in more than one pathway. Potential is a threshold, not a partition; whoever quotes 59.09% as a share of a divided cake has already misread the instrument.

In aggregate, the system had room. Kenya counts 9,540 public senior schools with capacity for 2.2 million learners — nearly twice the cohort. And yet 20,000 of the cohort sought one of roughly 500 Grade 10 places at each of three storied national schools — Alliance, Kenya High, Mang'u — a demand figure the education ministry itself put on the record. Both facts are true at once, and neither cancels the other: the capacity headline says the country can seat its children; the distribution reality says the seats are not the ones being fought over. What the state did next is the part most systems skip. It published its own correction ledger: 355,457 applications for review after the December placements — very nearly one placement in three contested — of which 211,636 were approved and 143,821 rejected, mostly for a missing subject combination or want of capacity in the school requested. Then it opened a second window, 6–9 January, for those still unplaced to their satisfaction, and by reporting day could state that 88% of learners stood in a school of their original or revised selection. Read one way, a third of the country disputing its placements is chaos — the word the Kenyan press reached for. Read the other way, a state that runs a million-child algorithm, counts the objections in public, and reruns the window is doing something rarer than getting it right the first time: it is publishing its own error rate. The ledger, not the algorithm, is the innovation worth exporting.

Placement, though, is not provision. Before the cohort ever reported, the Teachers Service Commission had put its own deficit on the record: 58,590 teachers short for the senior-school roll-out, 35,111 of them in STEM alone — a projection built, tellingly, on the 60% target rather than the 51% eventually placed, on a planning basis of 677,144 STEM learners in some 15,046 classes. By late January the press was reporting Grade 10 STEM learners in some schools going without specialised teachers as the term began. Willy Kuria, the serving principal who chairs the Kenya Senior School Heads Association, put it without varnish: "We have a serious staffing deficit… I doubt there is a school in the country that is fully equipped with these teachers" — naming aviation, marine technology, building and construction, the specialised subjects the new pathways promise on paper. By June the commission had moved: at the heads' annual conference in Mombasa it announced it would scrap the rule requiring teachers to qualify in two subjects, so that a person able to teach one scarce thing can be hired to teach it. The machinery is adapting, which is both the reassurance and the measure of how far provision trailed placement.

Two quieter seams belong on the record. The first sits inside the placement score itself: 20% of the number that placed each child was scored by the child's own school. Kenya has blended an externally-set examination and a self-administered component inside a single national figure — the two rulers a country like Brazil keeps as separate published numbers, Kenya carries within one. That is a design choice, not a scandal; but a score that is one-fifth self-reported should be named as such wherever it is quoted. The second seam is the dispute about who should place at all. KESSHA — the same association whose chairman receives the placed cohort — has demanded a return to school-led placement, the principals choosing their intake as they long did. The dispute is carried here, not settled: the operators who live with the algorithm's output would rather choose; the ministry, having published its ledger and run its second window, evidently holds that a central formula plus a public appeals process is fairer than ten thousand local decisions. Where a reader stands may depend on which failure they fear more — one algorithm's blind spots, or ten thousand gatekeepers' ones.

What Kenya has done, in the end, is run the tracking debate as an operation instead of an argument. The four STEM numbers will be quoted for years, usually one at a time and usually without their siblings; they are better read together, as a sentence: the state wanted 60, the instrument found 59, the families asked 53, and the seats held 51. Every education system that sorts children tells itself a version of that sentence — most just never write the middle clauses down. Kenya wrote them down, published the objections, and reran the window. The part that remains unwritten is the one Kuria keeps pointing at, and it outlasts placement week: not where the children were put, but who is standing in front of them.
