The Education (Amendment) Act's central sentence is fourteen words long, and its grammar is the whole story. *"A child who is a citizen shall have the right to free education."* Not a programme, not a commitment, not a budget line renewed each October: a right, vested in a person, enforceable against the state. Zambia had already abolished school fees — a policy announcement in January 2022, delivered by administrative circular and sustained by annual appropriation, the kind of thing a later government can end with a memo. What the President assented to on 4 June 2026 converts that policy into statute. The change is not what a child receives. It is who has to justify taking it away.

That is the accomplishment, and it is real. It is also, in the way of instruments read closely, not the most consequential thing the Act does. Buried in the same text is a redefinition: *primary education* now means grade one to grade six. It used to mean grade one to grade seven. Zambia's headline access statistic — the one cited in every donor report, every ministerial speech and every country brief for two decades — is Grade 7 completion. The law that guarantees free education also moved the line at which primary school is finished. **What happens to the statistics that measured whether primary school was working, when a country rewrites the law that defines primary school?**

## What the statute actually contains

Four clauses are worth reading in the text rather than in the coverage of it, because the coverage carries none of them.

**The right is gated on citizenship.** Section 15(3) requires a parent or guardian to *"provide proof of citizenship of that child, as prescribed"* at the time of enrolment. Free education is a citizen's right. Zambia hosts long-established refugee settlements at Meheba and Mayukwayukwa, and the Act does not extend the right to the children in them.

**Helping a non-citizen child access it is a criminal offence.** Section 15(4) makes anyone who *"aids, abets, counsels, procures or solicits"* free education for a non-citizen child liable to a fine of up to 300,000 penalty units or three years' imprisonment, or both. The word doing the work there is *counsels*. On the face of the text, the person exposed is not a smuggler but a head teacher.

**Accommodation is now inside the fee ban, and defined.** Section 119(1) bars public and community institutions from charging admission, tuition **and accommodation**, and section 2 defines accommodation precisely: *"the provision of bed space, a bed and mattress to a learner residing at an educational institution."* This is a genuine expansion beyond the 2022 policy rather than a restatement of it. When tuition went, boarding cost was the barrier that stayed — the reason a rural child admitted to a distant secondary school still did not go. The statute names the bed.

**And there is an escape hatch.** Section 119(2) lets the Minister, by statutory instrument, prescribe fees *"in respect of services or programmes not covered under free education."* The Act does not define that category. Any future re-charging will happen through this subsection, and it will not require Parliament.

The structural reform sits alongside. Secondary education becomes form one to form six; the basic-school and high-school categories are deleted outright; the Schedule gives schools from 30 May 2026 to 30 May 2031 to convert. A new eight-key-stage framework writes **day care (0–3), nursery (3–4) and reception (4–5)** into statute for the first time — early childhood entering Zambian law not as a service the ministry provides but as a stage of education the law recognises.

## The audit, and why the aggregates are missing from this piece

There is a number this article does not contain, and the omission is deliberate enough to state. Zambia's government has said that 2.3 million children returned to school under free education; and 2.5 million; and 2.6 million. Three official ledgers, three figures, none disclosing a base year or a counting rule.

We know why now, and it is the government that tells us. In its performance audit of the free-education policy, conducted under Article 250 of the Constitution and laid before the Committee on Education, Science and Technology, the Auditor General records the Ministry's own admission that the 2022–2023 Education Statistics Bulletin was published only in **November 2024** — a lag of roughly two years — and stakeholder testimony that outdated enrolment figures had limited the Ministry's capacity to plan and monitor the very policy being audited. The national statistical system was two years behind the claims being made from it. A number produced that way is not usable, whatever its provenance, and the honest move is to say so and carry the piece on the instruments that do hold.

The audit is the best of those instruments, and it comes with a limitation the reader is owed. In its own §2.6, the audit states that resource constraints meant it *"sampled schools that were near the Central Business District."* Questionnaires went to 61 further districts and 27 responded — 44 per cent. This is the only independent implementation audit of Zambian free education that exists. It is not a national picture, and nothing below should be read as one.

## The table that does not add

The Ministry submitted its teacher-recruitment record to the audit. For 2024, against plan:

| Level | Planned | Recruited |
|---|---|---|
| Early childhood | 2,000 | 548 |
| Primary | 3,000 | 1,057 |
| Secondary | 3,000 | 2,595 |
| *Printed total* | *8,000* | *7,841* |

The three line items sum to **4,200**. The printed total reads **7,841**. The table's other three columns add exactly, which is what makes this an internal arithmetic failure rather than an ambiguity of scope we have invented. Read from the printed total, the Ministry delivered 98 per cent of its 2024 recruitment plan. Read from the components, it delivered 52.5 per cent. We do not adjudicate between them, and we do not round: the components are the part we can stand behind, the total is what the document prints, and they disagree.

The line that matters most for this publication's remit is the first one. **Early childhood: 548 teachers recruited against 2,000 planned — 27.4 per cent** — in the same year the country wrote day care, nursery and reception into statute. The law recognised the stage; the workforce arrived at a quarter of plan.

The construction ledger, by contrast, reconciles, and it rescues a figure that has been repeated for a year without a traceable composition. Of 115 government secondary schools, 69 are complete and operational, 36 have contractors re-engaged and 10 await final accounts. Under the separate ZEEP programme, 202 schools comprise 82 operational and 120 under construction. Sixty-nine plus eighty-two is **151** — which is exactly the "151 secondary schools completed" that circulates in speeches, now traced to its two component programme lines for the first time and usable because of it. The early-years equivalent carries a qualifier that must travel with it: 222 ZEEL centres, 110 hub and 112 satellite, *all at different stages of construction*.

Three further findings from the same audit describe the system that has to absorb all this. One Education Standards Officer may be responsible for inspecting up to 69 schools. Between 2021 and 2024 the communications regulator distributed 480 computers and 24 printers to 24 schools across ten provinces. And in most schools the learner-to-textbook ratio remains worse than the recommended one-to-one.

## What the teachers cost, and what the results say

The workforce build is where free education was always going to be won or lost, and Zambia's 2022 push is documented in a register that has held up. In a ministerial statement to the National Assembly in June 2022, the Minister of Education, Douglas Syakalima, reported **K1,994,817,600** provided in that year's national budget for recruitment, and **100,877 applications against 30,000 posts** — with a provincial breakdown showing where the pressure sat: Central province, 17,513 applicants for 4,150 places; Western, 7,153 for 3,600; Lusaka, 5,318 for 740. That statement was delivered before the exercise closed, and it reports 30,000 posts *allocated*. It is not a record of 30,000 teachers in classrooms, and the two are not interchangeable. The Minister also gave pre-recruitment ratios of one teacher to 58 pupils in primary and one to 38 in secondary, against national standards of 1:45 and 1:35 — announced figures, vintage 2022, with the underlying source unstated.

At the other end of the system, the 2025 Grade 12 results were announced in January 2026 and read, on their face, as vindication: a pass rate of **70.26 per cent**, up from 68.19 per cent the year before, and above 70 for the first time. The figure needs its scope stated in the same breath. It counts full School Certificates — 136,434 of them — among the **194,148 candidates who sat**. A further **54,771** received a statement of results, which is not a certificate. The denominator is entrants, not the age cohort; and the cohort has just been swollen by the very intake this Act is about. A rising numerator over a self-selected denominator is not the same thing as more young Zambians finishing school. The Minister attributed the gain to free education, teacher recruitment, monitoring, grants and school feeding — an attribution, offered by an interested party, not a finding.

One number in that announcement is unambiguously an achievement and is worth lifting out. The Examinations Council built its own in-house results-processing system for **K4.7 million**, a move its Executive Director says saved the government about **K15 million** that would otherwise have gone on procuring an external system, and cut processing from as long as eight days to **48 hours**. Its Executive Director, Michael Chilala, put those figures on the record. A state agency writing its own software, cheaper and faster than buying it, is the kind of institutional competence that never makes a headline and quietly determines whether everything else works.

## The floor the law does not reach

Against all of this sits a measurement that has not moved. UNESCO's institute for capacity building in Africa puts Zambian learning poverty at **99 per cent**. On the Human Capital Index, a Zambian child can expect 8.8 years of schooling, which — adjusted for a harmonised learning outcome of 358 — is worth **five years** of learning; a child born today is projected to reach 40 per cent of their potential productivity. The same brief carries Grade 7 completion at 97 per cent in 2019 and 86.4 per cent in 2020, with Grade 9 pass at 53 per cent and Grade 12 at 64 per cent.

This is not a rebuttal of the statute. It is the measurement of what a statute can and cannot do. A law can remove a fee, and Zambia's now removes the bed charge too, which no previous instrument did. A law can make a right enforceable, which is a real and durable thing to have built. What a law cannot do is teach a child to read; that requires the 2,000 early-years teachers the plan called for rather than the 548 it got, and the textbooks the audit found missing, and the standards officer who is not covering 69 schools.

## The finish line, and the election

Which returns to the redefinition. In 2002 Zambia's Free Basic Education Policy abolished tuition, in its own words, *"for grades 1 to 7."* In 2026 the Education (Amendment) Act defines primary education as grades one to six. The policy that opened the series and the Act that closes it are drawn on the same boundary — and the boundary has moved. Grade 7 completion, the country's most-cited access statistic for twenty years, now measures the completion of something the law no longer calls primary school. The series does not become false. It becomes discontinuous, in a way that is easy to miss and hard to repair afterwards, and the moment to say so is before the first post-Act figures are published rather than after they have been compared to the old ones.

The timing deserves naming rather than tiptoeing around. Zambia goes to a general election in **August 2026**, two months after assent, with free education at the centre of the campaign. That does not make the statute less real — the citizenship gate, the criminal penalty and the accommodation definition are not campaign material, and no one running for office would have written the escape hatch in section 119(2) if the point were only to be seen to give something away. But it does mean that the aggregates being quoted around the Act are being quoted in a season, and that the audit, the sums that do not reconcile and the 27.4 per cent are the parts a reader should hold on to.

Zambia did the difficult, unglamorous thing: it took a policy that could be revoked by memorandum and made it a right that has to be repealed in Parliament. Then, in the same document, it moved the marker the country had used to measure itself. Both are in the text. Only one is in the coverage.

— *The Editors*
