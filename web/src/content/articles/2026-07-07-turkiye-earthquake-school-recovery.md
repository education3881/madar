The Ali Sayar Vocational and Technical Anatolian High School reopened in Hatay in October 2024: sixteen classrooms, steel-framed construction, a new building in place of one the earthquakes of 6 February 2023 had left unusable. Reopenings in Türkiye's earthquake provinces follow a script by now, and the script's most interesting line belonged to the man who runs schooling in the hardest-hit province. "Work continues at 113 of our schools in Hatay," Harun Tüysüz, the provincial director of national education, said at the ceremony. "Thirteen of them are again being built by the Ahbap Association. God willing, we expect even more by the end of next year." No ribbon-cutting rhetoric — a work-in-progress count and a delivery forecast. In this recovery, a reopening is a ledger entry, and the ledger, not any single building, is the mechanism worth studying.

Read in order, the ledger's lines run like this. The earthquakes put about 9,500 classrooms out of use across eleven provinces — the education ministry's own count of the loss as it stood in February 2025, which its own February 2026 report revised to 9,799, against a pre-quake stock of 119,200. By 9 September 2024, the start of the following school year, more than 7,500 destroyed classrooms had been rebuilt and returned to service — a figure the minister, Yusuf Tekin, specified excludes repair and strengthening work. By the third anniversary, 6 February 2026, the ministry's own report put the count at 14,202 classrooms completed, with 655 schools and 8,648 classrooms still under construction and a further 70 schools and 2,352 classrooms being strengthened. A news agency carrying the minister's remarks that same day put the completed count at 14,304 and the under-construction count at 9,763. The two do not reconcile, and they are not of equal rank: where a podium and its own owner's published document disagree, the document is the register. The report's decomposition is the ledger at full resolution. Before 6 February 2023 the eleven provinces ran 11,567 schools and 119,200 classrooms. After it, 10,272 schools and 109,401 classrooms stood undamaged or lightly damaged, and 1,295 schools with 9,799 classrooms could not be used. Against that loss the ministry sets 915 completed projects worth some 72 billion lira at today's value, about 85,000 lightly damaged classrooms repaired, and 233 schools with 2,965 classrooms strengthened rather than replaced. Read together, those lines reorder the story the headline tells. The number the releases lead with is new construction, and the largest number in the ledger is repair: about 85,000 classrooms brought back against 14,202 built new. A rebuild is what gets announced. Most of a recovery is mending. Behind the running totals sits an investment programme of 1,241 new schools and 19,784 new classrooms, with 3,119 more buildings strengthened rather than replaced. And above all of it sits the target that reframes the whole exercise: some 24,000 additional classrooms by September 2026, lifting the region's classroom stock to a projected 137,505 — at least ten percent more than existed on 5 February 2023, in every one of the eleven provinces. That last clause is the design choice this piece exists to point at. The programme's finish line was set above its starting line: not restoration but surplus, poured as insurance against the next shock. A system that rebuilds to a hundred percent has recovered; a system that rebuilds to a hundred and ten has admitted, in concrete, that the hazard has not gone away.

One discipline note belongs beside every figure above: this is a ministry counting its own work. The delivery numbers are ministerial statements — anniversary press events, provincial directorates reporting upward — and no independent audit of the classroom counts has been published. That does not make the numbers wrong; it makes them a particular kind of number, and they are labelled accordingly wherever they appear here. A second labelling matter sits inside the totals themselves: donor-built schools — the thirteen Ahbap Association sites in Tüysüz's Hatay count among them — are financed by charities and philanthropic campaigns but appear within the ministry's completion figures. The state counts what it did not always pay for. Neither caveat is a scandal; both are simply what reading a self-reported ledger honestly requires.

Concrete was the slow mechanism. The fast one — and the more transferable — was assessment: in 2023 the exam calendar held, and the system moved everything else instead. The high-school entrance exam, the LGS, was limited that year to the first semester's curriculum — the semester that had actually been taught when the ground moved; the university entrance exam, the YKS, likewise excluded the final grade's second-semester topics. For students in the affected provinces, the year-passing rule was rewritten so that the higher of their two semester grades stood for the whole year — no one's certification would hinge on the semester spent in a tent or a relative's spare room. Then the exam authority, ÖSYM, published its logistics package on 31 May 2023: candidates assigned an exam centre outside the province or district where they lived were moved on state-coordinated transport and housed in state dormitories, KYK's first, with the national disaster agency, AFAD, coordinating. The fee relief came separately, two weeks earlier, and in a form worth naming: announcing the KPSS arrangements on 16 May, ÖSYM's president said no exam or application fee had been taken from earthquake-zone candidates for their YKS applications either, and listed the eleven provinces and the district of Gürün it covered. The higher-education council added a 25% placement quota at earthquake-zone state universities. Every line follows the same principle: move the candidate, not the calendar; shrink the tested syllabus to the taught one; hold the machinery still while everything around it is moving.

Hatay is the stress test, because Hatay is where the destruction concentrated. When Tüysüz spoke in October 2024, his province counted 110 schools completed against 113 under construction — the ledger's two columns almost exactly level. By the 2025–26 school year, provincial reporting — carried in the regional press from governorship figures, a secondary source worth naming as such — had the columns moved: 199 new schools in service, 798 repair jobs done, 70 schools still in construction. Between the two snapshots, the under-construction column drained into the in-service column at very nearly the rate the director's ceremony line had forecast. This is what a rebuild ledger is for: it converts an unanswerable question — has Hatay recovered? — into a checkable one: which column did this school move to, and when. The ministry, for its part, said in February 2026 that classroom stock already exceeds pre-quake levels in all eleven provinces, by an average of sixteen percent in its own telling — a claim the programme's September 2026 completion date makes checkable, and that date has now passed. The date has now been read, and what the ledger published next was provincial rather than national. On 14 September 2026, opening the school year, Hatay's governor Mustafa Masatlı put his province at 254 schools completed and returned to education against 47 still under construction, with more than 360,000 students and about 22,000 teachers across 1,566 institutions — the governor's own nouns, and the same two columns the 2025–26 snapshot above reported as 199 and 70. The national line is the one we could not read. No ministry statement setting the eleven provinces' classroom stock against the 24,000-classroom target or the 137,505 projection turned up on the routes tried here — the ministry's own news pages, its anniversary releases and the national agencies' education wires — and that is a record of our search rather than a finding about the ministry's publishing. The province published; the programme's own headline number we did not find, as of that reading on 27 October 2026. Read again on 31 October 2026, that sentence needs a correction, and the hedge inside it is the reason it survives one. The ministry's national ledger for the eleven provinces had been published since 6 February 2026 — 161 pages in thirteen chapters — and this piece had never cited it. What was missing on 27 October was our search, not the ministry's publishing, which is precisely what that sentence reserved. The report sets the loss at 9,799 classrooms against a pre-quake stock of 119,200, and the completed count at 14,202. What it does not do is the one thing the September 2026 date was meant to make checkable: it is dated seven months before that deadline. Nor does anything published since — the ministry's own national opening release of 14 September 2026, read today, does not carry the word *deprem* at all, and names only Ankara. The ledger's lines exist. Its last line does not yet.

Then there is what the ledger does not carry, and the piece owes the reader both omissions. The count counts classrooms; it does not count children back into them. Rebuilt rooms and returned students are different quantities — families displaced from the earthquake provinces settled elsewhere, and whether they have come back is not published alongside the classroom totals. The ledger's most important companion number does not yet exist in public. Nor does a second: the wind-down of the temporary stock. Container classrooms carried a real share of the region's teaching after February 2023 — teachers' union statements described heavy container use in Hatay well into 2024 — yet no ministry-published count records how many temporary classrooms remain in service or have been stood down. We flag the absences rather than adjudicate them: a rebuild ledger is credible in what it publishes, and legible in what it leaves out.

What travels from Türkiye's three years is not the tonnage of concrete; it is two design choices and a habit. The choices: set the rebuild target above the loss, so that recovery ends with insurance rather than parity; and hold the assessment calendar by shrinking the tested curriculum to the taught one, so that a cohort's certification never becomes part of the damage. The habit: publish the running count, dated, line by line — and accept that a published ledger invites exactly the scrutiny this piece has applied, including to the lines it still lacks. September 2026 is written into the programme as the completion date, and it has now passed; from it the classroom stock, the container wind-down and the returned children are all countable, whether or not all three are counted in public. The finish line stands above the starting line. The open question is who will be standing at it.

— *The Editors*
