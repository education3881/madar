In 2024, a Year 11 student in New Zealand could do everything the national qualification asked — assemble the credits, pass the internal assessments, sit the externals — and still hold no qualification at all. The National Certificate of Educational Achievement is built on accumulation: credits earned in many subjects, by many routes, summed toward certificates at three levels. From that year, none of the sum counted until a student separately held twenty particular credits — ten in literacy, or te reo matatini for students assessed in te reo Māori, and ten in numeracy, or pāngarau — earned chiefly through standalone national digital assessments, the Common Assessment Activities, sat in two rounds a year, May and September–October. The system calls the twenty credits the co-requisite, and the name is exact: not a component of the qualification but a condition standing beside every level of it. Excellence elsewhere cannot substitute for it; no accumulation of credits can route around it. A country that had long let schools assemble its school-leaving qualification almost any way they liked had bolted one fixed gate onto the middle of the assembly.

The first mandatory year put numbers on what the flexibility had been absorbing. In the May 2024 assessment round, 45 per cent of the students assessed met the numeracy standard; reading stood at 58 per cent and writing at 55. Beneath the national rates sat the figure that made the gate a political object: in the schools the government's release called lower decile — the funding shorthand of the time for schools serving communities facing the steepest socio-economic barriers, since replaced by an Equity Index — numeracy attainment in that round was 19.8 per cent. The gate had not created that gap. The qualification's flexibility had long allowed passing totals to be assembled around it; the standalone assessments turned it into a single published number, measured against the same bar as everyone else, twice a year. Whether that exposure is cruelty or honesty is the whole argument about measurement, and New Zealand proceeded to run the argument with data rather than speeches.

The response followed the measurement. The government announced NZ$2.2 million of targeted support for students in 141 lower-decile schools — aimed, that is, where the first round had counted the need. The second mandatory year moved. In the May 2025 round, numeracy rose from 45 to 57 per cent, reading from 58 to 61, and writing held at 55. In the same lower-decile schools where numeracy had stood at 19.8 per cent, it reached 34 — still far under the national rate, but the steepest climb anywhere in the system — and reading rose from 34 to 41. Year 10 students sitting early, about 95 per cent of them for the first time, cleared at higher rates again: 72 per cent in reading, 68 in numeracy, 66 in writing. And on the measure the gate itself is judged by, attainment of the full co-requisite by the end of Year 11 rose from 69.5 per cent in 2024 to 73.8 in 2025.

"The increase in Year 11 Level 1 attainment amongst participating students follows a 2024 decline that was largely due to the strengthened co-requisite requirements coming into effect," Jann Marshall, the qualifications authority's Deputy Chief Executive Assessment, said when NZQA published the 2025 attainment picture in March 2026. "The increase in achievement suggests a stronger focus on teaching and learning across schools, as well as improved support for students to meet the requirement." Two details in the fine print reward attention. Marshall's "amongst participating students" is doing real work: Level 1 is optional, and roughly a third of Year 11 students skip it, so its pass rates describe only those who enter — while the co-requisite figures count the whole cohort by the end of Year 11. The two bases should never share a sentence. And the gate's arithmetic runs backward through time: the 2024 Level 1 pass rate stood at 71.5 per cent when first counted. By mid-2025 the ministry was counting the same cohort at 79.6, because students who had missed the co-requisite in 2024 cleared it in 2025 and collected, a year late, the qualification they had already earned everything else for — a recount, not a restatement: the authority's own year-end series still records 2024 at 71.5. A hard gate, the recount shows, mostly delays rather than denies. The question it leaves a system is who gets delayed, and for how long.

The equity ledger answers part of that, and NZQA carries it in its own words: "Significant disparity in attainment associated with socio-economic barriers to attainment (school equity index group) and ethnicity remains." Even after the steepest gains in the country, the May 2025 round in lower-decile schools ran roughly twenty points under the national figures — 34 against 57 in numeracy, 41 against 61 in reading. Read with the discipline they demand, those numbers measure the distribution of prepared teaching, stable schooling and assessment readiness across a country; they say nothing about what any community's children can learn. What the gate changed is that the distribution is now re-measured twice a year, in public, against a bar that moves for no one. The school operators appear to have accepted the terms: the Education Review Office's evaluation of the new assessments drew what the national broadcaster summarised as a pass mark from principals.

Then, having built the gate and watched it begin to work, New Zealand moved to demolish the building around it. In August 2025 the government opened public consultation on replacing NCEA outright; in March 2026 the replacement was confirmed; on 16 May 2026 came the details. From 2028, students will sit a Foundational Award in literacy and numeracy, benchmarked at Year 11. From 2029, a New Zealand Certificate of Education at Year 12; from 2030, an Advanced Certificate at Year 13; grades will run A+ to E, the credit mosaic giving way to something closer to a conventional certificate. All of that is announced, none of it yet operating, and the transition pathway that lets approved literacy- and numeracy-rich achievement standards stand in for the standalone assessments runs only to the end of 2027. The detail that matters most sits quietly inside the announcement: the Foundational Award must be gained before either new certificate can be. The co-requisite — the bolted-on gate, the least NCEA-like thing in NCEA — is the one component carried forward, renamed, and made the entry condition of the successor.

There is a reading in which the gate killed the building: the 2024 numbers were the most public evidence ever assembled that the qualification's flexibility could certify students the fixed bar would not pass, and within two years the qualification itself was gone. There is a gentler reading in which the gate was simply the only part everyone still trusted. Both readings end at the same finding: when the system chose what to keep, it kept the standalone, external, twice-a-year measurement, and let the flexibility go. The arc the gate has measured so far — 45 to 57, 19.8 to 34, 69.5 to 73.8 — is the best argument its defenders have; the twenty-point gap inside the arc is the best argument that a gate is a diagnosis, not a cure. A fixed bar teaches no one to read. What it does is make it impossible to say the reading happened when it did not — and a country that keeps choosing that visibility, even while replacing everything around it, has told you which kind of honesty it is prepared to live with. The next data point arrived as this edition was closing. In the May 2026 round — more than 82,900 students sitting, around half of them Year 10 students sitting early — writing rose from 55.4 to 56.8 per cent, while reading eased to 60.5 from 61.3 and numeracy fell back from 57.1 to 51.7, on the authority's own tables: the first backward steps in the gate's short public series. The gate reported the fall as flatly as it had reported the climb. That is what it was built to do — and, as of this year, it is the part of the old system the new one will stand on.

— *The Editors*
