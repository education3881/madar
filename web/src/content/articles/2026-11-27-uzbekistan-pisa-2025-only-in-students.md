Edition 06 has spent a week on one habit: before reading a list of improving school systems, find out what
clock it runs on. Four pieces have asked that of four systems. This one is about a system where the question
to ask is not which clock, but **which unit** — because the PISA 2025 release measures Uzbekistan's movement
in only one of the two it uses, and the one it uses is the one almost nobody quotes.

## The number

**Table I.2.8, "Change between 2022 and 2025 in low- and top performers in science"**, has two columns:
**"Change in percentage of low performers"** and **"Change in percentage of top performers"**. Uzbekistan's
row reads **−46.1** and **+0.5**, both in percentage points.

That first figure is the largest movement in the table. The next largest reduction is **Cambodia at −22.1**,
less than half of it. The **OECD average-35** row reads **1.2** and **−0.3** — the OECD's own average share
of low performers in science went *up* over the same three years.

The chapter gives the population those figures sit in: **"the share of low performers remained unchanged in
many systems (45), increased in 14 systems and decreased in 16 systems"**. Uzbekistan is one of the sixteen,
at the far end of them.

And it is named in prose, in a passage that is itself about statistical significance, among the systems that
moved at both ends of the distribution at once: **"By contrast, in Jordan, Uzbekistan, Montenegro, Saudi
Arabia, Türkiye, the United Arab Emirates and the United Kingdom, the share of low performers decreased
while the share of top performers increased"**. *(The register's sentence ends in a comma and runs on; the
quotation stops where the clause does.)*

## The two absences

Everything above is counted in students. The release's other unit is the score point, and it has two tables
for it. **Uzbekistan is in neither.**

**Table I.2.6**, which prints each system's change in mean performance between 2022 and 2025, says in its
own note that it shows **"Only countries and economies that can compare PISA 2022 and PISA 2025 results in
all three subjects are shown."** Uzbekistan has no row.

**Table I.2.9**, the fitted decennial trend, says it shows only systems with **"at least two previous
assessments among PISA 2015, PISA 2018 and PISA 2022"**. Uzbekistan has no row there either.

**Neither absence is a verdict about Uzbek schools. Both are statements about the instrument.** A table that
works by subtraction needs two comparable points, and a trend fitted across cycles needs at least three; a
system with a short record in PISA cannot supply either, however much its schools have changed. The same
chapter does this to two other systems on the same both-ends list of seven — Jordan and Uzbekistan are both
missing from the decade's table — and a reader who takes an absence from a list as a finding about a country
will get all three of them wrong.

## What the front page says, and what the report behind it prints

The report's own front page names Uzbekistan among systems that are bucking the trend: **"Among non-OECD
members, scores also went up in Cambodia, El Salvador, Georgia, Jordan, Mongolia, Montenegro, the
Philippines, Saudi Arabia, Thailand, the United Arab Emirates, Uruguay and Uzbekistan."**

**Scores. Went up.** That is a claim in score points, and the chapter behind that page prints no score-point
change for Uzbekistan in either of the two tables that would carry one. The claim may well be right — this
piece does not dispute it and has no basis to — but **a reader who wants the figure behind it will not find
one in this report**, and nothing on the page says so.

That is the whole finding, and it is worth being exact about what it is and is not. It is not an error. It is
a gap between the unit a summary speaks in and the unit the evidence underneath it is published in.

## Where the system stands

**Table I.2.1, "Comparing countries' and economies' performance in science"**, gives Uzbekistan's 2025
science mean as **438**, and the chapter's Table I.2.3 — whose first column is that same overall mean —
prints it beside the **OECD average of 482**.

Table I.2.1's other column is the company the measurement puts a system in. For Uzbekistan it is a crowded
one: **Chile, Israel, Iceland, Brunei Darussalam, Mauritius, Montenegro, Albania\*, Qatar, Greece and
Thailand** are all listed as not statistically different from it. *(The asterisk on Albania is the
register's own marker; the chapter does not define it and points to an annex this piece did not read, so the
mark is reproduced and left uninterpreted.)*

Those are levels, in one subject, in one year. They say nothing about movement, which is the point: the
level is the one thing the register states about Uzbekistan in both units without qualification.

## The same fall, split by gender

**Table I.2.13, "Change between 2022 and 2025 in low- and top performers in science, by gender"**, splits
the figure. Uzbekistan's low-performer columns read **−49.7 for girls** and **−42.5 for boys**; its
top-performer columns read **+0.6** and **+0.4**. All four are percentage points, over the same three years,
in science.

The chapter reports that passage in prose too. It first gives the population — **"the percentage of girls
who scored below proficiency Level 2 in science did not change on average across OECD countries, nor in most
countries/economies (45 out of 75 with available data), between 2022 and 2025"** — and then names the
largest movers: **"in Cambodia, Georgia and Uzbekistan, this share decreased by more than 20 percentage
points"**.

So the fall is present in both halves of the cohort and larger among girls. That is as far as the register
goes, and this piece goes no further: **it offers no cause for any of these figures, because the chapter
offers none.**

## The caution the chapter attaches to exactly this kind of figure

Chapter 2 carries a passage about who sits the test, and it is the one qualifier a reader of a fall of 46.1
**percentage** points has to have. PISA coverage — the share of a country's 15-year-olds eligible to be sampled — has risen in
many systems, and the chapter says **"this expansion in education opportunities makes it more difficult to
interpret how mean scores in PISA have changed over time."** It states the mechanism plainly:
**"Typically, as populations that had previously been excluded gain access to higher levels of schooling, a
larger proportion of low-performing students would be included in PISA samples"**.

**Uzbekistan is not named in that passage.** The systems the chapter names as having gained the most
coverage are given precisely — **"Most notably, over the past decade, Cambodia, Ecuador, El Salvador,
Lebanon, Morocco and Paraguay saw increases between 11 and 24 percentage points in the PISA coverage"** —
and Uzbekistan is not among them.

That is stated here as an absence from a list and nothing more. The chapter does not apply the coverage
mechanism to Uzbekistan, and this piece does not apply it either, in either direction. **The question of who
was in the Uzbek sample in 2022 and who was in it in 2025 is not settled by this chapter**, and a reader who
wants it settled needs the figures the chapter points to — Annex tables and Coverage Index 3 — which were
not read for this piece.

## What is still owed

**No Uzbek first-party register on PISA 2025 has been read for this piece**, and the doors were tested today
rather than assumed. `uzedu.uz` redirects to the state portal at `gov.uz/oz/edu`, which answers **200 with
171,467 bytes** carrying no occurrence of `pisa`. `stat.uz` answers **403**. `pisa.uz` does not resolve at
all.

**The portal's link set was then read rather than only its front door**, which is the method that opened
this edition's Qatar piece: four of its own paths were followed — its news index, its official-response
index, its statistics page and its reports page — answering **200** with between 146,912 and 419,740 bytes,
and **not one of them carries the string `pisa`**. Its `sitemap.xml` answers 200 with 475 bytes and carries
none either. *The method was applied in full and returned nothing, which is a different and more useful
result than not having applied it.*

So everything above is the OECD's, read in the OECD's own report. **This piece also names no human**, and
that is a gap rather than a style: no Uzbek register was reachable to name one, and the OECD's launch speech
— already the named voice in three pieces of this edition — dates nothing in this piece and would be here
only for decoration. When an Uzbek authority publishes its own reading of PISA 2025, that will be a
different piece.
