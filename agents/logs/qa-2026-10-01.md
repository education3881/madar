## 1. The 09-30 forward question, tested first: **which claims in `sources[]` are machine-checkable against the document itself?**

### The answer is not the one the question expected, and the useful half is cheap

**A pair makes every claim about a register twice.** Its URL, publication date, byte count and serving
state are properties of the document, not of the language the annotation is written in — so **the pair
is its own control, offline, with no fetch and no third-party host in the loop.** That is strictly
cheaper than the three candidates the question named (a byte count, a declared fetch date, a
carries-no-date claim), all three of which need the network and therefore cannot gate.

### The measured negative result, recorded so it is not re-attempted

The obvious implementation — a **numeral multiset** over each shared annotation, which is the
body-level discipline applied one level out — was built and measured across the whole corpus:

| | |
|---|---|
| shared annotations compared (44 pairs) | **338** |
| disagreeing | **117** |
| of those, actual defects | **essentially none** |

Three structural causes, every one a deliberate house convention:

* **Quote-plus-gloss doubling (#72).** An Arabic annotation carries the register's own English sentence
  *and* an Arabic gloss of it, so every figure inside a quotation appears **twice** on the AR side and
  once on the EN side.
* **Numeral-word rendering.** EN `876,000` against AR `876 ألف`; EN `20,000` against AR `20 ألف`.
* **ISO against prose dates.** EN `2026-05-22` tokenises to three numbers; the AR annotation writes the
  same date in Arabic month names and tokenises to one, or none.

A check with a 35% false-positive rate on a clean corpus is not a check; it is a thing somebody turns
off within a week — the 2026-09-13 hand-run rule arrived at from the other side. The normaliser that
would fix it (numeral words in two languages, decimal and thousands separators, quotation spans) is a
larger instrument than the question implies, and it is named here as **work**, not smuggled in
half-built.

### The sweep's own advice was wrong, and that is ruling #80

`qa_sources_alive` reported the four `parliament.gov.zm` URLs as `tls — invalid cert, document still
served 200`, with the standing advice *"a reader meets a browser interstitial either way, so say so"*.
**Measured today, every step:** the failure is `unable to get local issuer certificate` (verify code
20/21), **not** `certificate has expired`; the host sends **exactly one certificate**; that certificate
is **valid 26 May 2026 → 10 December 2026**; its own AIA extension prints
`http://cacerts.rapidssl.com/RapidSSLTLSRSACAG1.crt`; and with that intermediate supplied,
`openssl verify` returns **OK** and Python gets **HTTP 200 with validation enabled**.

So the certificate is fine, the document is fine, and the reader is fine. **Taking the tool's advice
would have put a false sentence about the reader into twelve annotations across the Zambia pair.**

**Fixed:** `tls` split into `tls` and `tls-chain`, and the two carry opposite advice — `tls` keeps the
interstitial sentence (true for expired, self-signed and mis-hostnamed certificates), `tls-chain` says
*measure before writing anything* and states that **the reader is not Python**. **Proved both ways
against five live hosts** rather than an injection: `expired`, `self-signed` and `wrong.host` on
badssl.com must stay in `tls` and do; `incomplete-chain` on badssl.com and the live
`parliament.gov.zm` must move to `tls-chain` and do.
