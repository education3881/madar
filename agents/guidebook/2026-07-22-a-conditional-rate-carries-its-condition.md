# Ruling #25 — a conditional rate carries its condition
**Date:** 2026-07-22 · **Origin:** Ed04 Netherlands doorstroomtoets draft — the data offers two rates that look interchangeable and invert the story if swapped · **Family:** figure-discipline (#12/#12b administration & coverage · #14 funnel stage · #21 vintage · #22 scope · #23 direction · #24 position)

## The case
The Dutch transition-test data carries two rates about the same event — an upward revision of a child's track advice (*bijstelling*) — and they answer different questions:

- **Eligibility rate** — the share of pupils who *qualify* for a revision (the test advice comes out higher than the teacher's preliminary advice). The PO-Raad reports this ranges from **under 1 in 10 to more than half** per municipality — highest where no local havo/vwo upper school exists.
- **Event-given-eligibility rate** — of the pupils who qualify, the share whose advice is *actually* revised. The Inspectorate reports **8 of 10 in IJsselstein, 4 of 10 in Stadskanaal**.

Both are "revision rates." Read the 8/10 as an eligibility rate and IJsselstein looks like a town of high-fliers; read the <1-in-10 as an event rate and the reform looks dead. The recon flagged this exact trap (§6: *heroverweging ≠ bijstelling*). The draft keeps them apart by naming the condition in the sentence: the 8/10-vs-4/10 is written as revision **when the test shows a higher advice**; the <1-in-10-to->half is written as the share who **qualify**.

## The rule
When a figure is a rate, before writing it, name its denominator's condition:
- **Is this "of all pupils" or "of pupils who already met condition X"?** An unconditional (base) rate and a conditional rate are different numbers about the same event; they are not comparable and must never be substituted for each other.
- **Carry the condition in the sentence** (per #19). "8 in 10" is meaningless without "of those who qualified"; "half" is meaningless without "qualify for."
- **Never chain two rates from different conditions into one trend.** A rising eligibility rate plus a flat event-given-eligibility rate is not "revisions rose then stalled" — it is two facts about two populations.
- **Watch for the inversion tell:** if a rate makes the disadvantaged region look advantaged (or vice-versa), you have probably swapped the base rate for the conditional one. Stadskanaal's *low* event rate and *high* eligibility rate are the same equity story from two ends; mixing them tells the opposite of both.

## Why it compounds
This is the base-rate/conditional-rate distinction, and it is adjacent to funnel stage (#14) but not the same move: #14 says *which stage of a pipeline* (registered → sat → passed); #25 says *which population a rate is conditioned on* (all → eligible). Every assessment system that gates one action on a prior result — remediation given failure, revision given a higher score, an appeal given a first mark — produces this pair, and reporting bodies routinely publish both without labelling which is which. The verification move is mechanical: *for every rate, write the words "out of ___" and check the draft's ___ matches the source's.*
