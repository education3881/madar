# Ruling #72 — a translation policy applied unevenly across a piece's voices is an editorial claim about whose words count

**Filed:** 2026-09-28, Arabic Editor's gate on Edition 05 row 6 (Rwanda), the edition's sixth pair.
**Family:** composition discipline / the annotation and the sentence.
**Origin:** the first pair in this publication whose registers are **not available in Arabic at all**.

## The condition that produced it

Five of Edition 05's six pairs had at least one Arabic register. Egypt's Arabic was composed *from
Arabic documents* — the statute, Youm7's reproduction — so its quoted clauses are the register's own
words and no translation decision arose. Rwanda has none: two ministry yearbooks that are English PDFs,
four English pages on `mineduc.gov.rw`, a World Bank feature, a Kigali newspaper. Every quotation in the
Arabic can therefore only be **our translation of an English sentence**, which is a different object
from a quotation.

The corpus precedent (Sierra Leone 08-29, Sudan 09-03) renders such a quotation in Arabic alone. This
pair departed from it, because the piece's argument is made of specific words: *declined only
marginally* is the ministry's own adverb and the pivot of an entire movement; *may not have received
training* is a modal the piece spends a paragraph reading; *aimed to* against the headline's *in less
than one year* is a contrast between two clauses of one document. **An Arabic paraphrase of a hedge is a
hedge we chose** — which is the 09-13 rule (the annotation and the sentence are written from the same
open register) arriving in a second language.

## The ruling

So far that is only a fidelity argument, and it would have licensed a **selective** policy: carry the
English where the wording is the evidence, render in Arabic where it is not. The first composition
considered exactly that, and it would have rendered Pelagie Abayikunda's two testimonial quotes in
Arabic alone — she is the only human voice in the piece and neither quote carries a hedge or a scope.

**Rejected, and not on grounds of consistency.** The piece's own closing movement observes that every
voice in the record reaches the reader through the funder's channel or the ministry's: the one teacher
who speaks does so inside her funder's feature, and the piece says so rather than balancing it away. A
policy that carried the ministry's words in their own language and translated the teacher's would have
**printed that hierarchy into the typography**, in the section that exists to name it. The reader would
have met a ministry quoted and a teacher reported.

> **A translation policy applied unevenly across a piece's voices stops being a policy and becomes an
> editorial claim about whose words count.** Choose the treatment once, per piece, and apply it to every
> voice — or state the distinction in the piece, as a distinction, where a reader can weigh it.

## What it costs, stated because it is real

The Arabic body carries more Latin than any pair this publication has shipped, and it is heavier
reading than Egypt's. Nine glosses open with the same «أي إنَّ…», which becomes a tic; varying the gloss
verb was considered and refused, because a varied gloss verb implies distinctions between quotations
that do not exist (#43's verb hierarchy pointed at our own apparatus). The alternative was lighter
prose that quietly re-authored five hedges, and between a tic and a re-authored hedge the choice is not
close.

## Generalises past translation

The rule is about **any per-voice editorial treatment**, not about language. Italicising one speaker's
words and not another's, giving one source a full annotation and another a bare URL, naming one person
by role and another by name — each is defensible as a local judgment and each, applied unevenly across
voices in one piece, becomes a claim the piece did not argue for. The test is cheap: **list the piece's
voices and ask what treatment each got, then ask whether the piece would defend the difference in
print.**
