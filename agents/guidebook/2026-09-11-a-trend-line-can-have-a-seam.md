# Ruling #48 — a trend line can have a seam

**Filed:** 2026-09-11 · **Guidebook Section 1, row 43** · Produced by the TIMSS 2019 South Africa national-report read (`content-drafts/recon/2026-09-11-ed05-timss-2019-national-report-read.md`)
**Family:** scoped-instrument (#19 multiplier-without-base · row 20 deficit-is-a-ratio · #21 vintage) · which-register-speaks-for-the-owner (#38/#40/#42/#43)
**First ruling of Edition 05 produced by a *research read* rather than a verification verdict** — the run of three verdict-born rulings (#45, #46, #47) ends here.

---

## The rule

**A long series is not automatically a series.** When the owner has spliced together measurements of *different populations, grades, instruments or scales*, the join is a construction the owner made, it has a size, and that size is part of every figure that crosses it.

Operationally, three obligations, in order:

1. **Find the seam before quoting the span.** Before any "X improved by N over M years", locate the earliest and latest points *in the register* and ask what changed between them besides the thing being measured.
2. **A figure that crosses a seam carries the seam in the same sentence** — its size, named as the owner names it.
3. **Prefer the owner's certified window.** When the owner itself reports a shorter, unseamed span as its headline, that is the span; a longer one assembled by a reader is the reader's construction, not the owner's finding.

---

## What bit

The commission for Edition 05's slot 3 carried two South African figures from a secondary channel: **+104 points (2003–2019)** and **0.9 SD (1995–2015)**. The 09-04 re-verification had already quarantined both — the DBE 2023 Highlights' trend chapter starts at 2011 — and blocked the draft until the national report was read in the body. It was read today.

**The +104 survives, cleanly.** The report prints it twice, decomposes it (+67, +20, +17), and states that every cycle-to-cycle difference is statistically significant. It also prints, unprompted and in the same eight pages, the deceleration (7.4 points a year in the first half, 4.6 in the second) and the floor (the 5th–95th range narrowing from 320 to 252, with the top end flat since 2011). The figure came out of the primary *better* than it went in.

**The 0.9 SD does not survive, and the way it fails is the ruling.** The report never prints 0.9 anywhere. It prints **one** standard deviation, and it prints it for **2003–2019**. Reconstructing what a reader would have had to do to get 0.9 from 1995–2015 gives the answer: 276 → 372 = +96 ≈ 0.96 SD. Both endpoints are real numbers on the report's own Figure 7. The subtraction is where it dies:

> "Figure 7 presents the **Grade 8** average mathematics scale scores for the TIMSS 1995, 1999 and 2003 cycles, and the **Grade 9** average mathematics scale scores for the TIMSS 2003, 2011, 2015 and 2019 cycles."
>
> "In 2003, we assessed learners in both Grade 8 and Grade 9. On average, **Grade 9 learners scored 21 points more than Grade 8 learners.** This 2003 assessment of both Grade 8 and 9 learners **provides the bridge** for the 1995 to 2019 trend line."

276 is a **Grade 8** score. 372 is a **Grade 9** score. Roughly 21 of the 96 points are one extra year of school — a quantity the report measured on purpose, in one year, *in order to* make the two arms joinable. And the report's verdict on the arm the longer window buys is null: *"from 1995 to 2003 there was no significant change."*

So the longer window purchases eight flat years and one grade step, and pays for them by abandoning the only span the owner certifies. It looks like more evidence and is strictly less.

**Nothing here was a misquotation.** Both endpoints exist; the arithmetic is right; the owner is first-party and is the one who published both arms on one chart. The error is entirely in the *span* — which is why no instrument the operation owns would have caught it. A numeral-multiset check compares numbers that are present. A two-channel check (#27) confirms both endpoints, because both endpoints are real. The seam is not a number; it is a **boundary between two populations that the figure quietly steps across**, and the only thing that finds it is reading the axis of the chart the numbers came off.

---

## The second half: "one standard deviation" is a scale unit

The figure that *does* ride carries its own trap, and it belongs to this ruling because it is the same defect one level down — a unit doing work the sentence is not showing.

The report defines its metric (p. 14): the TIMSS scale has a centrepoint of 500 and **a standard deviation of 100, by construction**. So "one standard deviation" means *100 points of a fixed international scale*. It is **not** an effect size computed on South African learners — whose own 5th-to-95th spread in 2003 was 320 points, i.e. more than three times the unit being invoked.

A reader hearing "a one standard deviation improvement" hears an effect relative to the population described. That reading is wrong and the wrongness flatters. The rule: **carry the unit's owner.** *104 points, which is one standard deviation on the TIMSS scale* — never "a one-SD gain", never "a full standard deviation of improvement" unhosted.

This makes the scoped-instrument family's fourth shape: **#19** a multiplier with no base, **row 20** a deficit that is secretly a ratio, **#44's corollary** a converted figure with two owners, and now **a standard deviation that belongs to the ruler rather than to the measured.**

---

## Corollaries

1. **The axis is part of the figure.** A number lifted off a chart without reading what the chart's x-axis and series labels say has not been read; it has been extracted. Applies to our own charts as much as to theirs.
2. **A bridge is a finding, and it is usually the most interesting sentence on the page.** When an owner runs two instruments on one cohort in one year to make its series joinable, that year's dual measurement is a *result* — here, "a year of schooling was worth 21 TIMSS points in South Africa in 2003." An operation that only mines the series for its endpoints throws away the best number in the chapter.
3. **Seams to look for, in rough order of frequency:** a change of grade or age band; a change of instrument or its scale; a change of coverage (which schools, which languages, which provinces — cf. #44's *a trend chapter has its own coverage*, where Niger's national-language pupils were excluded from the very trend the piece was about to cite); a change of definition (cf. the Zambia Act moving the end of primary from Grade 7 to Grade 6 — the same defect arriving from the *policy* side, which is what that piece is about); and a change of owner.
4. **The longest available window is a rhetorical choice, not a neutral one**, and it is almost always the one a secondary channel has already made for you. Ask what the owner's own headline span is and why the source in hand chose a different one. Here the secondary span was longer, older, larger-sounding, and weaker.
5. **A figure can fail on its span alone.** Add it to the Verifier's trace as its own question, beside *which register was this read in* (#43): **does this figure cross a join, and does the sentence name it?** A figure whose endpoints both verify can still be false.

## Applied to our own copy, today

- The slot-3 commission's blocker-2 language is now resolved rather than deferred: +104 rides with its window, its grade, its decomposition and its significance; the 0.9 SD is struck and **is not to be repaired into 0.96** — the repair would be the same crime with better arithmetic.
- The piece's improvement crown now carries its floor from the same section, satisfying the commission's standing prohibition (*improvement is never reported without its floor*) out of the primary rather than by assembly.
- The registers disagree in one place and it is recorded rather than smoothed (#43): the launch release compresses *at-or-above intermediate* (13%) into *"reached the international intermediate benchmark"*, where the report's own figure shows 10% in the band and 3% above it. The piece uses neither; it uses the 11→41 percent claim, which both registers state identically.
