# Ruling #62 — a date that matches is not a date that agrees

**Filed:** 2026-09-23, by the daily run, from the Verifier's verdict on Edition 05 row 5
(`content-drafts/verdicts/2026-09-23-egypt-baccalaureate-published-first-verification.md`).
**Family:** *whether the words around the number are true* — the **non-numeric** family
(#45 noun, #46 quotation and clock, #47 description-date, #49 superlative, #51 scope, #61
compression). Seventh member, and the first that the family's own catcher cannot catch.
**Origin case:** three sentences in one piece, dated correctly and about the wrong thing.

---

## The ruling, in one line

**A register's date belongs to the register.** It becomes the date of the event the register
reports **only when the register says so** — *today*, *yesterday*, an explicit dateline for the
act. Absent that, the piece dates the **account** and never the **act**; and because the two
are written with the same digits, **string equality between an annotation and a sentence is not
agreement between them.**

## The case

Edition 05 row 5 makes this move six times. Three are right and three were wrong, and every one
of the six passed the Editor's five-test pair verdict, the Arabic gate, the numeral multiset, the
eight figure axes and the `annotation == sentence` hand-off line.

| the piece said | the register served | verdict |
|---|---|---|
| *"On 25 July 2025 … jointly published"* | the mirror is dated 2025-07-25 and says the ministries announced **"today"** | **licensed** |
| *"On 17 August 2026 the ministry announced"* | «أعلنت وزارة التربية والتعليم … **اليوم الاثنين 17 أغسطس 2026**» | **licensed** |
| *"Daily News Egypt **reported on** 16 August 2026 that"* | the paper, 16 Aug 2026 | **licensed — and it is the model** |
| *"On 21 July 2025 … EIPR published its objection"* | the statement's dateline is **7 July 2025**; 21 July is the **Wayback capture timestamp** | **wrong** |
| *"Speaking in London on 18 May 2026"* | the paper names the forum, the session and its title, and gives **no date for the speech** | **wrong** |
| *"on 3 August 2026, the admissions arithmetic was set out"* | the portal says *"has announced"*, carries its own article date, and names its source as another newspaper | **wrong** |

The first three are not luck and the second three are not carelessness. They are the **same
operation** performed six times with **no rule attached to it** — so it came out right wherever
the register happened to license it and wrong wherever it did not, which is exactly the
distribution you get when a move is habitual rather than governed.

## Why nothing we own could see it

The 2026-09-13 rule put teeth in the hand-off: *the drafter states, per source, that the hedge,
the noun, the date and the setting in the body match what `sources[]` says about the same
register.* That rule was written for the shape where **the annotation is right and the body is
wrong** — and this **is** that shape. It still could not catch it, and the reason is worth
stating precisely:

> The annotation says *21 July 2025*. The sentence says *21 July 2025*. **The check passes on
> identity.** The annotation means the day a crawler saw the page. The sentence means the day an
> organisation published a statement. The referent moved and the string did not.

Same for the other two: *18 May 2026* is in both, meaning the paper's issue in one and a man
standing up in a hall in the other; *3 August 2026* is in both, meaning a portal's posting in one
and a ministry's announcing in the other.

**This is the first defect in the non-numeric family that survives the family's own instrument.**
#45, #46 and #47 are all caught by putting the annotation beside the sentence. #62 is what is
left when you do that and the two agree.

## The binding practice

1. **A date in a body sentence is attached to a verb, and that verb has an owner.** Before the
   date ships, say out loud which of the two it belongs to: *the document*, or *the thing the
   document describes*. If the answer is the second, the register must license it.
2. **A register licenses a date for an act only by saying so** — *today*, *yesterday*, *on 3
   August the Minister said*, an explicit dateline over the act. A publication date over a
   report of an act licenses **nothing about the act**.
3. **An archive capture timestamp is never a publication date.** It is later than publication,
   by an unknown amount, and it is the one date in an archived citation that is guaranteed **not**
   to be the document's. Read the document's own dateline; it is almost always on the page.
4. **When the register does not license it, date the account.** *"In an account published on 18
   May 2026, X said …"* / *"Daily News Egypt reported on 16 August 2026 that …"* This adds no
   claim, removes one, and costs a preposition.
5. **The `annotation == sentence` line gains a referent column.** Stating that the date matches is
   no longer enough; the drafter states **what each of the two dates is the date of**. A line
   that can be satisfied by copying is a line that will be satisfied by copying.
6. **The Verifier's clock row (#46) is run as a sweep, not a read.** Enumerate every date in the
   body, pair each with the register it came from, and ask the licensing question once per pair.
   Item 3 of the origin case was found this way and by nothing else — it had already survived two
   thirds of a verification pass that was *looking for exactly this defect*.

## The corollary, and it is the uncomfortable half

**A recorded distinction is not an enforced one.** The origin piece's own prose says, two
sentences after the third defect, *"This reaches us through a government portal that names its own
source as a newspaper, so it is reported here as that report and not as the ministry speaking."*
The piece knew it was at two removes, said so, and dated the sentence as though it were at none.

That is #61's corollary in a different costume — *a recorded disagreement on one axis is not a
check on the others* — and the two were filed one day apart off the same piece. The generalisation
both point at: **a guardrail written into the prose protects the sentence it is written in, and
no other.**

## What this does not cover, named so it is not assumed

A date spelled in words; a season or a quarter standing in for a day (that is #51, and the held
set carries an open instance of it); and the case where the register licenses a date and the
register is wrong. The first is invisible to any comparison of digits, the second is already
numbered, and the third is not ours to fix — but it is ours to attribute.

**Sweep run on filing:** the whole corpus was searched for the archive-capture limb (only one
piece has ever cited a capture, and it is the origin case, now fixed) and the held set for the
publication-date limb (two candidates, both **licensed** — Sudan's BRIDGES release is datelined
*KHARTOUM, 4 May 2026* and says the programme *"launched today"*). **The class is contained to row
5 and is closed there.**
