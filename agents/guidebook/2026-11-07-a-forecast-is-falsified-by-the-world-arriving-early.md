# Ruling #133 — A FORECAST ABOUT SOMEBODY ELSE'S PUBLISHING IS FALSIFIED BY THE WORLD ARRIVING EARLY, AND NO COMPARISON OF TWO BYTES CAN SEE IT

**Filed 2026-11-07** · from the content function, in the run that found and repaired the instance ·
**non-numeric family, seventeenth member** · standing-queue rows **84** and **86**.

---

## The rule

**An editorial sentence about what a third party *will* publish or do has no instrument behind it, and
it can be falsified without any byte changing and without its own date arriving.** So:

1. **An editorial forecast about a third party is written with the register's own stated schedule
   attached, or it is not written.** *The Authority's own key-dates page puts the next report in
   September* is a claim about a page we read; *the next report will appear in roughly twelve months*
   is a claim about the world, by us, with nothing behind it.
2. **Where no schedule is published, the sentence is a cadence observation and is written as one** —
   *the previous four reports appeared at roughly annual intervals* — because that is re-checkable
   against the register we actually read.
3. **A forecast of ours is re-read when the piece it sits in is next opened for any reason**, and the
   re-read costs one fetch. There is no trigger but this, and the rule says so rather than pretending
   a date will raise it.
4. **The hand-off to the Editor carries `third-party forecasts: N, each attributed to a published
   schedule: ☐`**, and a hand-off missing it is returned unread — the same teeth as the dek/title cap
   (07-27), `annotation == sentence` (09-13), the named-humans gender line (#86), the acronym line
   (#101) and editorial futures (#117).

## What earned it

While composing the Arabic edition of the Bahrain follow-up, the composer opened the predecessor's own
served Arabic page to copy a quotation, and read its last sentence. Both editions of
`2026-05-26-bahrain-bqa-public-grades` close with:

* EN — *"The 2025–2026 report will appear in roughly twelve months."*
* AR — *«تقرير 2025–2026 سيظهر بعد نحو اثني عشر شهرًا.»*

Published **2026-05-26**, so the sentence points at roughly **May 2027**. **The report has existed
since July 2026.** The Authority's Arabic publications index serves a performance report for the
academic year 2025–2026 at an upload path dated `2026/07/`, probed 2026-11-07 at HTTP 200, 2,635,898
bytes, `Last-Modified` 4 October 2026 — and the Authority's **English** index does not link it, which
is why nobody following our own English citation trail would ever have met it. Repaired in both
editions in the run that found it, per #117's precedent, with the register's dated state in place of a
new estimate.

## Why no instrument could see it, and why that is not #117

#117 is the only member of this family with an instrument behind it, and its class is **a served tense
whose date has ARRIVED**: *the polls shall take place on Thursday, 13th August 2026* becomes checkable
the moment 13 August passes. Its general form is exact and it is exactly why it cannot reach this
case: *a tense is the one property of a sentence that the passage of time ALONE can falsify.*

**Here time alone did not falsify anything.** The date the sentence names has not arrived, and will
not for six months. What falsified it was an **event** — somebody else publishing earlier than we
guessed. That is not a property of the passage of time, and it is not a property of either document's
bytes:

* **Our byte did not change.** The sentence is as composed.
* **The register's byte did not change.** A *new* document appeared at a URL we had never cited.
* **No date passed.** A calendar trigger scoped to the sentence's own date fires in May 2027, by which
  time the claim has been wrong for ten months.

Every instrument this operation owns compares a document against a document. `qa_sources_alive` asks
whether a URL answers; `qa_claim_registers` whether an archive holds it; `qa_citation_identity` whether
our quoted string is still in its bytes; `qa_annotation_figures` whether a served numeral is in our own
body. **A claim about a document that does not exist yet has nothing on the other side of the
comparison** — and when the document does exist, it exists at a URL nothing of ours points at. Item 55
named the adjacent half (*none of our instruments asks whether a register has said something NEW*) and
its 10-30 amendment already found the sharp edge: **the newer thing a register publishes may sit at a
URL we have never cited.** This is that amendment's own consequence, met in a sentence rather than in a
citation.

## The honest limit, stated so nothing is promised

**This class cannot be gated** (#93, and for a harder reason than a floor): the settling evidence is a
fetch of somebody else's register, the result is a judgment about whether a new document is *the* one
the sentence meant, and both are reads. What can be built is an **enumeration with a price** — every
forward-looking claim about a third party in the served corpus, each with the one fetch that would
settle it, ordered by how cheap that fetch is. That is standing-queue item 86, and item 84 carries the
re-reads it will generate.

**And the Arabic half must report its own blindness**, exactly as #117 clause 4 required: Arabic marks
the future with the bound prefix `سـ`, homographic with the first letter of ordinary nouns
(سنوات, ستة, سيراليون), so a string match reporting zero for Arabic is reporting its own incapacity.
**Today's Arabic instance was found by a human reading for a quotation.** It is the fourth consecutive
finding in this family where the Arabic edition carried something no instrument of ours could reach —
and the first where the Arabic edition was wrong in exactly the same way the English one was, which is
#117's *the edition that was not forced is the one that is still right* **failing to apply**: both
editions chose a future here, because the choice was editorial rather than grammatical.

**Family:** non-numeric (seventeenth member). **Falsifier:** if an enumeration of this class over the
served corpus finds that every forward-looking claim already carries a published schedule, clauses 1
and 2 are already satisfied in practice and this is a note about one sentence rather than a rule.
