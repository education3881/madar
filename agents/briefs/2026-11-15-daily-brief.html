The one thing worth your attention today

Fifty-six foreign names on our Arabic pages have been printed wrong for three weeks, and the reason is that a rule about a script was written as a rule about a page. Three weeks ago we fixed something real: Arabic was being slanted, which Arabic typography does not do. The fix said nothing on an Arabic page is slanted. But an Arabic page is not all Arabic. Nature, Elsevier, Poynter, Kurikulum Merdeka — journal titles, institutions and foreign phrases, where the slant is not decoration but the thing that tells a reader it is a title. All of them have been upright since 25 October.

Nothing we own could have caught it, and nothing we own was wrong. Our check asks whether Arabic text is slanted. These are not Arabic. The check and the rule were built on the same idea of what a thing is, so they are blind in the same place. The repair is not a stylesheet line — it is ten published pieces going back through the Arabic Editor, and it is queued rather than rushed.

A separate, cheerful one: one character now costs thirty-three kilobytes less on almost every page. The macron in Madār sits one letter past the end of the font file the page already downloads, so the browser was fetching a second 33 KB file to draw one accent. Writing the accent a different way — same letter, same pixels, measured at zero difference across 108,000 of them — removes the second file.

Nothing needs you. Issue #13 is open its twenty-second day with its default running — pieces ship as singles, the option your own recommendation ranked third, and the operation keeps saying so. Everything below is on origin.

Today
Sunday 15 November · brief 131

Quality — item 39 closed at 21 days, and re-measuring it before taking it changed what the day was about. Yesterday's ruling made that re-measurement compulsory: a run taking a job off the board re-reads the example the job quotes, in today's files, before planning. Yesterday that rule found an example that had already been fixed. Today it found one that had doubled.

Item 39 named three measurements. Two reproduce to the byte after three weeks — a 147 KB italic font fetched to paint 196 characters on an English article, and 71 KB fetched to paint the five letters of Madār in an Arabic footer. The third said the Arabic edition had lost its emphasis mark on twelve article pages. The real number is twenty-three article pages and the Arabic home — 195 emphasis marks, 139 of them Arabic. The twelve was a true number about a different thing, carried across without being re-taken.

And the other fifty-six are Latin, which is the finding. They are the journal and institution names in the push block above. The question item 39 asked was what should replace italic in Arabic — it assumed something had been lost that should come back. The answer to that question is nothing: Arabic has no italic convention, emphasis is carried by the sentence and by the quotation marks, and that is now written into the standing ledger as a term every future Arabic composition is written under, so a fourth run does not decide it again. But the question about what to add found something that should never have been taken away.

It is not fixed today, and the reason is a finding rather than a shortage of time. Every stylesheet-only repair would make our own check fail on 139 Arabic runs — and the check would be right to fail, because a stylesheet can only talk about whole elements and one element can hold both scripts. So the repair belongs in the text of ten published pieces, through the Arabic Editor's gate. It is the board's only defect and tomorrow's first job.

Item 39's other half, the typeface question, was answered by refusing the question. It asked which of two type families to drop. Neither: one of them sets 982 characters on the home page and the other 1,507 on the editions page. What the numbers actually pick out is not a family but a family on a particular page, and the measure is one the build has been printing all along — bytes per character painted, which runs from 13 to 33,736 across today's pages.

Content — the Editor judged the England draft and returned it on test three, with every citation re-checked against the live registers first. All eight sources were fetched today and 35 of 35 quoted sentences were found word for word in what those pages are currently serving. Every figure quoted without quotation marks was checked in the source's own table. The claim the drafter struck last week — that the Department said the grade change was not retrospective — is confirmed struck in the strongest way available: the word "retrospective" does not appear anywhere in either register that could have carried it.

And the piece's central trap is tighter than the draft knew. Two different "17%" figures from one regulator mean near-opposite things — one is the share of schools that kept their top grade, the other the share rated requires improvement. The draft placed them fourteen weeks apart in two documents. They are in one table, one row, four columns apart. The draft's decision never to print a bare "17%" is therefore necessity, not caution.

Test three is the one it fails: the reader does not know by paragraph two why this is in front of them in November 2026. The answer is in the piece and it is a long way down — the inspection grade has been abolished and replaced, so the 2022 measurement is the only one that will ever exist, and the first report under the replacement was published five days ago. The fix is a move, not a rewrite; nothing is re-reported. Tests four and five were not judged, because the rule says a verdict stops at a failed test — said plainly, because a list of five ticks with two of them unexamined reads exactly like a full read.

Two smaller things the Editor found: one source has no web address at all — it is the only one cited for something about the page rather than for a sentence in the body, so the drafter's habit of writing the address next to the claim had no claim to attach it to; and the dek is not in the draft, only its character count, which is a measurement with nothing under it. The Editor supplied the address and required the dek. The three calls the drafter left open are all decided, so the revision is one pass.

Growth — one macron, and the date register's second real reading. The saving is described above; measured before and after on the build's own probe, four of six sampled pages drop 33,736 bytes each and the two that keep the file keep it for a good reason — the editions pages print the wordmark sixteen more times, and one of them carries a sentence about the macron. The corpus-wide total is deliberately not claimed: six pages is a sample, and whether the file is needed depends on which typeface paints a character, which is only knowable while the page is being drawn.

The bet read a real change for the first time. The register that dates each page by its own served bytes read zero yesterday on a genuinely quiet day, and today, with the site chrome changed, it read 137 of 138 pages moved. The order matters: a measure that reads zero and then cannot read one is simply broken. Its declared margin of error collapsed from 134 estimated entries to one in an afternoon. That retires a job on the board — by event, not by design: nothing was fixed, the world moved under the measurement, and the code that produced the estimates is unchanged, so the question is re-queued in its narrower and harder form.

Research — ruling 143, and it is the general statement of the day. A rule is exact only where the thing it selects is the thing the fact lives in. Where the selector is coarser, the error is the number of mixed cases — and the rule's own checker cannot see them, because it was built on the rule's idea of a thing. Three instances today across two functions: the script rule written as a page rule; "drop a family" asked of a cost that belongs to a family on a page; and a citation discipline organised around claims that had no slot for a source cited for something else. Three in one day is a rate, not an anecdote.
