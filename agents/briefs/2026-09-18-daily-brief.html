Manager → Founder

The date was true. The instrument was wrong. Nobody read it for a day.

Every page in our sitemap carries a last modified date, and a crawler decides how often to come back by reading them. We generate those dates from git, and we have a standing check that refuses to let a date be newer than anything that could honestly have changed. It failed the deploy yesterday. It was right to be suspicious and wrong on the facts: the page it flagged had genuinely changed that morning.

The reason is worth the minute. Our date-generator has three rules — one for articles, one for the index pages, and one for VALENCE, the standalone instrument, which is dated from its own single file. The check that verifies it had modelled two. It has been enforcing the right bound on most of the site and no bound at all on that one page since the day it was written. The one URL it failed on is the one URL it had never actually been checking. The fix makes it stricter, not looser, and that is proved rather than claimed.

Second thing, and the one I am most pleased about: yesterday’s brief named a candidate test for whether Arabic letters actually join on the page. Today we tested the candidate before building anything on it. It does not work — and it fails for a beautiful reason: yesterday’s own fix makes it impossible to run. Two ideas written hours apart in the same document, quietly incompatible. We found the test that does work instead, and it is now gating every build.

Today

One repair, one honest “no”, and the edition’s last editorial obstruction cleared

Friday 18 September 2026 · brief 77
Quality · P0 · the red deploy
The check computed one ceiling — the newest commit touching a published article or a shared component — and applied it to all 120 URLs. VALENCE is built from a hand-written file under web/public/, which is in neither of those sets, so the ceiling had never had anything true to say about it. Yesterday that file legitimately changed; the date was real; the check refused it.

The ceiling is now derived per URL, from the generator’s own rule, and the set of standalone pages is discovered from disk rather than listed — so a second one inherits the bound without anyone remembering.
Deliberately not fixed by widening the ceiling to cover web/public, which was the obvious one-line repair. Held pieces’ artwork lives there, so that ceiling would have risen every time an unpublished draft was touched — re-opening the exact leak this check was written to close. Never widen a bound to admit an honest case.
Quality · and the proof that it is stricter now
Five runs, each injection verified to have changed the file before the check read it. Control silent on the real build. Bites on a future-dated article, on a stripped date, and on removing the page from the sitemap so the new machinery would have nothing to check.

The fifth is the one that matters: with a scratch commit raising the global ceiling, a fabricated VALENCE date planted between its own file’s commit and that ceiling prints PASS on the old check and DEFECT on the new one. That is a date the old version accepted in silence — which is why this is a strengthening.
Quality · the forward question, tested, and answered no
Yesterday’s proposal: force letter-spacing onto an Arabic word and compare its width against the natural one; if they match, nothing is shaping. Measured today on a healthy page, the two widths matched to the pixel, on every test string — because yesterday’s own fix pins Arabic letter-spacing with !important and tracking can no longer be forced here at all. The proposed check would have fired on every correct build we ever ship.
A standing rule says the next run tests a named surface before adding anything new. This is the first time testing one came back negative, and it is precisely what the rule is for. A forward question is a hypothesis, not a work order.
Quality · standing assertion 18
The test that does work: compare an Arabic word against itself with a zero-width non-joiner between every letter — the same font, the same page, the known-broken case by construction. A word that joined is far narrower than its own unjoined letters: 0.433 for a four-letter run that fully connects, against 1.000 when shaping is off.

Two halves, because there are two defects: the font failing to join, and our own text or CSS breaking joins the font would happily have drawn. Proved four ways. 16 of our 18 standing assertions now gate the deploy, and the two that do not say why in the file.
It bit twice on its own machinery before it bit on anything real — once selecting a block of machine-readable metadata as “the page’s longest Arabic text”, once filtering out the very text it exists to catch. Both are in the log. A check that cannot run, wearing the costume of a check that found something, is the failure mode worth naming.

Content · the source we had buried was alive
Yesterday a citation behind ruling #49 was recorded as archived-but-unreadable, with one of its three options being to retire the demonstration the ruling stands on. We had asked the Internet Archive’s convenience endpoint, which returned a single 5 KB snapshot and refused to serve it.

Today the same endpoint returned nothing at all for the same URL — for a fact about the past that cannot change. The archive’s actual index holds five captures, four of them around 30 KB. Three were read in full, on the first try. The interview says exactly what #49 says it says, and the read makes the ruling sharper: the bounding clause is in the very next sentence, and the whole answer was given in reply to a question explicitly about 2030. The headline did not just drop a qualification; it dropped the subject it was answering.
Cited now as an archive of the interview rather than as the newspaper, in both languages, with the publisher’s death stated. One correction to our own record while we were there: we had written three times that the interview was dated January. It is 7 February 2023 — January was the workshop.
Growth · what this site asks of a reader’s browser
Every link and asset in all 121 built pages, grouped by origin. The claim we print in every brief — no third-party tracker, by design — is true, and now verified from the bytes rather than from memory. No analytics, no tag manager, no pixel, no embed.

Two origins are contacted without the reader asking, and they are the same vendor: Google Fonts, on 120 of 121 pages. Not a tracking problem. A distribution problem: Amiri, the Arabic face, is the one asset in the Arabic edition we do not control, and the Arabic edition is written for the readers most likely to sit behind a network that cannot fetch it. Recommendation on file to self-host all five families — all openly licensed — deliberately not bundled into the push that repairs the deploy.
The ~180 other origins are sources[] citations — links a reader chooses to follow. That is the method working, and it must never be counted as a third-party request.
Research · ruling #56
A convenience answer is not the index. When an instrument can return a representative case or the whole set, asking the cheap question gets you a well-formed answer to a narrower one — and the wrongness is invisible, because a representative answer is perfect. Two origin cases in one day: an archive that answered “the nearest capture” when we meant “what do you hold”, and a ceiling that answered “the bound for most pages” when we meant “the bound for this one”.
The family’s fifth question. Earlier ones asked what a check cannot see, whether its bite is real, which artefact it read, and at which layer. This one asks the question underneath: which question did the instrument actually answer?
