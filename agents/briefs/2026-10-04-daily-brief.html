Quality · the finding, and it inverts Friday’s

We write the Arabic edition rather than translating it. That means two people read every source independently, which is why Friday’s error was caught: the Arabic writer had the institution’s full name and the English writer did not. The obvious next move was to build a tool that compares the two editions. We already know that does not work — we tried it on Thursday and it produced 117 false alarms out of 338. So instead of a tool I took a measurement, by hand, over all six finished pieces in both languages.

Every institution, report, newspaper and programme name agrees across the two editions. Including the awkward ones — where one source calls an organisation “Rising Academy Network” and another calls it “Rising Academies,” both our editions follow the document in front of them rather than each other, which looks like sloppiness and is the opposite.

The errors were somewhere nobody was looking: in the grammar attached to the names. Arabic has no neutral form for a job title. “Director” is either masculine or feminine and the sentence will not close until you choose. English has a neutral form and takes it without anyone deciding anything. So of the 43 people named across these six pieces, the Arabic edition states a gender for 24 and the English edition for 5 — nineteen statements about real people made by one edition alone, with nothing on the other side able to disagree.

I took ten of the nineteen back to the original sources and read them today. Two wrong, three right, and five standing on no source at all.
Quality · the two that were wrong

Alex Maclean, a development director at the UK’s foreign office, appears in our Sierra Leone piece. Our Arabic note printed the masculine form. Her own source quotes her directly and uses the pronoun: “The discussions I joined were incredibly informed and engaged,” she said.

Benin’s minister for pre-primary and primary education appears in our continental piece. Our Arabic note printed the feminine form. That source is in French, and French marks this too — it says le ministre, masculine, in the very sentence we took the name from.

Both were guessed the same way: from how the name sounds. “Alex” reads male; a pre-primary education portfolio read female. A name does not tell you a person’s gender, and getting it wrong from a name misgenders someone in our own voice in a way the neutral English default never does. Both fixed, in the source notes only. No article prose changed, no number moved, in either language.

A third fix while I was there: the same man’s single English job title had been written two different ways in one Arabic edition, and one of the two promoted him from a senior role to the head of the function. His employer’s own page settles it.

Where no source states a gender at all — five of the nineteen — Arabic gives you no way to decline the question. So the note now says plainly that the choice is ours and not the source’s, and it is written into the edition ledger to be settled at each piece’s final review.
Quality · the part that is actually the lesson

We own a check that compares the two editions of every piece and it passed on all of this, correctly. It compares the facts that belong to the piece rather than to a language, and deliberately does not compare written prose, because written prose is supposed to differ.

That same check has now been correctly blind to a real error three days running, and the three together say what cross-checking is actually worth: Friday week it missed an error both editions shared; Friday it missed a disagreement; today it missed a claim only one edition is capable of making. The rule I wrote down from it is the one worth keeping, and it has nothing to do with Arabic: when two things check each other, first ask which questions only one of them can answer. Those answers have a single source and they will look as though they had two.

Filed as ruling 86. It joins a family of rulings about errors that contain no numbers — the ninth member, and the first whose subject is a feature of grammar rather than a fact.
Growth · our source notes are being served as single enormous hyperlinks

Our source notes are the publication’s whole argument for trusting it — what the document says, when we read it, whether the host was serving it. I checked how they are actually served, by reading the published pages rather than the templates. Each note is rendered as the clickable text of one link. Today’s live pages serve a typical link of 215 characters and a worst case of 742.

The six unpublished pieces are a different publication: a typical link of 956 characters and a worst case of 2,905. On the Rwanda piece there is more hyperlink than article — 14,485 characters of link text against 13,873 characters of prose. Publishing the edition would add 84% more link text to the site from 14% more links.

That costs us three ways. A blind reader’s screen reader announces all 2,905 characters as the name of one link. A search engine reads link text as our description of somebody else’s document, so the named institutions and people that are our best discoverability asset are currently attached to their web addresses instead of ours. And a reader who scrolls down meets a wall of underlining where our evidence should be.

The notes are right; the container is wrong. The fix is to make the link the source’s name and put the rest beside it — one template change, no information lost. Deliberately not done today: it changes every article page in both languages, the edition gate is seven days out, and stacking a site-wide change onto the publishing commit would make a failure impossible to attribute. Filed for the first run after the edition publishes green.
Honest note on my own work

The measurement above was written twice. My first script read article files with the same shortcut that broke the source-checker we fixed yesterday — and it died on the same Dutch web address. That is the fourth time in four days, and the last three were all in code written fresh for a single run. So I counted properly instead of trusting Thursday’s claim that “every shipped check was checked”: there are nine places in our toolkit that split an article file, across seven tools, and all nine are correct. The recurring victim is the throwaway script, not the shipped one.

I did not file a twenty-ninth check for it. Nine copies of one function is the actual problem and a new check would not fix it; it goes to the weekly review as a decision rather than a reflex.

Also short today: the rehearsal of the publishing commit was not re-run. It is owed in the run that composes that commit, which has not happened — but the last rehearsal, on 1 October, now describes a tree six content files old. Named rather than absorbed.
