The one thing worth your attention today

Eighty-seven phrases on forty-one of our published pages have been printed as footnotes in the middle of a sentence, in both languages, since the day the article template was written. Not a missing accent and not a wrong byte — a foreign phrase like học phí or fuṣhā, sitting mid-paragraph, was being pushed onto its own line, shrunk to about half the size of the text around it, greyed out, and given a gap above it. Fifty on the English pages and thirty-seven on the Arabic.

The cause is one word in one stylesheet rule, and the word is doing something nobody intended. The template has a rule for the small dagger note at the foot of a piece — a paragraph that is entirely one emphasised run. The way it asks that question cannot tell the difference between a paragraph that is one emphasised phrase and a paragraph that merely contains one. To the rule they are identical. Seventy-nine were the footnotes it was written for; eighty-seven were not.

It is fixed, and the fix had to change where the question is asked rather than how. The thing that separates the two cases is whether the phrase accounts for the whole paragraph — and a stylesheet has no way to compare a phrase with the paragraph around it. It is not a matter of writing a cleverer rule; the comparison is simply not available there. So the question moved into the build, which can answer it in a line, and the stylesheet is now handed the answer.

How it was found is the part worth keeping. It was not found by a check. Yesterday's job — fifty-six foreign names losing their italic on Arabic pages — was repaired this morning, and the repair worked on fifty-two of them and silently failed on four. Chasing four stragglers is how the eighty-seven turned up. The small failure was worth more than the large success.

Nothing needs you. Issue #13 is open its twenty-third day with its default running — pieces ship as singles, the option your own recommendation ranked third, and this brief keeps saying so. Everything below is on origin.

Today
Monday 16 November · brief 132

Quality — the board's oldest defect closed in a day, and its failures were worth more than its successes. The job was yesterday's: fifty-six foreign-language runs on ten published Arabic pages, upright since 25 October because a rule meant to stop Arabic being slanted was written about pages instead of about scripts.

The first thing the repair did was discover the fifty-six were not one problem. Reading each one against the same word in the English version of the same piece split them in three. Thirty-two are emphasis both editions agree on, and the Arabic page was dropping a mark its own pair carries. Twenty-four are something else entirely: the English body prints Nature, Elsevier and Poynter plainly, with no mark at all. On the Arabic page the writer added one — not to emphasise, but to show where a stretch of Latin letters begins and ends in a line of Arabic. There is no other way to mark that in our writing format. So the obvious repair would have made the Arabic edition italicise company names that the English edition sets plainly, inventing a disagreement between the two editions in the act of fixing one. Those twenty-four were left alone and sent to the Arabic Editor as a judgment, not a bug.

The last two are two cells of a three-cell table row whose third cell is Arabic. Repairing them would slant two thirds of one row and leave the rest upright. They are not a defect, and saying so is part of the work.

Thirty runs were marked, and each one's language was read rather than assumed. The Krio phrase on the Sierra Leone piece is marked Krio because our own English text says it is a Krio phrase. The three spellings of one Arabic name are marked as Arabic-in-Latin-letters, which is what they are; calling them English would have been convenient and false.

Then four of the thirty refused to change, and that is where the day turned. The new rule matched them — confirmed in a real browser, not reasoned about — and lost anyway, to a fourth rule in the stylesheet that nobody had written down. That rule is the footnote rule in the push block above, and following it produced the eighty-seven-phrase defect across both editions and forty-one pages. Measured, verified by looking at what a browser actually computes, and fixed. The four stragglers then came right on their own: thirty of thirty.

Content — the England piece passed all five tests and its third gate closed. Yesterday it was returned because a reader could not tell by the second paragraph why this was in front of them now. The remedy was a move, not a rewrite, and it was executed as one: the sequence that matters — the grade abolished in September 2024, the replacement in force from November 2025, its first annual figures published on 10 November this year, and the regulator's own sentence that the new grades cannot be compared to the old ones — now sits in the opening. Not one figure was re-reported and no source was re-read.

The dignity test passed on a structural reason rather than on an absence of slips. The piece discusses 367 schools that were re-graded after eight years of not being inspected, and it names none of them. Not one school is put on the record as having lost a grade. The only named person in 3,700 words is the then Chief Inspector, quoted in office. And the piece says before it draws its conclusion that the cohort holds the grade on the record steady and does not hold the school steady — leadership, intake, neighbourhood and funding all moved over an average of thirteen years. A piece about an instrument could easily have read as a verdict on people.

The fifth test found the one thing no check of ours could: the piece's list of related reading named two Bahrain pieces and missed The verdict, taken apart, our own July piece on England's first term with no Ofsted grade — the same regulator, the same replacement instrument, and the nearest neighbour this piece has anywhere in our corpus. Our link checker verifies every link a piece declares; it cannot see one that was never declared. A missing cross-reference is invisible to everything except a reader who knows the whole corpus.

Growth — the links between pieces were made to point both ways. Today's editorial finding was one missing cross-reference found by reading, so the growth job was to measure how many there are. Of 153 links between pieces, thirty-one pointed only one way. Twenty-two are the known kind: a new piece links back to an older one and nobody goes back to add the return link. Nine are a kind we had not named — someone did go back and add a forward link, and then did not add its counterpart. Twenty-two were written, in both languages, leaving nine.

Those nine cannot be written, and the reason is structural rather than a backlog. Six is the most links a piece carries. The pieces that cannot return a link are precisely the ones everything else points at — Oman, Korea, Sierra Leone — because they are already full. The most referenced pieces are structurally the least able to reference back. That is recorded with its reason rather than filed as work.

And adding those links turned one of our own checks red, which turned out to be the check's fault. It reported a blank region at the bottom of an Arabic page where nothing was wrong at all. It measures a page in 900-pixel bands, that page is 5,450 pixels tall, and the leftover fifty-pixel strip — the footer's bottom margin, blank on every page we have ever served — was being judged against a standard meant for a full screen. Whether it complained was decided by the page's height divided by 900, which has nothing to do with whether the page is right. Fixed so every band is a full band, and proved both ways: it stays quiet on a good page and still fails on a genuinely blank one.

It also obliged a correction to something this run itself had already said. This morning's commit claimed that check had found the footnote defect. It had not. It complained because the page got shorter, which happens to be what the defect did. The defect was found by reading a number. Keeping a noisy check because it once coincided with a real problem is how a check stops being a check.

Research — three rulings, and a gate caught a bookkeeping home that our own rule about bookkeeping does not mention. Filing a ruling obliges five counted places to move. The instrument that checks them found a sixth: a ratio elsewhere in the register whose denominator is the size of the whole register. First time one of those places has been caught by a machine instead of by a person noticing.

It outranks all sixty-seven open rows whatever their age.
