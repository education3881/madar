Daily brief · No. 104 Saturday 17 October 2026
Nothing owed by you · issue 11 is still open · its default falls on 22 October

Since the day we first published, every Arabic page has been setting its Arabic in the wrong typeface. Not Amiri — the face this publication chose for Arabic, pays to host, and ships thirty-four files for. Whatever serif the reader’s own computer happened to supply. For 145 days, on every Arabic page, in the edition the charter calls our distribution advantage.

The cause is one line of stylesheet that was correct and never took effect. It said Arabic pages use the Arabic face, and it was attached to the outermost element of the page so that everything inside would inherit it. A second, older line said the page body uses the Latin face — and a rule that names an element directly always beats one the element merely inherits. The two were never in competition. Reading them side by side does not show it; only asking the browser does.

And it was being paid for. Because the page declared a Latin face, the browser downloaded one — 278,872 bytes on every Arabic page, to draw nothing. The Arabic edition was the heavier of our two editions the whole time, which nobody had ever measured; it is now 43 per cent lighter than the English. Measured across all 88 article pages before and after, with the entire English side used as a control and unchanged in every statistic.

A published piece also stopped spelling a name our way instead of its own. The Kuwait piece called a programme the Multi-sensory Structured Arabic Language Programme. Its own source prints Program. Programme is our spelling — the house style, applied correctly, to the one kind of word on the page that is not ours to spell. It read right, it read consistent, and it was wrong for 142 days, in the citation as well as the sentence. That is ruling 104.

Today

A rule that was in the stylesheet and never in the page, and a name spelled in our accent

2026-10-17 · Saturday · five functions, seven commits, all on origin
The queue head · seven days old, and the specification was the part that was wrong

The board chose today’s first job by rule for the eleventh time in twelve days, and this time without needing yesterday’s newly-invented tiebreak: six items stood past the passed-over threshold — the most it has ever offered — and age alone separated them at seven days. Yesterday’s log had named this item in advance, in those words.

The item asked for one of two things: shrink the typefaces to only the letters we use, or drop a typeface. Neither was needed, and neither was taken. The whole saving came from not downloading a face that no Arabic page renders a single word in. What the item had actually found was a symptom.

Three more Arabic rules turned out never to have matched anything at all. The site builder scopes each component’s styles by stamping a private marker on them; it stamped that marker onto a selector pointing at the page’s outermost element, which no component owns, so those rules compiled into addresses no page this site builds can ever have. The Arabic footer’s alignment has been written and inert since it was typed.

Quality · and why six years of green checks never saw it

Build clean four times over. Seventeen gates inside the build, twelve more run by hand against the built site, twelve exit codes printed one at a time with nothing between an instrument and its verdict. The register derives 29 of 32 assertions gate the deploy from the three files that hold the answer.

The uncomfortable part is why none of them could have found today’s defect. We own three checks that drive a real browser over Arabic pages. One of them reads the page’s computed styling — and it exists because of this exact family of bug; its own note in the stylesheet says so. It reads letter-spacing. It has stood one property away from this for thirty days. The other two ask which language a string is in and how many coloured pixels are in a band, and a different serif in the same colour is invisible to both.

One sentence I nearly put in this brief was false, and testing it is the only reason it isn’t here. The first explanation was lovely: a single full stop at the end of an Arabic sentence, the only Latin character in it, pulling down 147 KB of Latin italic. It was checkable, so it was checked — remove the full stop and nothing changed. A clean, memorable, wrong story, stopped by trying it.

Content · a programme’s name, spelled in our accent for 142 days

Every check we own compares the article against its citation. Here the citation carried the same error, so that comparison passed, correctly, on two matching wrong answers. And the Arabic edition — which has caught the last three of these — could not help: Arabic has no capital letters and no Programme/Program distinction, so it was right twice and could not have been wrong. A second opinion that is incapable of disagreeing looks exactly like a second opinion that agrees.

The source itself refuses every client we have — a flat block, not a dead link — so it was read from a public archive’s April copy and quoted rather than summarised. Five strings repaired in one file; the Arabic edition untouched because it was never wrong. Seven further references spell the institution’s name in British English where the institution uses American; that is a voice decision and has been put to the Editor rather than taken by me.

Growth · the measurement, and three corrections to last week’s

Arabic article pages: 574,500 → 295,628 bytes of typeface at the median of all forty-four. English articles: unchanged at every point of the distribution, which is the control.

The baseline this was measured against was wrong in three ways, and all three are one mistake. A total of 2.79 MB for “the bundle on disk” was summed over the stylesheet’s seventy-three declarations rather than its thirty-four files, in a row whose neighbouring cell said thirty-four — so the saving credited to last week’s font migration was overstated nearly threefold. And “an English article weighs 501,864 bytes” was one page reported as a fact about a hundred and eighteen, against a real spread of 216 KB. All three corrected in place on the original note rather than quietly restated here.

Research · rulings 103 and 104, and a gate that ran out of numbers

103 — a rule that cannot match is indistinguishable from a rule that matches and does nothing. Every instrument this operation owns reads an output. A rule that never applied leaves nothing in the output to read: it fails by looking exactly like a rule whose job was already done. That is why four of them survived 145 days of green checks.

104 — the house spelling is not ours to apply to a name. Every other rule in a style guide operates on our own prose, where we are the authority. A name is the one string on the page whose authority is somewhere else.

Filing two rulings pushed one of the guidebook’s tables to one hundred rows, and the build failed: the gate that checks those counts could read number words up to ninety-nine and no further. It refused correctly and then had no number it could accept. Fixed and proved both ways on scratch copies — control silent, six deliberate errors all caught — and the gate immediately found a count I had missed and would not have found alone. Six counts moved today; the instrument found two of them.

Tomorrow

How much of what we wrote never reached the page

2026-10-18 · Sunday · weekly review day · the forward question enters the board at the head
The forward question

How many of the styling rules this site ships match nothing on any page it serves? Today found one that had been in the file since the first day we published and in the page never, and three more compiled into addresses no page can have. The question is cheap to answer — ask the browser, for each rule, whether anything on any page matches it — and the answer is a number.

The interesting half is not the dead rules. It is the ones written for one edition that never reach it. All four found today were on the Arabic side, and that is unlikely to be chance: the edition with fewer eyes on it is the edition whose rules nobody notices failing.

And what is still waiting

Ten items on the board, one closed and two opened, so it is up one — and the honest reason is that closing today’s head split it in two. The half that was a defect is fixed; the half that is a judgment about the publication’s face is queued under its own number rather than buried in a closure note. An English article still downloads about 521 KB of typefaces, and on an Arabic page one typeface costs 71 KB to draw the single word Madār in the footer. The oldest item ever passed over reads six days, flat for a second day, because the item closed was again the oldest and the next inherited its place.

Tomorrow is Sunday and the weekly review runs. Issue 11 has five days to its default.
