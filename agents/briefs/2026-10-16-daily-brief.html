Today the operation made itself cheaper to run, and found out why it had got expensive. Every run is required to read six documents before it is allowed to plan anything — the charter, the rulebook, yesterday’s brief, the live edition’s ledger, the work queue and the repository guide. Those six had grown to 403 KB. One run in October ran out of its budget inside the reading and delivered nothing at all. Today they are 214 KB.

And the largest single item on that list had been the wrong document for three days. It was Edition 05’s working ledger — 194 KB of a wave’s day-by-day history. That wave shipped on 13 October. So since the flip, every run has been instructed to read a finished edition’s diary as though it were today’s state. It is now a 4.5 KB record of what shipped and what is still owed, with the full history archived and one new standing rule: a wave’s memo is cut down to its record in the week it ships.

A published piece also stopped saying something untrue, and the Arabic edition is again how we know. Oman’s school inspectorate has been named in both editions, in the present tense, by a name the Sultan’s own decree retired on 12 January — five months before that piece published, and 118 days of serving it since. Nothing we own could have caught it: there is no number in a name, both languages made the same mistake so the parity check had nothing to compare, and the source link answers 200 perfectly well. What caught it is that the Arabic citations and the English citations disagreed with each other. That is now ruling 102.

Content · a name the state retired in January, served until this morning

Both editions of the Oman piece called the inspectorate the Oman Authority for Academic Accreditation and Quality Assurance of Education, in the present tense. Royal Decree 12/2026, dated 12 January 2026, dropped the accreditation clause and moved professional licences and the national and international studies to it from the Ministry of Education. The piece published on 20 June — five months later.

The sharper half is in the citation, not the sentence. Both editions carried a government-portal source whose note read “renamed in 2026”. That page says nothing about a rename: read today, it prints the new name as its title and the old one in its staff listing, with no date and no instrument. The note was right by accident and unsupported by the document it was attached to. Both bodies and both citation sets are repaired, and both now cite the decree itself, each in its own language.

Growth · the newest six pieces had no way in

Measured on the built site rather than guessed: Edition 05 shipped three days ago with zero inbound links from the other 38 pieces. Its six pieces point outward into the older corpus twelve times — the Editor curated those — and nothing pointed back. A reader on any older piece had no path to the newest work except the home page or an index.

The cause is mechanical and will recur on every wave: a piece can only link to pieces that already exist, so a new wave is born un-pointed-at, permanently, unless someone goes back. The newest work is structurally the least reachable, and gets worse with each wave. Ten links added today in both languages, every one the reverse of a pairing the Editor had already approved, capped at five per piece. One piece is still unreachable from outside its own edition and is named for the Editor rather than guessed at — which older piece belongs beside a four-country comparison is a judgment about the argument, not about the graph.

Research · ruling 102, and it is about the tense of a name

A name is a claim about now. A body that names an institution in the present tense re-asserts that name every day the page is served — so a name read correctly during research can be false by publication and falser after. It is re-read at the last gate before a piece goes live, never at research, and the date of that reading is recorded. The second limb: a citation’s parenthetical that states a fact is a sentence, held to a sentence’s standard. And the register of record for a rename is the decree that performs it — never the renamed body’s own website, which shows the result and often both names at once without ever mentioning the event.

The forward question

Today’s “renamed in 2026” was a dated factual claim attached to a page that carries no such statement, and it survived 118 days and four gates for one structural reason: every check we own measures the article against the citation, and nothing measures the citation.
