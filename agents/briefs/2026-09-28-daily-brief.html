Manager → Founder

Edition 05 is a complete bilingual set, and the one channel we fully own has been quietly mis-arranging itself for six weeks.

The Rwanda piece now exists in Arabic. That was the last missing half of the last piece, so all six pieces of the edition are written in both languages and on disk. Four of the six are fully approved through every gate; the remaining two are the Editor’s desk this week.

The Arabic forced a decision none of the previous five pairs did. Every one of Rwanda’s eight sources is an English document — two ministry statistical yearbooks, four ministry web pages, a World Bank report, a Kigali newspaper. There is no Arabic original anywhere. So any quotation in the Arabic version can only be our translation of an English sentence, and this piece’s whole argument is made of specific words: whether the ministry wrote “declined only marginally” or something firmer, whether it wrote “may not have received training” or “did not.” A translated hedge is a hedge we chose. So the Arabic carries those sentences in the ministry’s own English with an Arabic gloss beside each one.

We then had to decide whether to do the same for the one teacher the piece quotes. She is the only human voice in it, and her words carry no hedge, so translating hers and preserving the ministry’s would have been the lighter, more readable choice. We did not. The piece has a section observing that every voice in this story reaches the reader through the funder or the ministry — and a policy that printed the ministry in its own language and the teacher in translation would have built that hierarchy into the page, in the very section that complains about it.

Separately, and this is the day’s other finding: our RSS feeds have been arranging 43 of their 232 text fields according to the reader’s language rather than their own. An English summary read in an Arabic-language app puts its closing full stop on the left. The Arabic feed’s own subtitle does the same. The brand line Madār · مدار reads in a different order for different subscribers. We had written direction-handling code for this in August and half of it does nothing at all — a fact we asserted as true in a comment in our own source for thirty-six days and had never once measured.

Nothing published today, and nothing was meant to be. Edition 05 ships as one gated wave. The sixty-second consecutive day without a new piece is the wave being assembled.

Today

The edition is complete in both languages, and a forty-day question finally got asked

Monday 28 September 2026 · Asia/Dubai
Content · Rwanda in Arabic, and the edition is a complete bilingual set
«عدَّت رواندا المعلِّمين الّذين لم تُدرِّبهم، مرَّتين» — composed, not translated, and passed the Arabic Editor’s gate. Headline 51 characters of 100, standfirst 194 of 200, both measured before the body was written and both inside the limit on the first attempt. That is the fifth pair in a row to manage it since we moved the ruler upstream from the final gate to the writer’s desk.
Every number in the English appears in the Arabic and vice versa: 110 against 110, zero one-sided figures in either direction. The edition’s second pair to achieve that. One real mismatch was caught while composing, and its cause is new to us: carrying an English quotation and glossing it in Arabic states every number inside the quotation twice.
Content · two names, verified rather than guessed
Rwanda’s State Minister for Education is named in this piece, so her name had to be spelled in Arabic. Two search engines offered the same spelling. Neither was accepted — our own rule forbids citing a name from a search summary, so a Qatari newspaper’s page from March 2025 was opened and the spelling read off it directly.
The teacher the piece quotes is a primary schoolteacher in Kigali with no Arabic coverage anywhere, as expected. Her name was spelled from a documented routing filed today — and the useful part is that a Rwandan name often needs two routings, because the given name is frequently French and the family name Kinyarwanda. Getting that wrong produces a plausible Arabic string that is nobody’s name.
Quality · the forty-day question, asked first and answered
We have known since 18 August that nothing verified our feeds are readable in an actual reader. It was named six times and never done, because it lived in the queue that had no teeth. Sunday moved it into the queue that does. It was this morning’s first work, before anything new was started.
The test renders every field a subscriber’s app renders — 232 of them — twice: once inside a left-to-right page and once inside a right-to-left one, with none of our own styling present. If a field carries its own direction it looks identical both times. If it does not, it inherits the reader’s. 43 fields inherited.
Quality · what was broken, and the half that was already right
All 38 English summaries put their closing full stop on the wrong side for an Arabic-language reader. Both feeds’ own descriptions and both their titles did the same. One Arabic headline put a closing quotation mark on the wrong side. All 38 Arabic summaries were correct — the wrapper we added in August genuinely works, and we now know that by measurement rather than by assumption.
The check also correctly stays silent on 37 of 38 Arabic headlines, which need no direction marking at all because they contain no punctuation or Latin at their edges. A check that shouted about those would have been switched off within a week.
Quality · the sentence in our own source that was not true
Our feed code has said since 23 August that a particular invisible character — a right-to-left mark — “sets the paragraph base direction.” It does not. It answers a guess a browser makes only when nothing else has decided, and a reader’s app has always already decided. Measured: the same Arabic text with and without that character lays out identically, to the pixel.
The character that does work is a different one, and was never tried. It is now on every plain-text field of both feeds. The general lesson is the valuable part: we wrote that sentence from reading a specification rather than from rendering anything. A promise made to somebody else’s software is verified in somebody else’s software.
Quality · the twenty-sixth standing check, proved seven ways
The test became a permanent gate in the same commit that proved it: it fails the build, so it cannot quietly stop being run. It was proved against the real defect as it was actually being served, before any fix existed — which is the only reason the check cannot have been shaped to pass our own repair. Then five further faults were injected into the fixed version and it caught all five.
26 checks now, 23 of which block a deploy, counted in both places they are wired rather than incremented from yesterday’s number. One of the five injections replaced the new character with the old useless one and the check still failed, which is the day’s finding stated as a test rather than as prose.
Quality · our own measuring tools were wrong three times in one day
Recorded plainly because the pattern matters more than the instances. A word-counting pattern treated 21,717, and 21,717 as different numbers. A fault we injected to test the new check landed somewhere other than where we labelled it, and still failed correctly — so the check was right and our description of the experiment was wrong. And a pattern used to count our own checks left one out because its name contains a digit, which for several minutes looked like a missing safety gate.
None of the three reached the publication; all three produced a wrong number about our own machinery, which is the failure we spent yesterday learning to distrust. Two became numbered rulings today rather than notes.
Growth · the experiment paid, on the first day of the week
The feeds are the only distribution channel this publication owns outright — no platform decides who sees them and nothing about them needs anyone’s permission. An Arabic subscriber meeting our feed for the first time was meeting a subtitle whose punctuation sat on the wrong side. That is fixed and measured.
On traffic, plainly: the site carries no third-party tracker by design, so we collect no visitor analytics and no number here is estimated. What can be stated exactly is that 43 of 232 fields were wrong and none are now. The second half of the week’s experiment is not done and is named as owed, not quietly rolled forward.

One ruling on translation policy as an editorial claim, one on verifying a mechanism where it is consumed rather than where it is specified, and one on proving that an injected fault landed where you said it did and not merely somewhere. The fourth entry is the Kinyarwanda name routing, so the next Rwandan or Great Lakes piece inherits today’s decisions instead of re-making them at a gate.
All four counts that track this register moved in the same commit as the rulings — the first run to execute that rule since it was written on Sunday. Doing so required re-running the reconciliation, which found three file references in the register that do not resolve. Nothing was lost; a filing convention was. Sunday’s review had reported zero, because it checked one of the two sections and reported the whole.
