The one thing worth your attention today

Every article we publish has been telling search engines that our sources are titled with our own commentary about them. Under each piece sits a machine-readable bibliography — the thing that lets a search engine see that Madār rests on named government registers rather than on other people's articles. It is the single strongest claim this publication can make to a machine, and we have been making it in the wrong field. Each entry carried a title, and into that title went the whole of our note about the document: why we opened it, what state we found it in, what we declined to use. 714 entries. The middle one ran to 274 characters. The longest ran to 3,237. A consumer stops showing a title at about 110.

So a machine was told that an Ofsted press release is called “read in served text 2026-11-14, HTTP 200, the three quoted sentences, gender: this page carries no third-person pronoun…” and so on, for paragraphs. It is the same defect as the share-card failure of August, one surface over, and worse in one specific way: a promise that loads correctly, renders correctly and says the wrong thing cannot fail. Our own checker passed it every single day, correctly — every reference parsed, every link resolved. Nobody had ever asked whether a field meant what its name says.

Fixed today, and measured on both sides of the change. The note moves to the field built for notes, so nothing a machine was told is lost. The title is now filled in only where the writer actually marked the document's own name — and left empty everywhere else, because telling a machine nothing about a title is better than telling it something false. Titles over the cut-off length: 663 before, zero after. The longest title on the site went from 3,237 characters to 105.

The obvious shortcut was tried twice and refused twice. Cutting each note at its first bracket improves 503 entries and damages 163, because a bracket is as often part of a name as the start of a remark. A narrower version measured this morning was worse. A better guess is still a guess, and this is a field we publish about documents other people own.

Today
Thursday 19 November · brief 135

Content — the independent check on the England piece, step six of eight, and it found something I did not expect. The writer's own figures all hold. Four of the five government registers were re-opened today by a different reader, and every number and every quoted sentence in the published text is there, word for word — the 367 schools, the two different seventeen per cents that mean near-opposite things, the thirteen-year average gap, the 2026 inspection counts. That is the result that matters and the piece is in good shape.

What did not hold was the writer's list of corrections. When he re-read the sources while drafting, he recorded four places where the commissioning brief had got something wrong. Three of those four are themselves wrong — and all three in the same direction. He wrote that a sentence “is not in” a register. It is. Another: “not confirmed”. It is in the sentence immediately after one he quotes. A third: “did not appear”. It appears twice. Nothing he published is wrong — the errors are in his working notes and in one source annotation that readers do see, and all four are now scheduled for repair before the piece ships.

The reason this is worth your attention rather than a shrug: a claim that something is in a document can be checked by anyone who opens it. A claim that something is not there can only be checked by the same unsuccessful search that produced it — so it confirms itself every time it is re-run. It is the one kind of claim we make that nothing we own can catch, and the fourth correction, written in the same hour by the same person using the same method, was perfectly sound. Nothing on the page distinguished them.

Quality — the oldest job on the board, closed at 25 days. It asked which of our checks look at a sample rather than at everything, and how much lives outside each sample. The answer is now derived rather than guessed: of 42 checks, 32 read everything and five read a sample, and the widest of those five sees five per cent of what it is a statement about. The list of five was previously written by hand and had missed one.

Then the harder half, which nobody had ever done: run one of those samples over the whole site. Every page we serve carries Arabic — the wordmark alone puts it on all 69 English pages — which is a denominator nobody had written down. The check reads seven pages; the full sweep covered 139, found 4,709 pieces of Arabic text and zero faults. A clean result, and it is worth saying plainly: the sample is narrow and the thing it was sampling is fine. The sweep took under two minutes, which quietly removes the usual excuse for sampling and is now the next question on the board.

Research — one new standing rule, and a pattern across the whole day. The rule is the one above: a failed search is not a silence. What makes it more than an anecdote is that the same underlying mistake — a program or a person being wrong about the edge of a category — happened three separate times today, in three different parts of the operation, one day after we wrote it up as a rule. A list of file types was read as a list of web pages. A search missed a sentence because the page uses a curly apostrophe and the search used a straight one. And today's new title-finder read the apostrophe in “the release's own words” as a closing quotation mark, and produced a nonsense title from it. All three printed something that looked exactly like a healthy answer. All three were caught by looking at the output rather than at the exit code.

Nothing editorial published today, and I want to be plain about that. The England piece has two steps left — its illustration and share card, then the flip that publishes it. It will ship on the day its own gate closes. The last piece to publish was Bahrain, on 9 November.
