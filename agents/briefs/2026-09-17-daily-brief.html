Manager → Founder

We had written the rule down. We had written it down for headings.

Arabic is a cursive script: the letters join, and the font draws the strokes that connect them. Put Latin letter-spacing between them and the joins are pulled apart — the word is still a row of recognisable shapes, and it is no longer a word. Our stylesheet has known that since the first build. It says so, in one rule, applied to Arabic headings.

Tracking is not a property of headings. It is a property of the script. So this morning, asking a question that had been owed for two runs, we measured what the browser actually computes rather than what our CSS says — and found 149 Arabic words and labels served with their joins broken, on every page of both editions: the navigation, the country and date lines, the footer, the caption under every drawing, and العربية, the link that carries an English reader into the Arabic edition, on all sixty-one English pages.

It is fixed, in one rule, proved four ways, and asserted on every build from now on. The part worth your minute is not the bug. It is that the share card advertising the Arabic edition was correctly typeset the whole time — we fixed that one thirty days ago and never looked one directory over.

Today

A question asked at the right layer, and a crown with no reading behind it

2026-09-17 — Brief No. 76
Quality · the forward question, and what it found
The question had been carried, unanswered, for two runs: nothing we own can see whether Arabic renders as Arabic. It came with two stated obstacles and both turned out to be wrong, which was the first useful thing. We thought we lacked a browser to drive — we have had one in the cloud since the render check landed on 09-15. And the fallback we had planned, reading our own CSS to see which rules reach Arabic, would have been the wrong check: there are about 130 letter-spacing rules in the built stylesheet, working out by hand which ones land on an Arabic word means rebuilding a browser badly, and it would have missed the worst case entirely.

So we asked the browser instead: for every element whose own text is Arabic, what letter-spacing does it actually compute? Four Arabic pages, two English pages and the standalone instrument. All seven bit. One hundred and forty-nine runs of Arabic with tracking on them.
The worst instance had nothing to blame. On the VALENCE page the tracking comes from a rule set on body — a rule that names no class, no element and no language. Nothing to audit, nothing to grep, no component at fault. The value only exists after the browser has finished resolving everything, which is precisely why reading the stylesheet would have found nothing there and why the check reads what resolved instead.
Quality · the fix, and four proofs
One rule, keyed to the language rather than to the writing direction — because the Arabic on the English pages sits inside a left-to-right layout and would have been missed by a direction rule. It is the only !important in the stylesheet outside the print block, and deliberately so: a component that tracks its Latin form must not be able to win that back on an Arabic word.

Proved against a control and three separate injections, each one checked to have actually changed the file before the check was run on it. Control: silent, 326 Arabic elements, all clean. Delete the fix: 148 failures — and that number is itself the proof, being the 149 we found minus the one whose fix lives in a file the injection did not touch. Add a rule that outranks the fix: caught. Strip an Arabic page of its Arabic entirely, leaving the CSS perfect: caught, because a check that sweeps nothing has failed rather than passed.
Filed as ruling #55 and wired into the build in the same commit that proved it. Fifteen of our seventeen standing checks now gate the deploy, and the two that do not say why, in the workflow file, at the point where the others are listed. The whole build with all of them runs in eighteen seconds.
Content · Edition 05 slot 3 verified — five items, none of them a number
The cloud run’s queue head, taken as written and closed on day two of a seven-day clock. All four source documents were downloaded and read in the body today rather than summarised — which is the only reason two of the five items exist, because both are a clause in French and neither survives a summary.

The piece said the report repeats a finding for mathematics. It does not. It names Senegal and Chad there, and separates them. The word only belongs to the page about reading. That is exactly the ruling we filed two days ago — a claim inherits the scope of the cell it was printed in — recurring inside the very piece the ruling was filed on, two sections from the note that produced it. A ruling filed on a piece does not sweep the piece.

And the report hedges where we did not. It says Senegal appears as the only such country. We wrote is. What makes this one new: for three verdicts running, the pattern has been that our citation was right and our sentence was wrong, and we built a hand-off check for exactly that. Here the citation was wrong too — so the check we built could not have caught it, because both sides of the comparison agreed. Only re-opening the source catches that, and that is what a verification pass is for.
Third item closed: the World Bank brief prints a year beside every figure, and we dropped two of them — 40 percent (2006) and 4.8 percent (2018) — behind the brief’s blanket “pre-COVID”. Pre-COVID is a floor, not a date, and the older of those figures is fourteen years adrift of it. All three fixed in both languages; the numbers on both sides re-counted afterwards and still matching.
Content · two items I did not close, and one of them blocks the wave
The piece crowns two countries on a ruler whose source names four. Ruler two asks how much learning a system produces given what it can afford. The analyst’s own sentence clears Gabon, Senegal, Benin and Burkina Faso over the line. It matters because of which two were dropped: Gabon is an upper-middle-income country and Burkina Faso is among the poorest on earth, so on a ruler about means, the piece let go of the strongest claim and the weakest without a word either way. In the article whose entire argument is that a rank without its ruler is an opinion with decimal places. Growth · the bilingual entry point, counted
The charter calls Arabic a distribution advantage rather than a translation cost. That advantage is not delivered by the Arabic articles existing — it is delivered by the handful of Arabic words sitting inside the English pages that say this exists in your language. Counted off the build: 61 English pages, every one of them carrying Arabic, 264 such elements — the language switch 119 times, the wordmark 60, the link to each article’s twin 38, the caption 38.

Every one of them was tracked. The links worked, the crawler was happy, and a reader who clicked arrived. What was wrong is narrower and worse: at the exact moment an Arabic-reading visitor is asked to believe this publication can set their language, it was visibly failing to.
No traffic number here or anywhere — the site carries no third-party tracker by design and nothing is estimated. What can be said is a count, and the count is now zero, asserted on every build. The share card was rendered and looked at: its Arabic is correct, which is the finding rather than the comfort.

The forward question, and it is the residue of today’s
Today’s new check proves no Arabic is tracked. It does not prove the letters actually joined. A missing font would pass it in silence, because a computed style reports what was asked for and never what the font delivered. The candidate is to stop looking at pixels and measure text instead: a correctly joined Arabic word is narrower than the same letters unjoined, so render one known string twice and compare the widths. The control we do not yet have is a font deliberately broken — and a check for “the font worked” that has never seen a font fail is a green light wired to nothing.
