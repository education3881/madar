The one thing worth your attention today

Two jobs on the board had been waiting for somebody to have an opinion about typography, and neither needed one. Both asked the same question: is a single letter worth the file the browser downloads to draw it? One was our own name — the bar over the a in Madār. Drawing that one bar was costing the English home page 98,232 bytes, because the letter sits just outside the character set in the font file the page already has, so the browser fetched a second, much larger file to paint one mark.

There turned out to be a mechanical test, and it answers both jobs in opposite directions. Our font files advertise, in their own configuration, that they can draw a bare macron as a separate mark. So the accented letter can be written as a plus the bar, and the page draws it from the file it already downloaded. That is not a compromise: it is the same shape, and it was proved so before anything was changed — identical letter widths in three different typefaces, and zero differing pixels out of 3,120,000 on a full-page comparison of the Editions page before and after.

The other job was the opposite and is now closed as a decision, not a defect. A Krio phrase on the Sierra Leone piece contains the letter ɔ, which costs 10,532 bytes. Unlike the macron, that letter has no cheaper equivalent — there is no way to build it from pieces the page already holds. So the choice was between spelling a named language correctly and saving ten kilobytes, and the answer is that we spell it correctly and write the price down. A cost is a defect only when a cheaper version of being right exists; otherwise it is simply what being right costs.

Measured, after: 284,492 bytes removed across the six pages we measure, with nothing on screen changed. The English Editions page lost 140,900 of that — more than the home page the job was written about, because the five edition covers set our name at display size and pulled in two more font files. The Arabic Editions page was the only Arabic page paying anything, for the same reason.

Today
Tuesday 17 November · brief 133

Quality — the board's only outstanding defect closed, together with the job the board itself had said was the same question. Both are described above. What is worth adding is what was deliberately not touched: our name appears in page titles, link previews, feed entries and the hidden text that screen readers announce, and in all of those it was left exactly as it was. None of them costs a byte of font, and all of them are read by machines that match text letter for letter. The rule applied was narrow on purpose — change it only where a human actually sees it drawn.

Three published articles still spell our name the expensive way in their own text, and that is the Editor's call rather than mine. Seven pieces carry an accented letter in their body. Four of them — fuṣhā, ʿāmmiyya, Māori, pāngarau — cannot be made cheaper at any price, for exactly the reason the Krio letter cannot. The other three are our own name, and they could be. But that means editing the text of a published article for a technical reason, and a published sentence is not mine to edit. Handed over with the proof attached.

Content — the Arabic edition of the England piece is written, and writing it found something the English edition had been saying invisibly. This is the fourth of eight steps for that piece; the Arabic Editor's gate is next. Three of its sources were re-opened and read from scratch by the Arabic desk today rather than trusted from the English draft, and every figure held, including the one that matters most: the government's own table lists 17% twice in a single row, four columns apart, meaning two near-opposite things. Neither edition prints that figure bare.

Arabic forced a decision English never has to make, twice. The first was the named official. The press release we quote her from introduces her by job title only and contains no pronoun anywhere — and Arabic cannot write “she said” without committing to it. Her own speech calls her His Majesty's Chief Inspector, where the His belongs to the monarch and tells you nothing about her. The determination was taken from a different page of the regulator's own site, whose summary line says her first speech. Read from the source, not guessed from the name.

The second one is the find of the day. Arabic marks the future tense by attaching a letter to the verb, so a writer cannot put a verb on the page without deciding whether the sentence is about the future. Making that decision exposed a sentence in the English edition — “the only one that will ever exist on that scale” — which is our claim about the future, not the regulator's. The regulator says only that its new grades cannot be compared with the old ones, which is not the same as promising nobody will ever measure that way again. The English piece's own checklist records zero such sentences, and the Editor approved it with that line on file. The Arabic has been rewritten to attribute the dated guidance and drop the prediction; the English sentence goes back to the Editor as one correction.

We have a standing note saying that a text search for future tense in Arabic finds nothing useful. That is true — today's search returned four matches and all four were fragments of unrelated words. But what makes a machine blind to it is exactly what makes the writer unable to avoid it. The same grammar, pointing two ways depending on whether you are asking a program or a person.

Growth — for nine days the publication told every search engine it was Edition 05, on every page we serve. Editions closed at five on 8 November. The piece published on 9 November belongs to no edition, and the one being written now belongs to no edition. Meanwhile the English home page's title — the line that shows in a browser tab, a shared link and a search result — read Madār · مدار — Edition 05, and the footer said the same thing on all 139 pages. A reader arriving at the front door was told the page was an edition, then handed a list whose newest item is not in one.

Two details make it worse than a stale label, and both are useful. Our own code already knows whether an edition is open, and carries a written instruction to check that before labelling anything. Three places on the home page check it. The title and the footer did not. And the Arabic home page's title has never claimed an edition — so the two language editions were contradicting each other on the single most visible line we publish, and nothing we own compares them, because our pair-checking tool reads articles and a home page is not an article.

Fixed by deriving the label instead of hard-coding it, and the real fix is a distinction: what the page is and what a section of it is are now different sentences. Inside the Edition 05 block the label is untouched, because that block is Edition 05. The claim that was wrong was never that the edition exists — it was that the whole page was it. Page-level claims of Edition 05 went from 139 to nil; one mention remains, and it is the correct one.

No decision of yours was pre-empted. Issue #13 is still open. If you set a frame for what comes next, one setting flips and every one of these lines follows it. The new English title is built from words this publication already uses about itself.

Research — two rulings, and both are about a line everybody trusted carrying a claim nobody checked. The first is the defect-or-price test above, which turned a week of scheduled opinion into three lines of arithmetic. The second is the Arabic grammar finding. Both were filed with the counts they move, and our register check caught me twice while I filed them — once for malformed table rows, and once for raising a family's stated size to sixty-one while leaving its list of members at sixty. That second one is the exact mistake a ruling from nine days ago exists to catch, committed by the run filing a ruling about unchecked claims. Caught by the gate, not by me.

And the first paragraph of the first file every run reads was wrong again, in a new way. It explains how to work out the date of the last piece published, and the method it names returns 23 October — the date printed on the piece — when the piece actually went live on 9 November. It names the right piece and the wrong date, which is why nobody caught it for nine days: anyone checking recognised the piece and stopped there. Corrected to the method that actually works.

The defect list is empty for the first time in five days, so tomorrow's job comes from the ordinary backlog and is the oldest thing on it. The board stands at seventy-three: none currently wrong, seventy open questions, three waiting on a credential or a purchase nobody here holds.

Four of eight steps remain after that.

What today could not see, carried forward in writing. Every measurement today was a comparison of two versions of our own work — and that is the only reason the answers are trustworthy. The macron was settled by putting both spellings side by side and subtracting. The Krio letter got the opposite verdict and got it by argument, because there is no second version to compare. Every instrument this operation owns works by comparing two things and reporting the difference. A price has no alternative to compare against, so none of them can price one — and the corpus is now full of costs that are entirely correct. Tomorrow's question: name one thing this publication pays where no second version exists, and say what could possibly measure it.

Nothing editorial shipped today and the brief says so plainly. The corpus is unchanged at 45 English and 45 Arabic, parity 100%. What changed on the live site is that every page stopped claiming to be Edition 05, the front door's title and share text changed, and six measured pages got meaningfully lighter with nothing visibly different.
