Nothing owed by you · yesterday’s deploy went green · no missed day · three days until one line on both home pages goes false, and it is yours

Nothing is waiting on you. Yesterday’s deploy fired at 11:24:14Z and went green. Yesterday’s brief had said, measured at 11:22Z, that no deploy had fired yet. Both were true: the step that publishes runs after the step that writes the brief, so a run reporting on its own deploy always reports on a moment that has not happened. It fired two minutes later — which is exactly the gap it was on 27 October, to the minute. A pattern measured twice is a pattern, and it is a known gap with a queue row, not a fault. The site now serves 28 October on 53 of its pages.

One thing is genuinely yours, and it has three days. On 1 November both home pages begin telling every reader that the current issue is October 2026 — a month that will have ended. The fix is to say what the next issue is an issue of, and that is an edition-scope decision, which is yours and not mine. It is GitHub issue #13, open four days. If nobody answers, the line goes false on its own and I will say so here rather than quietly relabel it.

Today’s one-sentence version. A job this operation had been calling impossible for six days was three-quarters possible on the day it was raised, and the reason is worth more than the repair: the organisation that prints a name is usually not the organisation we had cited for the story.

Four published pieces used an abbreviation and then spelled it out — a Kenyan national assessment, a Singapore examination, a Sierra Leonean teacher database, an American research lab — while the source list on those same pieces never gave the full name anywhere. The note on my desk said this could not be fixed, because two of the four sources will not open from this machine at all. That was true. I re-checked both this morning and it is still true. It was also the wrong conclusion three times out of four.

A ministry announces an exam. A board runs it. A system is the database. A lab is the lab. Those are four different organisations on four different websites, and the one with the name over its own front door is almost never the one that issued the press release. Singapore’s ministry would not open; the examinations board that actually sets the paper opened immediately and spells the name out in its first sentence. Sierra Leone’s research reports describe the teacher database without ever naming it; the database’s own website prints its name as its headline. Same for the lab. Three of the four are fixed, with the words read off the owner’s own page today.

The fourth one I made the publication say less. Kenya’s exam council does not open from here, the one archived copy of its page is an empty shell, the newspaper that carries ten of that piece’s twelve sources blocks this machine — and the sources that do open disagree with each other about the words, between “Junior School” and “Junior Secondary”. So both editions now simply use the abbreviation and keep the plain-English description that follows it, which our own sources do support. A reader who did not know the acronym is told slightly less than before. That is a real cost and I would rather pay it than print one of two wordings and hope.

And the Arabic edition had been quietly disagreeing with the English one about that name since July. Arabic cannot carry a Latin abbreviation, so whoever wrote the Arabic had no choice but to go and read the source — and what they wrote maps onto the other of the two disputed English readings. A hundred and seventeen days, and nothing we own was capable of noticing, because every name-checking tool we have is anchored on Latin letters. This is the third time this month the Arabic edition has turned out to hold the better answer in a place no instrument of ours can read. I am increasingly of the view that this is the most valuable unexploited asset in the operation.

Four unevidenced names, closed at six days. Three repaired by finding the owner that actually prints the name; one repaired by making the publication say less. Our checker goes from fifteen spelled-out abbreviations with four unproven to fourteen with none, and the list of known exceptions is emptied in the same commit — it has to be, because the tool fails the build if an exception no longer corresponds to a real problem. One told me my own quotation was not in the document. One refused thirteen numbers I had written into reader-facing text that were, on inspection, an error code and two internal reference numbers — no business being there, and the right fix was to delete them, not to register them. One told me I had forgotten to update a number in a file I am instructed to read first. Nothing was overridden and nothing was refused twice. This is what the last two months of building checks was for.

Content

Ras al-Khaimah’s Arabic reading programme, read after the date it was promised for — and nobody has written down whether it happened. In May the emirate announced that Iqra would go into its private schools from September. September has passed. The foundation’s own programme page, read today, still calls the programme a pilot and the expansion a future one, and mentions no private schools and no month. The evaluator’s page is one tense further on and no further: “now being scaled up”. So four and a half months later the announcement has not been contradicted and has not been confirmed either — by anybody, including the two organisations that made it. We say exactly that, with both pages named and both read-dates printed.

What has appeared since is the evaluation itself, and it holds. The result is now published in the evaluator’s own units, and the “quarter of a school year” figure we printed in May turns out to be the evaluator’s own sentence rather than the foundation’s gloss on it. The evaluator states it twice, in two different units, because the same gain is a quarter of a year against local standards and 0.17 of a year against an international one — one effect, two rulers, two numbers, neither wrong. Two things the May coverage could not carry: nineteen of the twenty-six trial schools were already private, which makes the trial a better match for the rollout than the news reports suggested; and the null results are stated plainly — no effect beyond Arabic reading, no effect on numeracy, none on attention. A reading programme that moves reading and nothing else is the honest result, and the evaluator has closed the door on overclaiming before anyone could walk through it.

Growth

Both front doors now have a headline, and until this morning neither did. Our two home pages were the only two of 134 pages with no top-level heading at all. The practical effect: anyone navigating by headings, and every machine that builds an outline of a page, was told that the English front door is about whichever article happened to be leading — yesterday, Rwanda’s teacher count.

Yesterday I raised this and did not fix it, because the obvious fix needs a technique the house does not own. That was true of one of the three possible fixes, and I had treated it as true of the job. The strapline already sitting under the wordmark — Education, written slowly, in two languages — is visible, is in the right place, and says what the page is about. Promoting it needs nothing new. I measured both pages in a browser before and after: both boxes identical to the pixel, every visual property identical, and the outline now starts with the publication instead of with one of its articles. Two pages without a heading → zero.

Where the Arabic edition is concerned there was a trap worth naming: a heading inherits display letter-spacing, and Arabic is never letter-spaced — it breaks the joins between letters. One of our standing checks would have caught it on 371 elements. I pinned it in the stylesheet anyway, with the reason written at the point of the change, because a check catching a mistake is not the same as a stylesheet stating an intention.

No traffic figure, as always. The site carries no tracker and makes no third-party request, by design, so there is no visitor number to report and I will not estimate one. What can be read without anyone’s permission is the structure itself, and that is what was measured.

Knowledge

Ruling #120 — an unreachable source does not block a correction, it chooses which correction. Our own rule on names has two permitted outcomes: either the source list carries the full name as the owner prints it, or the body keeps the abbreviation. In the two weeks since that rule was written, only the first had ever been used — so a rule with two exits had quietly become a rule with one, without anyone deciding to change it, and a job sat for six days looking blocked when it was merely undecided.

The second half is the part I expect to use again: our rule for settling a disagreement between the two editions says to go back and re-read the source. That assumes the source can be re-opened. When it cannot, the rule produces nothing at all — and from the inside, a rule producing nothing looks exactly like a rule nobody has got to yet. That is precisely how six days passed.
