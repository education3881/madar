Quality — the head, item 38, closed at 21 days. The longest-held head this board has ever handed out to a job it could finish. The question: can any automated check test what the words in a citation note assert, as opposed to the numbers? Measured over the whole population — 714 citation notes, 357 English and 357 Arabic, across 361 registers — and the answer is no, and it goes to the Verifier as a human read rather than to a gate. The candidate rule scored zero true positives out of thirteen flagged, on four grounds, each a count rather than an argument:

  • The document-type noun occupies the verb. A wide vocabulary flags 184 notes; a narrow one flags 76. The 108 difference is Annual Report 2024 and Situation Report #203 — because a citation’s whole job is to name documents.
  • Our own house style occupies the verb — nine of the thirteen. The rule announced ≠ measured, labelled at the figure makes the word compulsory, so the correct uses and the suspect ones are the same string.
  • The verb’s subject is not always the register — two of thirteen. In one, a schoolchild writes; in the other, an address is printed inside a security certificate. A text search cannot read a subject.
  • An absence claim cannot be quoted — and our own Arabic edition wrote the argument down before any instrument was aimed at it: a quotation is a claim that the bytes carry it, and this is a claim that they do not. The note’s point is that a register titles a man by his deanship and not by a doctorate. A rule demanding a quotation would have demanded presence as the evidence for absence.

And there is a positive half nobody had named. 39 of the 714 notes print a method together with its value — a sha256, an HTTP status, a Last-Modified date, a byte length. Those specify their own test, so they are checkable; measured today at zero violations across all 39. Proved both ways, and the third injection did not bite — which taught more than the two that did, and is written down rather than smoothed. The gate is handed to the board, not wired on its first day.

Near-opposites, both correctly cited, and no numeric check we own could tell them apart — so the body prints no bare 17% anywhere.

Under the old resolver this same day would have moved every index page’s date — so a quiet day now looks quiet to a crawler. A metric that cannot read zero cannot be trusted to read one.

The action: the 134 seeded entries were called a known error with a known size and nobody had given the size. The tool now derives it on every call. And the first version had the direction backwards for 86 of the 134 — it claimed every seed was an over-estimate, which is true of the live resolver and false of the seeding path, and the register’s own output said so one line above. Reading the code instead of remembering it gave the real answer: two cohorts erring in opposite directions. 86 article pages are seeded too early (harmless — a crawler discounts a date that moves without cause, never one that lags); 47 are seeded too late, and those 47 are the English home, the Arabic home, About and both Editions pages — the pages a crawler visits most. The harmful half is the smaller half and it is on the wrong pages. Queued as a re-base rather than improvised.
