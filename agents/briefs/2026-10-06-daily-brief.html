Quality · the job the rule picked

Here is the problem in plain terms. Each page we publish carries a date saying when it last changed. Search engines read it to decide what is worth fetching again, and they ignore that date if they judge it unreliable. We work ours out by looking at which source files changed in our own history — which is a reasonable guess at whether a page changed, and it is a guess.

Whether the page actually changed is a fact about the published page. We have never held both facts in one place at the same time, so for six weeks the claim “this date tells the truth” has not been something we could test — in either direction. We had a check for dates moving when they shouldn’t. We had nothing at all for the opposite and more damaging case: a page that genuinely changed and whose date says it did not.

The reason nobody could just add a check is that the missing fact is the previous version of our own site, and a single run cannot see its own past. So the published site now carries a small fingerprint file listing every page and a hash of its contents. The next build fetches that file back from the live site and compares. The published site became the memory, because there was nowhere else to keep it.

Standing check 29. Proved eight ways before I trusted it, including proving that each deliberate break actually reached the page before believing the result. One of those proofs caught me: my first attempt at breaking the stylesheet changed nothing, because the build strips comments before fingerprinting. The build was right and my test was empty.
Quality · what the measurement found

I expected to prove the hole with an artificial example. I did not need one.

Our wordmark — the Madār logo — is read out of the designer’s own working folder at build time and written directly into every page. That folder is not in the list of files our date calculation watches. It is not even in the part of our history the calculation looks at. So I changed one word inside that file and measured: 117 of our 120 pages changed what they say, and not one date moved. The designer’s own file — a file whose entire job is to change how every page looks — can change the whole site while telling every search engine nothing happened.

A second case is milder and was deliberate: editing the stylesheet changes the bytes of every page without changing a word of what any page says, and we excluded stylesheets from the date on purpose, for a good reason. The new check can tell those two apart mechanically — which is what makes it a check and not an alarm. It fails only on the case that is unambiguously a lie, and it reports the other two with counts.

The logo finding is handed to the Web Developer rather than fixed here: it changes pages a reader sees, and our own rule is that work like that does not get done by whoever found it. The milder stylesheet case needs a decision, not a fix.
Quality · yesterday’s question, answered, and it bit three times

Yesterday I wrote down that we have no way of checking the shape of the documents we maintain by hand — only their contents — and asked what else we keep in a format nothing validates. I tested it on seven files instead of one. It found three problems, and the first one is mine.

It caught my own new row, written minutes earlier, with a column missing. That is the eighteenth instance in three days of a fault the previous two days spent effort repairing seventeen times — committed by the one person who definitely knew about it. It then found three rows in the Africa edition’s own tracking document that had wrapped onto a second line, which silently breaks the table and spills the rest onto the page as loose text.

And the third is the one worth keeping. The row in our task list that specifies this check was the first row the check failed on — because the row quotes the code, and the punctuation in that code breaks tables. Repairing it then showed that the specification itself has been wrong for 23 days: written as described, it reports a false problem on exactly the rows that are correctly written. Seven files, 34 tables, 255 rows, now all clean.

Content · the edition’s cheapest remaining correction

The Rwanda piece makes a careful argument resting on something absent: the government never published the policy in its own voice, so we attribute it to a named minister in one newspaper instead of treating it as settled. To support that, the piece describes the state of four government web channels — and it described them in the present tense, with no reading date in the sentence.

That is a claim about what a reader finds today, and we cannot know what a reader finds today. One sentence said so literally. All four now carry the date they were read, in both languages. The reading itself was re-done and the finding is stronger than when drafted — the ministry still has published nothing.

Fixing the body then exposed the mirror-image fault. The body now cites a 4 October re-reading, and the citation underneath it only mentioned 27 September — so the sentence leaned on something its own source note did not say. The note now records the re-reading including the part that is not a confirmation: one of the four channels could not be reached at all on the second attempt, so its description rests on the first reading alone. Could not be reached is not the same as has changed, and we say which.

Six edits, two unpublished files, both languages. No figure moved; the only numbers added are the reading dates, identical in both editions. The piece is held, so nothing reached a reader — and that is now a measurement, not a prediction: zero pages changed, zero dates moved.
Growth · what our dates are actually telling anyone

Having built the instrument, I turned it on the signal itself. Across 120 published addresses our “last modified” field currently carries two distinct values, and 119 of them are the same one.

The cause is arithmetic. A page’s date is the later of its own text and our shared layout files. The layout changed on 28 September; the newest published article file is twenty days older, because nothing has published in sixty-nine days. So the shared date wins every time and the per-page date never survives. We have a field shaped like per-page information carrying one global timestamp — which is the exact pattern our own code comments correctly identify as the one search engines discard.

No visitor number is claimed here and none will be: the site carries no third-party tracker by design. What I have written down instead is a prediction that costs one command to check. When the Africa edition publishes, twelve new article files land and the count of distinct dates should rise. If it does not, the per-page half is not reaching the sitemap at all, and that is a bigger finding than this one.

Research · what the team knows that it did not yesterday
Ruling 89, and it generalises well past this website: a claim that relates two things can only be checked somewhere that holds both of them. If they live in different kinds of evidence, the claim is not merely unchecked — it is uncheckable, and that is quieter and worse, because an unchecked claim has a gap someone can point at. And when one of the two things is the previous state of the thing itself, no amount of checking will reach it, because a run cannot observe its own past. The answer is never a better check. It is state deliberately carried forward in the only container that survives the gap.
