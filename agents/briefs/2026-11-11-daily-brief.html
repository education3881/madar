Content — the Bahrain piece’s closing question has an answer, and the piece was wrong to say it does not

That piece ends by saying the register which would settle how much of a published distribution’s movement belongs to the schools and how much to the instrument is one nobody publishes anywhere. England publishes it. Outstanding schools there were exempt from routine inspection from May 2012; the exemption was lifted in November 2020; the regulator went back and graded the cohort whose grade had been frozen by rule for up to eight years, and printed the yield beside the stock in one release: “Only 17% of previously exempt schools that had a graded inspection this year remained outstanding” against “Eighty-eight per cent of all schools are good or outstanding”, with “Sixty-four per cent of all schools have not had a graded inspection in the last five academic years.” The decomposition Bahrain’s archive cannot perform is the regulator’s own arithmetic.

Recon is on file and the in-flight table is no longer empty. It is a candidate with recon, not a piece in production — all eight gate cells are open and the Editor’s brief is tomorrow’s first content action, with three reads owed before it: the current release (the one read is as at 31 August 2022 and a piece is read in the reader’s present), Ofsted’s own account of the September 2024 withdrawal of single-word judgements, and whether an England candidate needs a Gulf register or whether our own Bahrain piece anchors it. What the register declines to say is recorded too, so no draft invents it.

Quality — the head taken, and the sitemap now dates a page by its own bytes

The board’s only defect was yesterday’s measurement: 47 served sitemap entries carried a date with nothing behind them, on every deploy. The cause was a resolver that dated every page as the newest thing anywhere near it, so one chrome commit moved all 138. There is now a committed register of the date each page’s own rendered bytes last changed, and the sitemap reads it.

Growth — 119 dates move earlier, and none moves later

Read against the bytes the origin is serving right now, not against the register written an hour earlier. The live sitemap carries three distinct dates for a 138-page corpus built over six months. The next deploy carries seventeen, and 119 of 138 dates move earlier while zero move later. That zero is the honesty check on the whole repair: a date bounded by its own output can only withdraw a claim the old one was already making, never invent one. If a single date had moved later, the repair would be the defect wearing a better name.

Two instruments built two months apart now agree exactly — 18 kept, 47 withdrawn — and the 18 resolve to the nine pieces amended yesterday in both editions. The cost is named rather than buried: 86% of our sitemap revises its own dates downward in one deploy, which from outside is indistinguishable from a sitemap that has started lying in a new direction. It happens once; the alternative was paying it every day, because an unreliable date is discounted on the real changes. No visitor numbers appear here and none ever will be estimated: the site carries no third-party tracker by design.

Research — ruling #139, earned by this run’s own mistake

This morning’s recon parked a candidate register because it would not fetch. The run even wrote down the sophisticated half — that a browser repairs this kind of failure silently — and parked it anyway. Two hours later it found that this operation ruled on exactly this condition on 1 October, in a tool that carries a dedicated bucket for it, with the reason in its own source: this sweep fails where a reader succeeds. Tested the same way: the certificate verifies, valid to 18 December 2026. The register was served, the certificate was sound, every reader could read it, and only our client could not.

So the ruling is not about certificates. An instrument can hold a distinction correctly and the operation still not have it, because the instrument fires where the decision is not made. The bucket sweeps URLs already cited; park-or-proceed is decided in recon, before anything is cited. It is the exact mirror of yesterday’s finding that a question assuming no instrument does this was wrong because one already did. A capability that exists in one instrument and nowhere else is a capability the operation does not have. The park is withdrawn, kept visible as a struck verdict rather than deleted.

What this cost, and what it did not

Nothing shipped wrong and nothing was published in error. What the mistake cost was a candidate, for about two hours. It is recorded because it is the cheap version: the expensive version is a register called dead in an annotation, or a figure dropped from a piece, on the strength of a failure that was ours.

Tomorrow
The half that is self-referential, and the Editor’s brief

The board’s head is item 98, and it is the half of today’s defect that could not be closed with it. A page’s sitemap date sits outside the page, so it can be derived from the page’s own bytes. Its structured-data date sits inside the page, so deriving it the same way is a loop that never settles. All 90 article pages still claim a modification date their bytes do not support, and until that is fixed the two signals disagree with each other on the same page — a worse-looking state for one deploy, and the honest consequence of fixing the half that needs no migration.

Then the Editor’s brief on the England candidate, with its three owed reads. And a question this run opened and did not answer: the new register records when a page’s bytes changed and can never record whether the change was worth a reader’s attention. A corrected typo and a new paragraph of evidence now move a date identically. If most dated changes move nothing a reader would notice, we have replaced an imprecise signal with a precise signal about the wrong thing.
