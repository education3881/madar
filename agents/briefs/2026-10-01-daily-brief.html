Manager → Founder

We rehearsed publishing Edition 05 for the first time. It would have failed — and the thing that would have broken is one of our own safety checks, not the publication.

All six pieces are written, translated, approved and independently checked. They publish together, in one go, on one day. This morning we finally asked the obvious question: has that combined state ever actually been built and tested? It had not. Twenty-two of our twenty-five automatic checks are pointed at the published site, and the few that do look at the unpublished files read only their settings, never the pages they will become — so every check that examines a real page would have met the new edition for the first time on publication day. So we flipped all twelve files on, built the whole site, and ran everything. It went red — a check that orders the browse pages was reading only the first 8,000 characters of each file, and in exactly the six pieces whose sourcing is most detailed, the “ready to publish” marker sits past that cut-off. Fixed, re-run, green. Separately, the oldest outstanding review on the board is closed with two corrections, and a government website we have described wrongly three times in three weeks turns out to have been fine the whole time. Ten days to the 11 October gate.

Today

A publication rehearsal that failed, a government website we had libelled, and the only English article of forty-four that did not link to its own Arabic twin

Thursday 1 October 2026 · Asia/Dubai
Quality · the publication rehearsal, and what it caught
Edition 05 publishes as a single commit: twelve files switch from held to live at once. That combined state had never been built. Today it was — every file switched on, the whole site rebuilt, all twenty-five gating checks run, then everything put back exactly as it was.
The first attempt failed in forty places. The check that keeps our browse pages in a stable, predictable order reads each article’s header to work out what the page should show — and it was reading only the first 8,000 characters. The “approved” marker is the last line of that header, and the citations above it are long, so the cut-off selected almost perfectly for our most heavily sourced work. Ten files are over the limit and every one of them is an Edition 05 file. The check has been green every day only because those ten are, in fact, still held — the bug and the reality happened to agree. Publication day is precisely when they stop agreeing. Fixed to read the whole header; rehearsal re-run; green, 135 pages, every check passing.
Content · the oldest review on the board is closed
The Zambia piece — written 25 August, checked 8 September — had three loose ends owed before publication. All three are closed, and two real errors were found and fixed in both languages.
First: we wrote that UNESCO’s Africa institute “puts” Zambian learning poverty at 99 per cent. Its own page says the figure is estimated by the World Bank, UNESCO and other organizations — it is passing the number on, not producing it. The uncomfortable detail: our own citation already did this correctly for four other figures in the same bracket and not for this one. Second: we printed a score of 358 with no scale, where the source prints it as 358 on a scale where 625 is advanced attainment and 300 the lowest — a number on a scale, without its scale, in a piece whose argument is that a figure without its ruler is an opinion. Both fixed. The two fixes cost 24 words against a 2,300 limit; three were handed back in the same sentences that spent them, so the piece sits at 2,298 with no exemption.
Quality · the website we had described wrongly, three times
Four of Zambia’s sources live on the Zambian parliament’s website. We have recorded that site as unreachable (10 Sept), then refusing connections (16 Sept), then serving behind an invalid certificate (30 Sept). All three were wrong.
Measured properly today: the certificate is valid until 10 December 2026. The site simply doesn’t send one of the two certificates browsers need, and publishes the missing one at an address printed inside the first. Chrome, Edge and Safari fetch it and show the reader nothing unusual; our scripts don’t, so they fail where a reader succeeds. The costly part is not the wrong label — it is the advice attached to it. Our own tool told us to write “a reader meets a browser security warning, so say so.” Following that would have put a false sentence about the reader into twelve citations. The tool now distinguishes the two cases and says measure before writing anything.
Growth · the article that did not link to its own Arabic twin
Answering yesterday’s question produced a new automatic check, and on its first run it found that the Egypt article — one of forty-four — declared no Arabic version. Its Arabic twin pointed at it; it pointed at nothing back.
We measured what that would have cost rather than assuming: with the field missing, the English page serves no language alternates at all (a normal page serves three), no translation link in its structured data, and no visible link to its Arabic version. Arabic reach is this publication’s one genuine distribution advantage, and the search-facing half of that pair would have shipped without it. Nothing could have caught this, because every link check we own reads the published site, and this piece is not published yet. Fixed, and now gated.
Research · three rules written
80 — how a website behaves is something a particular program observed at a particular moment, not a fact about the website; and a check’s advice is writing that will end up in print, so it has to be proved like everything else. 81 — a check that looks at what is already published is blind to what is about to be published, which makes a simultaneous launch an unguarded event. 82 — a small, sharp one from inside our own testing: the most valuable test of a check is to re-break the thing you just fixed, and that test is indistinguishable from doing nothing if you ask the version-control system whether the file changed.
Rule 82 was earned the hard way. Our test harness threw away the best test of the day for exactly that reason, then “restored” the file by deleting the morning’s fix — after which four more tests ran against a quietly broken copy, each reporting two problems instead of one, with every exit code saying the same thing. Counting the findings rather than trusting the exit code is what separated them.

A search pattern of mine matched inside the word madar and told me a test had failed when it had worked — the first conclusion about the Egypt problem was briefly the opposite of the truth. Yesterday’s diagnosis of a build warning named one cache folder when there are two. The repository’s own instruction file has said “twelve checks” for two days while listing eleven. And the register of rules has a fifth place a count lives that our rule about counts does not mention — found, corrected, and carried to Sunday’s review as a proposal rather than quietly changed.
