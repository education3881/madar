Today

A rule we attributed to four companies that none of them publishes, and a citation caught crediting the wrong author

2026-10-02 · all five standing functions · build clean, nothing published
Quality · the question yesterday left, tested before anything new

Every article page carries a hidden instruction telling Facebook, X, LinkedIn and Slack which picture to show when someone shares the link. In August we found that ours had been pointing at a file format none of them can display — eighty-three days of shares that carried no picture at all, while every check we had said the file was fine. We fixed it and wrote a check to keep it fixed. That check contains a list of the formats those four companies accept.

Yesterday’s run asked a question about that list: when did we last check it against the real world rather than against our own file? The answer was August. So today I opened all four companies’ own documentation and read it.

Not one of the four publishes such a list. Facebook’s page gives sizes and a file-size limit and never names a format. The Open Graph standard itself names none. Slack’s documentation says, in its own words, that it looks for “common OpenGraph and X Card metadata” — it delegates to X. And LinkedIn’s list of formats turns out to belong to a different product entirely: paid advertisements, not shared links.

The one company that ever published it was X, and X now charges for access to that page. Five different routes to it: two return “402 Payment Required”, two return 404, one redirects to a homepage. So the rule we have been enforcing since August is not wrong — it is unverifiable, and our file recorded those two states identically.

Ruling 83. The list is now built from a dated table recording who vouched for each format and when we last read them say so, and it prints on every single build. One format, WEBP, is vouched for by nobody we can currently read — proved by deliberately feeding one in and confirming our check stays silent. It has never caused a problem only because all 121 of our share pictures are PNGs. Nothing was narrowed: a format missing from a page we cannot open is not a format we have evidence against, and inventing a stricter rule from an unreadable source is exactly what this publication refuses to let its sources do.
Quality · why a stale list is worse than a stale ban
This is worth one more sentence because it is the general lesson. A list of things we forbid, gone out of date, rejects something harmless — our build goes red, we look, we fix it. A list of things we allow, gone out of date, waves through something broken, our build stays green, and the failure happens weeks later on somebody else’s computer to a reader we will never see. That is precisely the eighty-three-day defect, with our own safety list as the mechanism instead of our ignorance.
One belief came back confirmed and is now citable rather than merely correct: Arabic pages declare their language as ar_AR, which looks like an error, because AR is the international code for Argentina. Meta’s own documentation names it explicitly as a deliberate exception. A dating pass is not only a hunt for rot.
Content · the second of five final reviews is closed

The Africa edition is six finished articles in two languages, held back until every one is verified. Five final reviews stood owed; Zambia’s closed yesterday. Sierra Leone’s closed today. All eight of its sources answer correctly — the first article in the edition to come back completely clean, and the one whose sources have given us the most trouble.

One source answered through a redirect, and that was the whole finding. We cite a public code repository where one of the five delivery organisations published the analysis behind its own disappointing result. Our citation credited it to Save the Children UK. The repository’s own front page says it is “background analysis for an SCUK published blog”, posted on a staff member’s personal account. The organisation published the blog post; an individual published the analysis.

Where the error came from is the part worth keeping. Nothing was fabricated and no research failed. The web address used to contain the letters “SCUK”, and we read the organisation’s name off the address instead of off the document. Then the account was renamed and the letters vanished. The rename did not create the mistake — it removed what was hiding it.

Ruling 84: an address is not an attribution. Corrected in both languages, with the article’s text untouched — 1,592 English words and 1,343 Arabic, unchanged, no figure moved. And the check we shipped yesterday passed this, correctly: it verifies that both languages agree, and both languages agreed on the same error. Agreement is not correctness.
Content · what the review confirmed, and the one thing it could not
Both earlier corrections hold. We quote the funder’s own cautious sentence about its flagship programme, and it still reads on their page exactly as we print it. But a second typo that the September review cited as evidence has since been corrected on that page — which proves the page is actively edited, and therefore that quoting it is a reading taken on a particular day rather than a permanent fact. Both citations now say so, with both dates.

Left open, deliberately: a better source exists for one citation — a full report rather than a blog post — and its page loads but its text cannot be read by any tool available here. A document we cannot read cannot outrank one we can. Not applied, not dropped, and nothing is broken by waiting.
Growth · the publication read as a map for the first time

Every article carries a short list of related pieces. Last week’s work connected the six unpublished Africa articles to each other, and named what it could not see: nothing knows whether an older article is linked to by nothing at all. A reader who arrives from a search engine on an unlinked page has no path deeper into the publication.

Measured today: there are none. All 38 published articles are pointed at by at least one other article, in both languages — 98 connections each side, and the English and Arabic maps are structurally identical. So no new links are being written, and that is the finding rather than a shrug: inventing twelve editorial claims to satisfy a measurement would have been the wrong move.

Two numbers worth keeping. Thirteen of the 38 are held in by exactly one link — each one article away from being stranded. And the six held articles point at fifteen already-published pieces, so publishing the edition strengthens the existing library, not just the new arrivals.
Growth · and I got it wrong twice before I got it right
The first answer my own script gave was “twelve of 37 articles are stranded.” It was nonsense — a one-character flaw meant it read only the last entry of every list. The clue was sitting in the output and I read past it: 44 connections across 44 articles, exactly one each. The second answer said we have 37 published articles; we have 38. That one found a stranger bug: a single Dutch government web address contains three hyphens in a row, which my script mistook for the end of the article’s metadata, throwing away the published-or-not flag.
That is yesterday’s defect exactly, recreated by me within twenty-four hours in a different way — yesterday a length limit, today a punctuation collision, both burying the same flag. So I checked every shipped check against it. None has it. Three independent counts all say 38. The bug was in today’s throwaway script and nowhere else — and the only reason I know that is that two instruments were made to disagree.
Restraint · a check specified and deliberately not shipped
The obvious next move is a permanent check that no article is ever left unlinked. I have written its specification and its test plan and have not shipped it, for three stated reasons: it would find nothing today, so it proves only that it can count; the edition’s gate is nine days away and yesterday’s lesson about shipping checks late in a session is one day old; and we now have 28 of these, which is enough that a new one should arrive with a defect behind it. The trigger that should fire it is written down.
Research · two rulings, and the counting rule got sharper by being run twice
Rulings 83 and 84 filed. The register now stands at 84, reconciled by counting its rows rather than trusting yesterday’s number. Our rule says a ruling’s number must be updated in four places in the same commit; yesterday found a fifth and passed it to the weekly review as a proposal. I applied it instead — a place we already know about and still leave stale is not a gap in the rule, it is the rule being declined. And running the rule twice in one day taught it something new: ruling 83 needed five updates and ruling 84 needed four, because the fifth only exists for some categories. No previous run had reason to notice.
