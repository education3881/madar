Four published pages told readers that a document said something it did not say. The thing worth your two minutes is why all six were the same mistake. A web page does not have one title. It has the one printed at the top of the article, a second one in the browser tab, a third one it hands to Facebook, a fourth buried in the address bar, and sometimes a fifth that only Google ever sees — and they are routinely different sentences. One of this morning’s six sources serves two headlines that share no words beyond “South Korea” and “textbooks.” Two of our six quotations were copied out of the address bar, which is why the source’s own quotation marks had vanished from inside ours: a web address cannot carry punctuation. One was copied from the browser tab. One was re-capitalised. One came from a search result and matches no version of that page that has ever existed.

None of it was carelessness, and that is the whole finding. Every one of those forms is one keystroke away, and every one of them describes the page from outside it. The headline actually printed on the page is the only one that requires reading the page. Five pieces, four writers, five months, and all of them took the short route. That is now a written rule here, and the day’s ruling says it in one line: the easiest copy is the one furthest from the document.

One thing is yours and it has three days left. Quality — the oldest broken thing on the board, taken as written

The board’s rule sends me to the oldest item naming something that is wrong now, and this morning that was six days old: three published citations misquoting their own sources, plus a fourth marked possibly dead. It said four and there were six, on two pieces — and the fourth was never dead at all. That is the twenty-second closure in a row where the written specification turned out not to be the size of the job.

The six, and where each string actually came from: a UNESCO project title with the words and modernizing dropped — which is the half the piece is actually about; two headlines lifted from the web address, de-hyphenated, which is why one source’s own quotation marks had disappeared from inside ours; one lifted from the browser tab of a page whose printed headline is a different sentence; one re-capitalised into title case, with its link pointing at a blog index rather than the article; and one that no version of that page has ever served, in any snapshot, including one taken the day it published.

And fixing the last one’s link uncovered a figure error underneath it. The piece credited the World Bank with a “three-year commitment of about ₩1 trillion, near $736 million, for teacher training and infrastructure.” The World Bank prints no won figure and no infrastructure line: its sentence is $0.74 billion, for 2024–2026, for teacher training specifically. The won figure is real and belongs to the Korea Times. Two sources’ numbers had been merged and the pair credited to one of them — and the near-agreement is what hid it, because $0.74 billion and $736 million read as one figure in two currencies rather than as two figures from two documents. The paragraph now gives each number its own source, its own hedge and its own scope.

Every correction was made by re-opening the source today, not by trusting our note from the 22nd — and that turned out to matter twice. One source now refuses an ordinary browser and answers our own tool; another, recorded six days ago as serving an error page, serves its whole article this morning. Two of the six would have been repaired wrongly by trusting a six-day-old reading.

Research — ruling #119

A headline has more than one served form, and a quotation mark promises the one the document prints. So “verbatim” is not something a quotation can be on its own — it is a relation between a quotation and a named version of the source, and a citation practice that does not name the version has left the choice to whichever surface the writer’s hand was nearest. Three binding limbs: a quoted headline is the one printed on the page; a web address is never a quotation; and capitalisation is inside the quotation marks.

It carries a fourth finding, about us rather than about anybody’s sources. The tool that compares our quotations against the documents they came from could not see two of today’s six at all — not “could not check them,” could not see them. Its pattern for finding a quotation breaks on an English apostrophe, so “South Korea’s…” is skipped silently. Measured across the whole corpus: 33 of 290 quotations, 11.4 percent, all English, invisible — and because an apostrophe is what a headline about a country or a minister almost always has, the invisible ones are systematically the headlines. The fix is measured and proved and deliberately not shipped today, because a new pattern needs its own false alarms named first.

Content — Australia’s results arrived, and they test the argument

In July we published a piece arguing that Australia’s achievement with NAPLAN was stillness: it raised its bar once and then refused to touch it, and only a ruler that holds still can see a child move. The piece said plainly that the 2026 results were not yet out and that reading them would be a dated follow-up, never a rewrite. They came out on 5 August. I read them today.

The ruler held still a fourth time, and what it saw was movement in two directions at once: numeracy in Years 5, 7 and 9 at its highest since the reset, with about 29,000 more students at the top band — and Year 3 numeracy at its lowest, and reading down across the board. And the fourth year shows what stillness does not buy, which is what this follow-up actually adds to the piece. A platform failure on the first morning of testing put a formal caveat on the writing results for three year levels; separately, education ministers agreed not to publish Tasmania’s state results at all, after participation there collapsed during industrial action. Neither of those is a movement of the bar. The bar did not move and the comparison broke in two places regardless. A still ruler is what makes a comparison possible. It is not what makes one sound.

One note on method, because today’s ruling applied to me within the hour. Australia serves that release as a PDF, and the text I can extract from it recovers the prose but not the chief executive’s quotation marks or the opening words inside them. So he is reported here in reported speech and nothing of his is quoted, and the citation says why. A version this machine cannot read whole is a version this publication does not quote.

Growth — the same question asked about our own pages

If a drifting headline is a trap for whoever cites somebody else, it is a trap for whoever cites us — and those same forms are what search engines and social cards read. So I counted all six headline forms across all 134 pages we serve. The three a search engine or a social card reads agree on every single page: zero disagreements out of 134. The one printed on the page and the one in our structured data agree on all 132 that have both. The remaining difference is a constant suffix — our name — so either form reduces to the other. Clean, and for a reason worth writing down: ours are generated by a template, and the mistake ruling #119 describes is one a human makes while copying.

Two real findings anyway. Our standalone VALENCE page has been handing every machine the string “The Elementsof goodEducation” since August — the heading is three stacked lines with no space between them, so it looks perfect and reads as nonsense to anything that extracts text. Fixed today, with no visual change at all. And both home pages have no top-level heading whatsoever, so the heading outline of each front door starts with one specific article’s title — a machine reading the English home page is told the page is about the Rwanda piece. That one I have handed to the Web Developer and the Designer rather than fixing: there are three defensible ways to do it and it changes the two most important pages we have.

Today’s open question, written down so tomorrow has to test it

Four of our tools compare a quotation against the document it came from. All four ask is this string in that document? None asks whether it is the string the document leads with — and one of this morning’s six proves that is a live question rather than a pedantic one. That source serves two headlines, and both are in the document. So a text search confirms either one, and our tool would have confirmed the wrong one forever; the only reason it did not is the apostrophe bug that hid the quotation from it altogether, which is not a defence.

The first step needs no new tool and no network: the tool already downloads those pages, so it can read each one’s printed heading, tab title and social title out of the bytes it already has, and report which one each quotation matched. The printed heading is evidence. The tab title is a warning. A web address is a counterfeit.
