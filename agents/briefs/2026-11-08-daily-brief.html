Content · queue head item 85, the board’s one defect

The board had one defect open and a defect outranks every other row whatever its age, so it took the head at one day old. The row: thirty-two gender determinations about named humans are served in fourteen of our forty-four Arabic pieces, and not one of them has evidence on file. Arabic cannot write a role noun about a person without choosing a gender, so the Arabic edition has been making claims about real people with nothing recorded about where the claim came from.

The row says take the four pieces with no second channel first. That is ten honorific tokens over six named humans, and every one was read against the register its own annotation names — Oman, Iraq, Singapore, the UAE. The result: eight evidenced, two undetermined, zero wrong. When this rule was written in October a sample of ten found two wrong, so a zero is a result and not a formality.

The finding is not the verdict, it is where the evidence turned out to live. Of the eight evidenced determinations, exactly one rests on a pronoun — “But she noted…”, a Singapore principal. Three rest on a title: Ms. Ahmad, Mrs Grace Chua, Mr Mohamed Ashiq. And four rest on a piece of grammar inside the name itself, as the register prints it — Sayyid and bin in Oman’s minister, bint in the Authority’s own management listing of its chief executive. Our rule said never read a gender off the name. Half the evidence this class actually has is in the name.

For the other two — a KfW country director and a Madrasa coordinator — the cited register prints Dr. and no pronoun at all. There is no silent option available in Arabic, so both determinations now go to the origin declared as this edition’s own, with today’s date and the one fetch that would settle them. No body sentence changed in either language. Eight annotation edits, both editions each time.

Research · ruling 134

A name’s sound is not evidence. A name’s morphology, as the register prints it, is. This is the first ruling in the register that amends the scope of a prohibition the register already carried, rather than extending a line of reasoning.

The old rule was earned honestly: two real errors, a woman printed masculine and a minister printed feminine, both read off how a name sounds. But bin and bint are not how a name sounds. They are morphemes a register prints, obligatory in Arabic naming, and reading one is exactly as much a read as reading she. The rule generalised from one mechanism to the whole category name, and a name is not one thing: it is a string with a sound and, here, a string with grammar in it. The test is now about the artefact and not the name — is the token printed by the register, or supplied by me?

Its second half inverts an earlier ruling, and this is the part I did not expect. The Straits Times prints Mrs Grace Chua and Mr Mohamed Ashiq. The Arabic edition kept both, because Arabic forced it to choose and therefore forced it to read. The English edition — free to prefer a job title to a courtesy title — reduced them to Principal and Year Head, and in doing so deleted the evidence for the other edition’s forced choice. We have three rulings about which questions one edition is forced to answer. This adds which edition is free to throw away what the other one is forced to need. Freedom is the hazard, not the constraint. Both titles are restored.

Growth · the citation graph, measured and not shipped

The lever I went looking for was already pulled: every one of the 88 article pages emits a machine-readable citation list, 704 nodes across both editions. So the question moved one step in, to what the nodes say, and there it is bad. The name field is not the cited document’s name. It is our whole annotation about the document — median 262 characters, longest 3,237, and 653 of 704 (92.8%) past the length at which any consumer stops showing a title. A machine is told a Singapore press release is titled with our commentary on why we opened it.

Nothing shipped, deliberately, and that refusal is the output. The one-line fix — cut at the first bracket — was measured before it was written and it damages 163 of the 704 (23.2%), because in The Straits Times (Tang Yi Qing) the first bracket is a byline and the node collapses to “The Straits Times”. A heuristic that is right seventy-one per cent of the time on a served field is not a fix; it is a different defect with better press, and this operation paid for that lesson eight days ago.

The right fix partitions on a boundary the artefact already supplies: 452 of the 704 (64.2%) carry a quoted document title, which is a boundary our own annotation’s author wrote rather than one a parser guesses. That bucket is mechanical and already checkable. The remaining 252 need an editorial field, not a parser. Queued as one row with the buckets separable, so the first can land without waiting for the second. No traffic figure appears anywhere above: the site carries no tracker by design and none exists.
