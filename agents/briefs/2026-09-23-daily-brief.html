Manager → Founder

We printed the day a crawler saw a page as the day an organisation published it.

The Verifier read the Egypt pair today against its own registers, independently of yesterday’s Editor, and found the same mistake three times. The piece kept taking a date that belongs to a document and attaching it to the event the document describes.

The clearest of the three: we wrote that a rights organisation published its objection to Egypt’s education bill on 21 July 2025. That is not its publication date. It is the timestamp in the Internet Archive URL — the moment a crawler happened to look at the page. The statement’s own dateline, sitting under its headline the whole time, reads 7 July. Two weeks out, in a sentence whose entire job is to establish that the objection came before the law.

The reason this is worth your attention rather than a shrug: every check we own passed. We have a rule, ten days old, that the citation and the sentence must say the same thing about a source — and they did. The annotation says 21 July. The sentence says 21 July. The digits matched and the meanings did not. That is a new shape, it is now ruling 62, and it is the first one our own instrument for this family cannot see.

Ruling 62 · a date that matches is not a date that agrees
Three sentences, one mistake. The rights organisation’s objection dated to the archive crawl rather than its own dateline. The minister’s London remarks dated to 18 May 2026 — which is the newspaper’s publication date; the register names the forum, the session and its title and gives no date for the speech at all. And the admissions arithmetic dated to a government portal’s own article date, on a page that says the ministry “has announced” and dates nothing.
What makes this a ruling rather than three slips is the three it got right. The piece makes this move six times. Two of the registers say the announcement happened “today” — so the transfer is licensed, and the piece is correct. And one sentence does it perfectly: “Daily News Egypt reported on 16 August 2026 that…” The writer knew the right construction, used it once, and reached for the register’s own day the other five times. That is a habit with no rule attached to it, which is exactly what a ruling is for. All three fixed by dating the account: “In an account published on 18 May 2026…”
And then we swept for it, rather than assuming it was contained
