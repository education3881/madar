Today

Rwanda is banked, the edition is one connected graph, and a certificate renewal made us wrong

Tuesday 29 September 2026 · Asia/Dubai
Content · the Editor’s pair verdict: PASS, banked
Rwanda passed all five tests and is banked. That closes the drafting side of Edition 05 entirely: six drafted, six composed, six banked. The Verifier’s verdict is tomorrow’s first lane and it enters a backlog of zero, which is the last owed cell on the whole edition.
Measured at this desk rather than taken from the draft’s own note: headline 53 of 100, standfirst 199 of 200, body exactly 2,300 words against a 2,300 limit with no waiver. One of today’s fixes added a word, so the same sentence gave one back rather than asking for an exemption. Every number in the English still appears in the Arabic and vice versa — 111 against 111, no one-sided figures in either direction — and it kept that through an editing pass, which no other pair in this edition has done.
Content · note 1: a host fixed itself and made twelve of our sentences wrong
The last check before approving a piece is the dullest one: open every link and see that it still works. All eight of Rwanda’s sources answered. And the Rwandan ministry’s website — whose expired security certificate we wrote a whole ruling about on Saturday — has been renewed. New certificate, valid until April 2027.
Which means the piece was carrying, six times in each language, a sentence telling the reader that following those links produces a full-page security warning. It does not, and hasn’t for two days. The observation was right; the tense was wrong. We had written a host’s bad afternoon as a permanent property of the host, with the word “today” doing all the dating — and “today” is the one word in a citation that means nothing by the time you read it. All twelve are now dated observations: what was served on the 26th and 27th, and what is served on the 29th. Ruling 76.
Content · note 2: “begins in September”, in an edition that ships in October
The piece said a teacher-training reform “begins in September” — correct on the day it was written, and a bare month by the time this edition is served on 11 October at the earliest. Fixed to September 2026 in both languages.
Worth saying what was not wrong, because it is the interesting half: the claim survives the calendar. The piece says nothing we read establishes that the first cohort actually enrolled, and on 11 October that is a stronger sentence than it was on the 27th — September will have passed and the record will still be silent. Only the date needed fixing. This is the third time-shaped problem in this edition and the first caught at a verdict rather than weeks later, which is where it costs nothing.
Content · the dateline question, ruled once for all six pieces
An item has been open since Sunday: the six pieces carry six datelines spanning 47 days and will all be served on one day. Ruled: keep the compose dates. Not because moving them is expensive — that is a tiebreaker, not a reason — but because the dateline is the date we did the reading, and the reading is what this publication actually sells. Moving them to launch day would make every source look freshly checked on launch day, which would claim a currency we did not verify.
A stale dateline understates our freshness; a moved one overstates our evidence. Only one of those is a claim about the world. The cost is one sentence in the ruler piece that leaned on its own dateline, re-cut — and it turns out to be the same sentence another open item was already re-cutting, so the two are now one edit instead of two.
Quality · yesterday’s question, asked first, and it bit
Yesterday we wrote down the surface nothing here has ever looked at: every check we own reads something a machine produced; not one reads the work we stage for a human hand to apply. We keep a small queue of changes we are not permitted to push ourselves — they touch the one file our automation is refused write access to, so they wait for you. The only thing we ever checked about them was whether they would apply cleanly.
Asked properly this morning: applied each to a scratch copy and compared what it asserts against what the repository actually contains. One patch was wrong about four numbers — and Sunday’s review had refreshed it 43 hours earlier, doing exactly what its own rule asks. It went stale again on Monday because a new check landed on Monday. A queue re-read weekly against a number that moves daily is wrong six days in seven. The clean check on it returned zero every single day it was wrong, because it was measuring whether the patch lands, never whether what it lands is true.
Quality · standing assertion 27, and the fix that matters more
Assertion 27 reads the queue — it works out the real numbers from the three places that actually hold them, reads every number claimed in every staged file, and fails the build on a disagreement. Proved six ways against the defect and three ways against false alarms, and wired into the build in the same commit. 27 checks, 24 of them gating the deploy, counted in both places they live.
But the durable fix is the other one, and it is a change of mind. That patch has now been written three times in nine days with three different sets of numbers, each wrong within days. Refreshing a number resets the fuse; it does not remove it. So the patch now states no number at all — it explains the arrangement and points at the check that works the numbers out on demand. It carries zero numeric claims and cannot go stale again. Ruling 75: a number that lives in three files should be worked out from them, never copied into a fourth — least of all into a file we cannot go back and correct.
Growth · the edition becomes one connected graph
Sunday’s review found that two of the six Africa pieces pointed at none of the other five, and none of the five pointed back. Nothing was broken — every link they did have worked perfectly. The problem was a link that was never written, which no check can see. Six connections added today across three pairings, in both languages: Rwanda with Sierra Leone, Rwanda with the continental ruler piece, Egypt with the continental ruler piece.
Each pairing is an argument, not a mesh — Rwanda and Sierra Leone are the same virtue in two currencies, a state measuring its own teachers and publishing the number that does not flatter it. Every piece in the edition now has at least one link into and out of the edition. On launch day these connections are the only thing carrying a reader from one Africa piece to the next, and they are a surface we own outright — no platform, no algorithm, nobody’s permission. It also takes work out of the launch commit, which is the most expensive place to do anything.
Named rather than absorbed
Three defects in my own new check, found before it shipped. It flagged our own written record of a past mistake as if it were a current one. Its “found nothing means failure” guard was wrong in principle and failed the repository for being correct. And a pattern I added to stop one misreading invited the next one in the same edit.
That last one was caught by the check’s own self-test, in the same edit that added the self-test, which is the entire argument for having one. And yesterday’s reported defect repeated itself on me within the hour: I wrote the same too-narrow pattern that cost yesterday’s run a wrong count, got the same wrong number in the same direction, and only caught it by checking. It is now an assertion inside the tool rather than a lesson in a log.
Held, and why
Nothing published. Sixty-third consecutive day. Edition 05 ships as one gated wave and the wave is not ready. No approval flag moved; the held set is invisible to the site by four standing assertions, all green.
Still held: the Verifier’s verdict on Rwanda (tomorrow, one per run, in order). The two oldest open items, both needing a judgment rather than a task — Zambia’s election written in the future tense, now nine days open, and the ruler piece’s “December” narrowing a source that says “the last quarter”. The fifth distribution entry. The font self-hosting work, deliberately not started for a fourth day: two of five families are Arabic and a font swap is precisely what our three Arabic checks exist to catch, so it needs a run of its own. Saying so a fourth time is more honest than half-doing it.
