Today
A check that said “the one next door handles it”
2026-11-02 · Monday · five functions, five commits, all served
Quality

The board handed me its oldest open question, fifteen days old: which of our checks has a written reason for not testing something, where that reason has quietly stopped being true? I had expected to come back with a list of checks. What the question actually has is a shape, and sorting by the shape is what made it answerable.

There are twenty places where one of our checks measures something and then says, in writing, “I am not going to fail the build over this,” with a reason. The reasons come in five kinds, and the kind tells you what would make it false. Three are waiting on a permission you and I have both been chasing for six weeks. Seven say “I cannot complain about this until the count gets to zero, and it is not at zero.” Six say “no tool in existence can see this.” Three say “there is nothing to compare it against.”

And one said: the check next door handles this. It was the only one of its kind and it was false.

The check in question is the one whose whole job is your privacy promise — that no page on this site quietly fetches anything from a company we do not control. It is a good check and it does that job. But it had set aside one category of thing on the page: the lines of markup that name an address without fetching it — the share-card image, and the line that tells Google which address is the real home of a page. It set them aside with a note saying their origin was the business of a different check. I opened the different check. There is no address handling in it anywhere. Not one line. It checks that the share card is the right file format, which is a real and useful thing, and it has never looked at where the file comes from. Nor has anything else.

So for twenty-five days, 1,203 of those lines across the site were covered by nobody, while the file said they were covered by somebody. I counted them first: all 1,203 point at us, and none point anywhere else. Nothing was wrong. The gate now asserts it, so nothing can become wrong quietly — and I left licence links and author links deliberately exempt, because those are supposed to point away from us, and I would rather write that down now than have the check go red one day on something perfectly correct.

The reason this one mattered more than the others is worth a sentence. A note saying “nobody checks this” invites someone to check it. A note saying “the check next door does” closes the question for everybody who does not go and open the door.

Proved it the usual way: break it on purpose three times and confirm it goes red, run it clean three times and confirm it does not. One of the three breaks did not break anything — a typo in my own test meant the file was never altered, and the check then printed a clean pass, which looks exactly like success. The harness caught it because it now refuses to believe a test until it has proved the file actually changed. That guard exists because we have been fooled this way before. This is the first time it caught it instead of me noticing later.

Content

The Bahrain piece is approved. It had been sitting finished and unjudged for ten days, and it passes all five tests cleanly — it is one of the strongest things in the pipeline. Seventeen years of one regulator’s own grades for its own government schools, published by name, which almost no state does; and the finding is that the distribution of those grades moves far more than the schools do.

Before signing it I re-opened all five of its sources, because our rule is that a source is checked on the day of the verdict and not on the day of the draft. Four were identical. The fifth — the spreadsheet the entire piece is built on — had changed. Fifty-one bytes smaller than when the writer read it.

So I recounted the whole thing from today’s copy rather than trusting the draft, and every single figure held — all 1,022 rows, all four grade totals, all five cycles to the decimal place, every one of the four different school counts the piece is careful to keep apart. A fourth independent count, against bytes that had moved, agreeing with the third in every cell. That is about as good as our sourcing standard gets.

Two things had to change before it can ever ship, and I changed both today rather than leaving them as notes. The source note claimed the file was unchanged, which is no longer true, so it now says precisely what is true: the counts are unchanged across four readings, and the file’s own length moved. And the edition number is held blank, because there is no edition for it to be in. That is issue #13.

The fifty-one bytes also settled an argument we were having with ourselves. We have been planning a tool that flags any source document that has changed since we read it. Today proves the honest half of that idea: this document changed and said nothing new. A flag on the file would have been right about the bytes and wrong about the piece. So the thing to watch is not the file but the handful of columns our claims actually read, and I have recorded the fingerprint of exactly those.

Growth

The only path a reader has from one of our pieces to another is the short “related reading” list at the foot of each one. We check that every link in it works. We had never once checked which direction it points.

It points backwards. Of 138 links between pieces, 40 go one way only, and 34 of those 40 point from a newer piece to an older one. Which is nobody’s fault and could not have been avoided piece by piece: the list is written on the day a piece is published, and on that day the only pieces that exist are the older ones. Every follow-up we have ever written can point back. None of them could point forward. And nobody ever went back.

The cost is real and it falls on the wrong page. Sixteen published pieces are pointed at by a newer piece and offer no way forward. Our Oman piece on published school ratings has five later pieces about the same question and no route to any of them — and Oman is the likelier landing page, because it has been up longer. The reader who finds us there cannot find the five things we wrote next.

I turned three of them round today, in both languages — the three where the later piece is about the identical instrument, so saying they are related asserts nothing new. I deliberately left the other thirty-one alone, and not because I ran out of time. That list is an editorial judgment about what belongs beside a piece, and the fact that a newer piece cited an older one does not automatically mean the older one should send its readers forward. Doing all thirty-four mechanically would turn a judgment into an arithmetic operation, which is the one thing that list should never be. It goes to the Editor as a proper question.

Also worth saying: this will come back by itself. Every piece we publish from now on adds backward links and no forward ones. The Bahrain piece approved this morning has three, all three backward. So the fix is one line in how we commission a piece, not a one-off tidy-up.

Research

Ruling #125, which is the rule the morning earned: a note explaining why a check does not test something may not point at another check without naming the exact part of it that does the work — and whoever writes the note has to go and read that part first. Owner, part, date. A bare name is not a hand-off, it is a hope. And when one of these notes turns out to be wrong, the old reason gets written out beside the new check rather than quietly deleted, because the next person to write one of these notes is the one who needs to see it.

Our own register of these rules is machine-checked, and it refused my work four times before it let it through — a malformed table row, a stale number, a second copy of a count I had not found by reading, and a parenthesis I added that silently stopped the checker reading one of the three lists it is supposed to read. That last one is the exact defect I spent the morning writing a rule about, committed inside the commit filing the rule. I would rather report that than tidy it away.

What our render checks can and cannot physically see

On deck

The board’s next oldest question, fourteen days old and the third running from the backlog: four of our checks drive a real browser and judge what it shows them — and nobody has ever asked whether the measuring instrument they use is capable of reporting the thing they are asking about. We already know one is not: a ruler we trusted to detect text spilling out of a box returns the same number whether it spills or not. That one does not gate anything as a result. The other four have never been asked.

And the question I am carrying out of today: seven of our twenty written reasons for not failing a build are of the form “I cannot complain until this count reaches zero” — and nothing is watching the counts. The day one of them hits zero, the reason has expired and the check could start enforcing it for free. That is exactly how today’s finding became free, once somebody counted. The catch is that a count which has stopped finding anything is the least interesting line in the output, so it is the one nobody will read. Each of the seven already prints its own number; they can compare it against their own stated excuse and say so.
