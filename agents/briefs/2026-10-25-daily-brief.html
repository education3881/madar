Daily brief · No. 112 Sunday 25 October 2026
Nothing owed by you · the site is current · no missed day · yesterday’s overstep is still held

The website is current and nothing is waiting on you. Yesterday’s deploy went green on the exact commit the repository held this morning, and today’s work went out in five pushes across the day rather than one at the end.

On yesterday’s broken promise: nothing further was sent. The twenty-three remaining archive requests are held and stay held. Issue 12 is open with the question, and its default date is 1 November. Today did not touch it.

Today the publication got measurably lighter for an Arabic reader, and the interesting part is why nobody could have noticed. Every quality check this operation owns — all thirty-eight of them — reads a file that exists: a page we built, a word we wrote, a number we printed. None of them reads the thing a reader actually downloads. Those are not the same list. A typeface file sits in the build whether or not anyone ever receives it, and it is sent only when the browser hits a letter it needs. So the weight of a page, as experienced, exists nowhere until someone opens it — and we had never once looked.

When we looked, two italic tags in a paragraph on the front page were costing every reader of it an extra 131,100 bytes — because Arabic has no italics, and asking for one makes the browser fetch a whole separate slanted Arabic typeface to draw twenty-nine characters. The same mistake sat in the Arabic article template, on twelve published pages. Fixing it took four lines. The Arabic home page now sends 30.7% less type than it did this morning, and the Arabic edition as a whole sends 1.57 MB less.

Today

A served byte is not a fetched byte

2026-10-25 · Sunday · five functions, five pushes
Quality · the oldest job on the board, closed

The job at the head of the board was eight days old and asked a blunt question: why does one English article make a reader download half a megabyte of typefaces? It had been pushed down the list seven times. This morning it tied, exactly, with the job beside it — same age, same number of displacements, raised by the same commit — and the tie-breaking rule had to go to its last rung, which it had never needed before, to pick between them.

Measuring it meant building something this operation has never had: a probe that serves the real site to a real browser and writes down what the browser asks for. Its first output was the proof that it works — it reproduced the job’s own eight-day-old figure of 521,408 bytes to the byte, by a completely different method.

Then it added the half nobody had: how many letters is each of those files actually drawing? The answers are uncomfortable in a useful way. 111,720 bytes to draw twenty-nine characters. On an Arabic page, 71,376 bytes to draw the five letters of the word Madār in the footer.

Quality · what was fixed, and what a reader gets

Arabic typography has no italic. Our own stylesheet already knew this and said so in two separate places — but it said it per component, like a note stuck to two doors in a building with twenty. So the emphasis tags in the about paragraph had been slanting Arabic since the page was written, and twelve Arabic article pages were doing the same from a different rule. On one of them, the Iraq piece, that is 686 characters of quoted Arabic rendered in a slanted face that Arabic typography does not use.

Quality · the thirty-ninth check, and the bite that did not bite

A fix nobody can repeat is a habit, so the probe became a gate: check 39, wired into the build. It asserts two things — no Arabic is ever drawn slanted, and no typeface file is fetched that the page draws nothing from. The register now reads 39 checks, 36 of them standing between a change and the live site.

We prove a new check twice: it must stay quiet on a healthy page and go red on the defect. The first test, today’s own bug put back, went red correctly. The second test did not. We restored a real defect from 17 October — 146,872 bytes fetched to draw nothing — and the check passed it, cleanly, like a healthy page.

That failure is the most valuable thing this run produced. The Arabic line in question ends in a full stop, and a full stop lives inside every Latin typeface file in the world. Those 146,872 bytes were never unused; they were justified by a single period. The check was not wrong, its unit was — and a check that cannot be shown to catch the thing it was built for is a green light wired to nothing. The limitation is now printed on every build rather than discovered next year.

Growth · the first count of what we actually send

A metrics read, of something nobody needs to give us permission to see. All 134 pages, loaded in a browser, every request written down: 55.3 MB of typeface across the publication. The English edition sends a median of 521,408 bytes a page; the Arabic sends 295,628 — the Arabic edition is 44% lighter than the English one, which is the opposite of what anyone would guess.

And the largest single cost in the publication is not where anyone would look for it. The Arabic text face is downloaded by 133 of our 134 pages — every English one included — because the footer carries مدار and the English articles carry a taste of their Arabic twin. 14.4 MB across the site, to draw eighty-nine characters on a typical English page. That is the price of being genuinely bilingual, and it is the first time we have been able to say what it is. We are not proposing to stop paying it. We are handing the number to the design judgment that has been waiting on it.

The same census caught the twelve Arabic article pages the new check could not see, because the check loads six pages and the site has 134. It had said so, in writing, that morning. It became true the same afternoon.

Content · the Bahrain piece, one gate earlier

Yesterday’s check found seven numbers in the Bahrain draft’s citations that were not about Bahrain at all — they were the apparatus of our own reading, including the byte length of PDFs we had opened. Today those came out. A reader of the finished page will be told what the Authority reported, and will not be told how many bytes our copy of its report was. Checked after the edit with the same tool: thirty figures in the citations, every one of them printed in the article. Had it shipped as drafted, the build would have gone red.

A finding we are not fixing today, and why

The new probe also reports eighty-nine pieces of Arabic text on our pages that are drawn in whatever Arabic font the reader’s own device happens to own — every Arabic date, every section label, the Arabic copyright line. The cause is a rule whose own comment says it is about Latin labels on Arabic pages. They are not Latin labels. They are Arabic. This costs nothing in bytes, which is exactly why no amount of measuring weight would have found it, and it means the Arabic edition’s furniture looks different on every machine. It is on the board as a design decision rather than patched quietly, because choosing how our own name and dates are set is not a thing a byte count gets to decide.

The team question you set a date on, read today

Three weeks ago we split one role in two and wrote down a single number to judge it by, with today’s date on it: the age of the oldest job that has ever been pushed down the list. It stood at 28 days then. Today it is 8. The split is working on its own terms, measured the way we promised rather than the way that would flatter it. Whether that earns a new role at all is the weekly review’s call, not this one’s — today’s job was simply to read the number on the day we said we would.

Tomorrow

How much of itself does each of our checks actually look at?

2026-10-26 · Monday · and the weekly review falls today
The question tomorrow starts with

We have six standing rules saying a check only finds what it looks at. Every one of them asks what the check looked at. None asks how much of it. Today’s new check loads six pages out of 134, wrote that down as a caveat in the morning, and by the afternoon twelve live defects had been found on pages it does not load. Four of our browser-driven checks sample; one covers everything; not one of them prints the fraction. The first fix is cheap — make each one say 4 of 134 instead of implying all — and the second is to run each sampled check over everything, once, and record what was hiding outside.

On the board

Eighteen jobs open, one closed today and three opened. The two oldest are now the design judgments this morning’s measuring was holding up: whether the publication needs four typeface families, and what marks emphasis in Arabic now that the slant is gone. Both have had the numbers they were waiting for since about 11am.

Traffic, plainly

No visitor numbers, and that is a decision rather than a gap. The site carries no third-party tracker and makes no third-party request; a check gates that posture on every build. We will not estimate. What we can count without anyone’s permission, we did count today, for the first time: 55.3 MB of typeface across 134 pages, 1.57 MB of it taken off the Arabic edition this afternoon.
