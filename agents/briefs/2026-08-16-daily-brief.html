Content · Ed05 recon 6 — the best-system slot, answered with a ruler instead of a crown
You worded slot 3 deliberately — choose the ruler honestly and say which ruler crowns which country — and the honest return is that no African system wins on every ruler, and each defensible ruler crowns a different one:

Levels → Mauritius. It converts 12.4 expected years of school into 9.4 learned years (harmonized score 473; learning poverty 40% against a regional 80%). The interesting sentence isn't the crown, though — it's that Kenya spends a larger share of GDP on education than Mauritius does (5.3% vs 4.8%) and scores lower.

Learning-relative-to-income → Senegal and Benin (with Gabon and Burkina Faso), the only ruler on which a low-income African system beats the world's expectation of it.

Improvement → South Africa and Niger. South Africa's grade-9 maths rose +104 TIMSS points 2003–2019 and 389→397 by 2023 — while its grade-5 pupils placed last of 58. Both true at once. And Niger, which is the finest accomplishment story on the continent for your positive frame: after poor 2014 results it audited every contract teacher and dismissed those below proficiency, built remedial maths, reformed curriculum and teacher training — and posted a top-two continental gain with no change in class size. It bought learning with quality, not headcount.

Shared gains → nobody. This is the rail that keeps the piece honest: between-school inequality rose in all 14 PASEC countries at all levels, and Burkina Faso's weakest tenth saw reading fall 45 points while the national average rose. A country can improve and abandon its poorest children in the same five years, and only one of those reaches a league table.

Research · ruling #29 — a ranking is not a measurement; a superlative is a figure
The first new number in the series since 2 August, and I want to be explicit about why this one earns a number when rows 21 and 22 deliberately did not: #29 rejects a source class outright rather than qualifying a usable figure.

(a) A composite rank is citable only if its ruler is published — named indicators, weights, vintages, and the comparison set, all four. Absent any of them, report the underlying indicator from its own register, and never launder the composite through its ingredients: "based on UNESCO and World Bank data" inherits none of their authority. The origin case is the aggregator tier that owns this query, recycled verbatim site to site — which manufactures the appearance of corroboration from one unexamined source. What makes it a ruling rather than a preference is that it is checkably wrong: it crowns South Africa first in Africa in the same window South Africa's own ministry published its grade-5 pupils last of 58. And composites must impute to produce a complete ordinal — precisely over the holes the honest sources declare (no learning-poverty data for Kenya; no stunting or gender split for Mauritius).

(b) "Best," "largest," "first," "fastest" are figures, each carrying a ruler, a population, a vintage, and a source stating the comparison — not one from which it could be inferred. Applied to ourselves twice today: the recon declined to print "the largest improvement in TIMSS history" for South Africa's +104 points, because no source read this run states it. And the sharpest edge — your own instruction contains a superlative, so it is an assignment, not a finding. A commission is not a source. The family now scopes three objects: a number (rows 15/19/20/21), an identity (row 22), and now a comparison.
