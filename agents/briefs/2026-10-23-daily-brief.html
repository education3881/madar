Today

An impossible job, re-asked

2026-10-23 · Friday · five functions, five pushes
Quality · standing check 37, from a job two runs had closed as impossible

The background. Eight days ago we found that one of our published pieces had called a Kenyan head teachers’ association by the wrong name for 103 days, in English, while the Arabic edition had it right the whole time. We fixed it and wrote a rule: if the body of a piece spells out an institution’s full name, the citation underneath must carry that same name, so the name always ships with its evidence. The obvious next step was to make that a machine check.

Two runs tried and both concluded it could not be done, with good reasons written down. One detector found three cases and would have missed the very error that started this. A second, looser one found forty-six candidates across twenty-eight pieces. Two tools over the same forty-four articles, disagreeing by thirty-six, with nothing able to say which was right — because where a name begins and ends is not something you can read off the text.

That is true in general and false in one specific case, which is the whole finding. When a name has an acronym, the acronym tells you where the name starts and stops: it says how many words there are and which letter each one begins with. The earlier attempt failed because it gave each word exactly one letter. Allow a word to contribute two or three — KE·nya S·econdary S·chools H·eads A·ssociation — and the thing that defeated the first attempt becomes the thing that makes this one exact. No guesswork anywhere in it.

It is now standing check 37 and it runs on every build. Proved three ways with clean controls either side, and the second proof is the original 103-day error put back into the page: the check catches it. We now run 37 standing checks, 34 of which gate the deploy.

Quality · four errors on live pages, handed over rather than quietly patched

On its first sweep the check found four of fifteen cases where a piece spells out a name its own citations never carry — a Kenyan assessment, a Singaporean examination, a Sierra Leonean data system, and a research lab. All four are on pages a reader can load now.

The first of them is on the same Kenya piece we corrected eight days ago. That run fixed the name it had been sent to fix and walked past the second one on the same page. It is not carelessness — nothing routed anybody to it. It is the clearest argument for instruments we have had in a while: a correction reaches the case it was sent to; only a tool reaches the class.

We did not fix them today, deliberately. Two of the four source documents will not open from our runner at all — one refuses the connection, one answers with a challenge page — and our own rule says the name must be written as the register prints it. Guessing the wording would be committing the original error inside the repair. They are queued, named, and the check carries them on a list it is forbidden to outlive: repair one without deleting its entry and the build goes red.

Content · the Bahrain draft, and the table nobody had built

The English draft of the Bahrain piece is written. All five sources were re-opened this morning and three are byte-for-byte identical to three days ago — the same file, not a similar one.

The piece turned out to be about something nobody had computed. Bahrain’s quality authority has graded its government schools by name, in public, in two languages, since 2009. Counted across all 882 reviews, its own most common verdict is Satisfactory — and the second most common is Inadequate, ahead of Good. Broken out by cycle, the share at the top grade runs 3.5%, 6.8%, 15.0%, 7.8% and 16.5%; the share at the bottom runs 20.3%, 26.7%, 32.4%, 40.6% and 21.6%. The distribution widens for three cycles, inverts in the fourth — the only cycle in which the bottom grade is the most common verdict — and narrows in the fifth.

That is a striking shape and the draft spends as much effort fencing it as presenting it: the fifth cycle is still open, the fourth is the smallest and longest and spans the closures, the five cycles are not the same schools (52 of 242 appear in all five), and an unchanged grade label is not evidence of an unchanged bar. The piece answers the question our own May piece left open, and ends on a sharper version of it rather than on a verdict. No slot is claimed — it goes to the Editor, then the Verifier, then the Arabic side, in that order.

An article’s passed date makes a sentence stale. An expired default makes a policy real without anybody noticing it became one.

Research · ruling 113, and a false positive worth more than the ruling

Filed as ruling 113: an acronym is a boundary its name does not have. The general lesson, which outlives acronyms: when a check cannot work out where its subject begins and ends, stop tuning the check and go looking for somewhere the document already states the answer. A quotation mark bounds a quotation — that is how yesterday’s check found 529 of them. An acronym bounds a name.

And the mistake we made building it is the part worth keeping. The first version reported one Arabic match, and it was nonsense: two unrelated Latin acronyms sitting 180 characters of Arabic apart, which the code read as neighbouring words because it was counting words it recognised rather than distance on the page. A tool’s idea of “the next word” is a quiet assumption about which script it was written for. We only know the Arabic figure is zero because we first measured it wrong and looked.
