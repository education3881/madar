It should go out within a minute or two of this brief landing, and tomorrow’s brief will report what it actually did.

Twenty-five of the documents in this series were broken, and seven of them were unreadable. Not the website — these briefs, the one thing the charter says you actually open. Seven briefs from August are between five and twenty-six screens wider than the window they open in, because the copy-paste block I used to send you sat in a box nobody had ever given a style. Seventeen more from May and June push half their bold phrases into a column twenty-four pixels wide. All twenty-five are repaired, and the check that found them now fails the build if it ever happens again.

The part worth your minute is where the answer came from. I did not have to invent how a push block should look: the right design was sitting in each of those seven files the whole time, written out in full, attached to a name the page had stopped using. The instruction survived; the page walked away from it. That is now a standing rule here — before deleting a style nothing uses, ask whether it is leftover or whether it is the last surviving description of what the page was supposed to be.

And one decision is yours, with four days on the clock. The home page tells every reader that the current issue is October 2026. On 1 November that sentence becomes false by itself and nothing here can make it true — it needs to know what the next edition is. That is GitHub issue 13, opened Sunday. Today it stopped being a note in a brief and became a row on the board with a date on it: on 1 November it moves itself into the column marked currently wrong.

Today

The evidence was the part we were about to throw away

2026-10-27 · Tuesday · five functions, six pushes
Quality · the oldest live defect on the board, closed

The board sends the run at whatever is currently wrong before anything else, and this morning that was eight days old: twenty-five of your briefs and reviews render broken. The written description named two causes. There were three.

Seven briefs, 10–18 August. The copy-paste block went into a box tagged push, and push was never given a style in any of those seven files. So the browser fell back to its own default, which neither wraps nor scrolls, and your one-line commit command dragged the whole document 7,277 to 34,254 pixels wider than the screen. Sixty-two days.

Seventeen briefs, late May to mid-June. Each bullet was laid out as a two-column grid with a 24-pixel gutter for the dot. A grid does something to the text inside it that reads like a technicality and is not: it breaks every bold phrase out into a cell of its own, and half of them landed in the 24-pixel gutter. That is Brief No. 01’s famous one-word-wide column, and the mechanism now has a name.

And one weekly review, which was not that at all. A full sentence of prose had been put inside a cell marked this cell holds a number, and that marking tells the browser never to wrap. Nineteen pixels. Found by repairing the other twenty-four, not by counting them.

136 of 136 now render clean, and the check that measures it has been promoted: it used to print a warning, and it now fails the build. It had been held back for two reasons; one of them — there are twenty-five known exceptions — stopped being true this morning.

And two more, found by looking rather than by measuring

A standing rule here says that every page rendered for a human gets opened and looked at before it ships. Looking at the repaired 17 August brief showed it still running edge to edge, with no margin at all. Both it and the 16 August brief are missing the single tag that centres the page — while the instruction that would have centred them sits complete in both stylesheets, pointing at nothing.

Those are the exact two briefs that caused us to write the rule about looking, on 23 August. We wrote the rule, built the checks it demanded, and never went back and fixed the two documents that prompted it. Sixty-five days. Both are repaired now. A rule written about a thing does not repair the thing.

Knowledge · ruling 118

Both halves of today’s repair leaned on something we were inclined to treat as rubbish, and that is the ruling. What the page was supposed to look like survived only in style rules that matched nothing — so an unused style is not dead weight, it is an unfiled bug report about the page. What actually went wrong survived only in the version history.

That second half cost me a wrong answer first. To prove the new check really bites, you have to break something on purpose and watch it go red. I rebuilt the fault by hand from a diagnosis I had got right — and the check passed. The reconstruction restored one half of a two-part mechanism, so it produced a different and harmless layout. Restored from the version history instead, the real defect went red immediately. When the fault is in the history, do not rebuild it from your understanding of it; your understanding is exactly what you are trying to test.

Content · Türkiye, read after its own deadline

Our Türkiye piece argues that a published rebuild ledger turns has Hatay recovered? into a question you can actually check. Its deadline — September 2026 — has now passed, so we checked it.

The province answered; the country did not. On 14 September, opening the school year, Hatay’s governor put his province at 254 schools finished and back in use against 47 still building — the same two columns the piece reported at 199 and 70 a year earlier, moved again in the same direction. The national figure, measured against the ministry’s own 24,000-classroom target, we could not find. Both editions now say exactly that, and say it as a record of our search rather than as a claim about the ministry’s silence.

One detail of method, because it is the kind of thing this publication exists to get right. Arabic cannot name a governor without declaring his gender; English can avoid it, and Turkish does not mark it at all. So rather than guess from the name, the Arabic edition read it off the man’s own official profile, which describes him as a father of three — and that page is now cited in both editions, with the reason written out, although only one of them needed it.

Growth · a claim that could never have been caught

Yesterday’s open question: which of our claims live in the code rather than in the articles? Enumerated today. Two kinds came back, and only one was expected.

The expected kind is a date that expires — October 2026 on the home page, false on 1 November, yours to resolve. The unexpected kind is worse, because nothing could ever have noticed it. The copyright line in the footer of all 134 pages read the year the build happened to run in. Every test we own compares one byte against another byte, and two builds run in the same year produce the same byte no matter what the repository says. On 1 January 2027 every page built in December quietly starts carrying the wrong year, nothing goes red, and nothing could.

It now takes its year from the repository’s own history, the same source the sitemap uses. The footer is byte-for-byte identical today — which is the right result, not a sign that nothing happened. The value did not change; where it comes from did. It is now the same on any day you build it, and for the first time it is capable of being wrong in a way something can detect. A claim that cannot be caught being wrong is not a strong claim.

The checks caught me three times today, and were right three times

Worth recording because it is the system working rather than the system being tidy. The pair check caught two new citations pasted one level too high in a file. Two citation checks went red because the new sources had never been archived or verified, and cleared once they were. And the figure check went red on the characters 86 inside a note I had just written — which is a reference to our own rule 86, not a measurement at all. Logged as such, rather than teaching the tool to ignore anything with a hash in front of it.

Today’s open question, written down so tomorrow has to test it

Today I promoted a check that catches a page rendering wider than the screen. It is the only tool of the thirty-nine that measures that at all — and it reads your briefs, not the publication. So the 134 pages a reader is served have never been checked for this, at any width, in two scripts, one of which runs right-to-left. The rule that created the brief check in August was written to add your briefs to a net that already covered the website. For this one property the net only ever covered the briefs, and the sentence reads the same either way.

Second half, cheaper: the check runs at exactly one window width, 1,280 pixels, chosen because that is what every previous inspection happened to use. A page that is fine at 1,280 and broken on a phone is invisible to it — and you open these on whatever is in your hand.
