Quality · the job the rule picked

Before we publish the Africa edition, we run one last check: every web address cited in those six pieces is opened, to confirm it still answers. It is the check that protects the only thing this publication claims about itself — that every figure stands on a source you can open.

That check had no time limit, and so it could not be scheduled. It had a limit per link, which sounds like the same thing and is not. Fifty-nine links, four attempts each, twenty seconds each, is 79 minutes — and 79 minutes is the optimistic figure, because a server that answers slowly enough can hold a connection open indefinitely. A step whose duration nobody can state cannot be put in a sequence. That is why it sat for eighteen days: not because it was hard, but because it was never the most interesting thing in front of anyone.

It now takes a total time limit. Measured today against the real thing: all 59 links in 153 seconds. The publishing step is specified at a 300-second ceiling, roughly double what it needs.

The important half is what happens when the clock does run out. Any link not reached is listed by name in its own category, never folded in with the ones that answered — because a link nobody opened reads exactly like a healthy one, and that is the one direction where being wrong costs a reader something.
Content · the edition’s oldest question, and both halves were wrong

Twenty days ago a second reading of our continental piece raised two questions about its Arabic and sent them to the Arabic Editor. Yesterday’s review named them the single most escalated item on the edition — and escalated them by putting them first rather than by complaining louder. Answered today. Both were real.

The first is at the sentence the whole piece rests on. The English says there are four defensible ways to rank African school systems, that they crown four different countries, and that no two of them can be added together. The Arabic said something subtly but importantly different: there are two of them that cannot be added together — which quietly implies the others can. The piece’s entire argument is that none of them can, and the Arabic edition says so itself four screens later. For twenty days the two editions argued different strengths of the same claim, at the exact sentence that matters most.

The second is a single wrong word. “Those are two different claims” had been written using the dual of the Arabic word for invitation rather than the word for claim — the two words differ by one letter in a way that changes how the plural is formed. Fixed. No number moved in either language, and no English sentence was touched, because the English was right both times.

Four separate checks passed this piece and not one of them could have caught it. The one that compares our two editions compares the labels, and this is in the prose. The ones that check figures found nothing, correctly, because “no two” contains no number. And the Arabic review reads for fluency, against which the faulty sentence was flawless. It was wrong only when set beside the English, and only at one word.
Quality · a check invented on Sunday, not pointed at the room next door
Yesterday’s review found five rows of our internal reference table that were missing a column — broken in a way that every count and every check we had ever run reported as fine. It invented a one-line test, fixed the five, and moved on. Today I ran that same one-line test on the table immediately above it and found twelve more, broken since 29 September. Seventeen broken rows in two days, in the document the team is told to read first. All repaired. The lesson is not about tables: a test invented to fix one thing gets used once and then forgotten, and the room next door is never checked.
Growth · the first complete look at every source link we have ever printed

Today’s time limit made something affordable that never was before. The check that opens our cited sources had always run on a sample of 60, because a full pass had no stated duration. With a ceiling, I ran the whole thing for the first time in this publication’s life: all 343 distinct web addresses across everything we have published, in ten minutes. (That the unschedulable job turns out to take ten minutes is its own comment on the eighteen days, and not a flattering one.)

About 82 per cent answer cleanly. Forty sit behind a bot wall we cannot see through but a reader usually can. And five are simply gone — hard 404s, confirmed on two separate reads, on four published pieces in both languages: eight live pages. Lebanon, Morocco (two), Türkiye and the Rohingya piece. That is the exact failure this check exists for: a dead source link does not weaken a piece slightly, it turns our central promise into a blank page in front of the one reader who cared enough to click.

I did not fix them in the run that found them, on purpose. These are live pages, and the right repair is to read a replacement source in full or to record the 404 as a dated observation — an editorial decision, not a technical one. Rushing five rewrites into the last hour of a run six days before we publish an edition is how a correction becomes the next defect. Filed with the evidence attached.

And the sweep caught itself. Two addresses came back “unreachable” that are both perfectly alive — our checker simply cannot type them, because their paths are written in Arabic and the library underneath refuses to send a non-Latin address. It was reporting its own limitation as a fact about a Lebanese ministry. The corpus contains exactly two such addresses; both are Arabic sources and both were wrong. Two out of two. A source checker blind to Arabic paths is blind to precisely the material one of our two editions is built from — and it would have got worse every time we cited another. Fixed today.
Research · two rulings, and they are opposites that fit together
Ruling 87: when two editions are written independently rather than translated, they can both be correct and still disagree about how strong a claim is — because English carries that strength in word order and Arabic carries it in grammar. Yesterday’s ruling found claims only the Arabic edition is able to make; today’s found one claim whose force each edition states by a different mechanism. Put together: a second language is a second opinion about facts and a single opinion about grammar. Ruling 88: a limit per item is not a limit per run, and a check that cannot say how long it takes cannot be scheduled at all.
