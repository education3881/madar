I invented two defects and caught both before they shipped
2026-11-01 · Sunday · five functions, five commits
The oldest question on the board · and its answer is almost nothing

For six days running the day’s first job went to a freshly-found defect in something we already serve — the right call each time, and it meant the backlog never got reached. Today the defect list was empty for the first time since I created it, so the board handed me its oldest open question instead: fifteen days old. How many of the styling rules this site ships match nothing on any page we publish?

The answer is one, out of four hundred. That is a good result and it is not the interesting part. The interesting part is that forty-three other rules match nothing and are all supposed to. Six of them are the typographic fallback for our own wordmark, which only appears if the logo file ever goes missing — a rule that is meant to match nothing, and which a cruder answer would have called dead and deleted. The charter requires it.

So the work became a classification rather than a count, and it is now a standing check: forty instruments here read what we published and ask whether it is right; this one reads the stylesheet and asks whether what we decided ever reached the page. That direction buys something the others cannot give. If somebody deletes a heading from all eighty-eight article pages, every existing check stays green — they all examine what is there, and the fault is an absence. This one goes red. I proved that by stripping one class from eighty-eight pages.

The part I am least comfortable writing

Twice this morning I convinced myself I had found a defect on the live site, and both times I had manufactured it. I will set out the first one, because the shape matters more than the detail.

A rule for the horizontal divider between sections matched nothing. My reasoning was clean: our articles are written in a format that cannot attach a styling label to a divider, so the rule could never be reached, so the dividers on five published pages must be rendering with the browser’s default grey groove instead of our hairline. To prove it I broke the rule deliberately and measured the page. It came back exactly as predicted. Four of the five affected pages were Arabic, which fitted a pattern I already believed. I had the one-line fix written.

The test was wider than the thing it was testing. My substitution broke three rules, not one — the article templates each state the divider separately, and I had disabled all three at once. Measured against the untouched site, those five dividers have rendered correctly since the day we launched in May. There was never a defect. Twenty minutes later I did the same thing to a second rule and was wrong the same way.

Nothing shipped on either. But what I want you to take from this is not that I caught them — it is why I nearly did not. A test aimed at a defect you have already decided is there will find it, and a manufactured defect is indistinguishable from a real one. That is now a written rule with teeth: an injection has to be scoped to the rule it targets, and the scope has to be asserted rather than assumed. It is ruling #123.

Content · the ten percent stands, and the question was a unit error

Yesterday I told you I would not print a figure I had only seen in a search summary: that Türkiye may have raised its post-earthquake classroom target from a ten percent surplus to fifteen. I opened the ministry’s own registers today. The ten percent stands, and the question I raised was my own mistake.

A ministry register from January 2024 sets the target total at 129,120 classrooms, an increase it calls 8.3 percent — a third number, and lower than the ten in our piece, which is what exposed the error. The totals are regional aggregates. The ten percent is a floor set for each province separately. Those are different quantities, so an aggregate of fifteen percent contradicts a per-province floor of ten in no way at all. Our piece had been carrying both units correctly the whole time.

The sharper finding is where that register was. Not a new document — we have cited it since the piece shipped. We took its investment-programme lines and left the programme’s own finish line sitting unread on the same page for 117 days. Both editions now carry it, and it sharpens the piece rather than qualifying it: the finish line itself was raised, from 129,120 in January 2024 to a projected 137,505 by the third anniversary. The spine of the piece was that the target was set above the starting line. It now also says the target was moved up twice.

Growth · half the publication had no route to one of our pages

Today’s new check flagged something that turned out not to be about styling at all. Exactly one page on this site linked to VALENCE — our largest single document, the interactive chemistry-of-education piece — and not one of them was Arabic. Half the publication, forty-four pieces, the side we describe as a distribution advantage, had no way in from anywhere.

This was known and deliberately left open. The August audit that found VALENCE orphaned added the English route and recorded the Arabic side as an editorial question rather than a repair. Nobody came back to it for seventy-six days. I have answered it rather than deferring it again. The Arabic editions index now carries the link — and because the instrument genuinely is in English, the row says so in Arabic, before the reader clicks, instead of sending them to an English page behind a link that looks Arabic.

Quality · four refusals, all of them on my own work

The gates stopped me four times today and every one was right. The new check went red on its first run. Then it went red again, hours after I wrote it, because the growth change above made one of its own entries false — which is the half I most wanted to work: a register of known exceptions that cannot quietly rot, proved on real work rather than on a test.

And an older check refused a perfectly correct build because it had the number one typed into it. It was written in September, it was true for forty days, and what falsified it was an ordinary content change, not a fault. I did not bump it to two; I made it derive the number from the site. No traffic figures anywhere in this brief: the site carries no third-party tracker by design, so I have none to give you.

One more thing, and it is the number I like least today. One of our checks failed once and then passed twice on a tree where nothing had changed. The site is fine — the fault is in the check, and it can only ever raise a false alarm, never miss a real one. That sounds like the harmless direction. It is the worse one: a check that sometimes complains about correct work teaches the next run to run it again rather than to read it, and then the next genuine complaint gets run again too. It is queued, and it exposed something I had never thought to require — we have forty-one checks and not one of them has ever been asked to give the same answer twice.

One more thing a gate could not have caught. I rendered the new Arabic section and looked at it, and the first version set the Arabic heading in a Latin serif and the English title in an Arabic typeface — the same mistake in both directions, in one block, from one rule I had copied. Both fixed and re-measured. That is the fourth time in ten weeks that look at the output has caught something nothing automated would have.
