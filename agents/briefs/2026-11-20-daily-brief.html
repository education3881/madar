The one thing worth your attention today

The page that lists everything we have ever published was, to a machine, blank. Every page on this site carries a small block of machine-readable data describing what it is — who published it, what language it is in, and, on a page that lists things, what is on the list. Our archive carried none of it. Not an incomplete block: nothing at all. No list, no trail, not even the two lines that say “this page belongs to Madār” that every other page we serve carries.

And the small pages were fine. Forty-six of our pages link to at least one article; forty-two of them carry a proper list, including pages that list two pieces. The four that did not were the two archive pages and the two home pages. So the surface that shows the whole corpus — 45 pieces a side, the deepest index we have — was the one a search engine could read nothing from, while a page listing two pieces from Bahrain was fully described.

What makes it worth your attention is that we read this page two days ago and did not see it. On Wednesday a run went through this exact page, found the one sentence in it that search results show was wrong, wrote a correct one and shipped it. Nothing in that reading asked whether the page said anything else at all. A check finds what it goes looking for; this one went looking for a sentence and found a sentence.

Fixed today, both language sides, measured before and after: zero machine-readable entries to 46 on each page — every piece in the order the page shows it, plus the standing interactive piece. And one small refusal inside the fix that I think is the right instinct: the existing code would have stamped the list “newest first,” which is true of every other list on the site and false of the archive, which groups by edition. There was no honest third option available, so we left the claim off rather than make a tidy one that is wrong. That is yesterday's lesson applied the next morning: a field that loads perfectly and says the wrong thing cannot fail, so nothing would ever have told us.

Today
Friday 20 November · brief 136

Content — the England piece reached step seven of eight. Its illustration and share card are drawn. The drawing is the regulator's own measuring edge, laid across the page and drawn once: graduated at the left, then a long stretch of plain line carrying no graduations at all — the eight years in which England's best-graded schools were not routinely inspected — then graduations again, twice as close together, because the regulator went back in and inspected a great many of them in one year. Then the edge simply stops. Set apart to the right, with nothing joining them, is a second, different edge whose marks hang downward: the instrument that replaced it, and which its own publisher says cannot be compared to the old one. Nothing in the picture encodes a percentage, which is a rule the Editor set when the piece was commissioned.

And where those two files are stored turned out to matter more than drawing them. The obvious home is the folder every published piece's artwork lives in. Put them there today and they would be served to the public immediately — a share card for an article that does not yet exist, at an address anyone can guess, with the drawing's own caption giving away the unpublished piece's subject. We have a guard against exactly that, built in September. It does not fire here, and I tested it rather than assuming. The guard works by asking which pieces are held; this piece is not held, it is not there yet, and those are different things no part of our system can currently tell apart. I copied a card into a test build and the guard reported everything clean while the file sat in the folder it had just swept. So the two files are staged outside the published folder and move across in the same commit that publishes the piece.

Quality — the two oldest jobs on the board, 25 and 24 days old, closed together by one new check. One asked that we guard the “last changed” date on every article page; the other asked the same of the copyright year in the footer. They are one question: does every date we print come from the repository, or from the clock of the machine that built the page? The second is the dangerous one, and it is worth a sentence because it is genuinely invisible: a date taken from the build machine's clock agrees with itself on every check we could run, because any two builds in the same year produce the same bytes. On 1 January every page would quietly start carrying the wrong year and nothing anywhere would change colour. So the new check does something none of our other forty-two do: four of its limbs compare what we published against what we published, and the fifth reads our own source code and asserts that it never asks the machine what day it is. Proved eight ways, with five controls that had to stay silent.

And the new check's first run accused the one file in the whole repository that was written to document the fix. It flagged a line inside the explanatory comment that says why the clock reading was removed — because the first draft read comments as if they were code. In a codebase where we write the reason beside every repair, the words you search for always appear in the prose about the defect. That is the third time in a week that an instrument has sprung the exact trap it was built to catch, and each time it printed something that looked like a healthy answer.

Research — the lesson written down. Our publication switch has two settings, published and held, and six separate tools read it. The gate a piece must clear has eight steps. A piece six steps in is in neither setting — it is absent — and a tool that checks a list reports “nothing found” in exactly the same words whether the list is clean or the thing was never on it. A gap in a classification does not announce itself as a gap. It announces itself as a pass.

Nothing editorial published today, and I want to be plain about that. The England piece has one step left: the flip that publishes it, carrying six repairs that are bound to it rather than left for a later tidy-up. It ships on the day its own gate closes. The last piece to publish was Bahrain, on 9 November.
