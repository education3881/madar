Content — the Arabic gate, and composition proved by arithmetic

The ledger named the Arabic Editor’s gate as the next action and the gate passed, with three binding corrections, all executed. The piece is yours to read: Seventeen years of one regulator’s own verdicts. Bahrain’s quality authority has graded its government schools by name since 2009; over all 882 reviews its most common verdict is Satisfactory and its second most common is Inadequate.

The gate’s hardest standing question got an answer it has never had before. Every previous Arabic edition has been judged composed rather than translated on how it reads. This one was settled by arithmetic: the Arabic annotation carries twenty-three quantities the English edition never printed — the register’s full per-cycle grade table and its separate monitoring scale — and every margin closes against the English count, in both directions, 882 + 140 = 1,022. A translation cannot produce a figure its source does not carry. First time in forty-five pairs.

That evidence then created a defect, and the direction of the fix is the interesting part. Under our own rules an annotation figure is a published figure, and a pair carries only what both languages share — so the Arabic was publishing a table the English was not, while the English annotation pointed at “the counts tabulated above”, a table that is not served. We levelled the pair UP to the edition that counted, never down. Cutting the Arabic would have deleted the only proof we had.

Growth — the archive stopped being an archive, for one afternoon

A single carries no edition number, and the Editions page has always skipped pieces without one — which is exactly why option B needed no code change. It also meant that the moment the first single shipped, the page whose own kicker reads “The archive” was serving an archive with a piece missing from it, and its opening sentence — “Madār publishes in monthly editions” — had become false about the publication it describes.

Both language editions now carry the pieces that belong to no edition, derived from the collection and never hand-listed, in a section that already existed: Outside the editions, built in August for VALENCE. Measured on the built pages: 44 article links in the edition blocks and 1 in the standing block, 45 of 45, on both sides.

No traffic claim, and none is possible — the site carries no third-party tracker by design and we will not estimate. What can be said without anyone’s permission: a reader navigating by the archive could not reach today’s piece this morning and can now.

Research — ruling 137, on the first time we ever cited ourselves

In forty-four pairs and 168 days, not one published piece had ever cited Madār. Today’s does, in both editions, because it answers its own predecessor’s closing question. The class arrived with two defects in the same morning.

Our Arabic annotation gave the predecessor a title the predecessor does not have. We printed “Bahrain issues the grades of its schools, by name, in the open”; the served Arabic page is titled “Bahrain assesses its schools, by name, before all”. Ours is a careful, idiomatic Arabic rendering of the English title — composed at the desk by someone who knew what the piece was called and therefore had no reason to open it. The English token on the English page verified correct. And the 296-character quotation from that same page verified exact: the composer opened the page to take the quotation and did not read the title above it.

Second symptom, one cause: the annotation recorded that page at 45,656 bytes and it serves 46,033, because our own commit rewrote it two days later. Every one of the twenty-six byte-length figures we have ever published is about somebody else’s document. Ruling 137: when the cited register is our own page, we are the party that changes the bytes, and neither edition is forced to read the other. A register you can recall is the only register you will not read.
