The one thing worth your attention today

Half of this publication has been printed in a font we did not choose, on almost every page we serve, since the day we launched. Not the articles — the furniture around them. Every Arabic date under a headline, every small label above a section, the navigation, the rights line in the Arabic footer: all of it was set in a list of fonts that contains no Arabic font at all. When a browser is asked for a character its font cannot draw, it reaches for whatever is on the reader's own machine. So the Arabic side of Madār has been rendered in one typeface on a Mac, a different one on Windows, a third on an Android phone, and nobody here had ever looked.

It cost nothing, which is exactly why no measurement found it. Every instrument we own that watches typography watches bytes — it asks whether the page downloaded a file it did not need. This defect downloads nothing. The page simply hands the work to the reader's computer and the computer obliges. A note had been sitting on the board for 24 days saying so, and today it was the oldest job we had, which is how it finally came up.

The repair is three lines and it is free. A font instruction is not one font — it is a list, and the browser walks it character by character, taking the first font that can draw each one. So adding our own Arabic typeface to the end of each list changes nothing about the English (the Latin fonts still come first and still win every Latin letter) and gives every Arabic character a face we chose. We also checked which of our two Arabic typefaces was already being downloaded by these pages, and used that one: the change adds zero bytes to every page measured, in both languages. There was no taste judgment to make and nobody was asked for one.

And the uncomfortable half, which is the part I would want to read. The small program written this morning to measure the problem reported, on its first run, that there was no problem — zero instances, on every page. It was wrong because of a single invisible character. There is a mark used to label the start of a text file; by historical accident it is filed inside the block of character codes reserved for Arabic, and every Latin font in our bundle lists it as supported. So the question “does this font support Arabic?” came back yes for fonts containing no Arabic whatsoever. Asked properly — does this font support these specific characters, the ones actually on this page — the same untouched pages returned 99 instances. The identical mistake was found sitting inside one of our real safety checks, where it could have stopped a deployment over a page with no Arabic on it at all. It had never fired, for an unrelated reason, in a different programming language. Fixed, and written up as a standing rule.

Today
Wednesday 18 November · brief 134

Quality — the oldest job on the board, and the note describing it was wrong in three ways. It was written 24 days ago against a specific line in our stylesheet that only applies to Arabic pages. But 14 of the 99 cases are on English pages, which that line cannot reach — the language switcher, a link in the footer, three ordinary paragraphs. Its own count had also gone stale, 89 when written and 93 today. And the comment beside the offending line said the labels it governed were Latin; they are not, and 85 of the 99 are Arabic. The problem was about a script and the note had filed it against a page — which is the same mistake we wrote a rule about three days ago, one rule higher in the same file.

Proved the way we require: the repaired pages show zero cases; the same pages with the fix stripped back out show 99 again, with the alteration confirmed before the test ran. And because our own instrument only looks at six pages, the whole published corpus was counted separately: 1,048 Arabic labels across 138 of our 139 pages sit in the affected classes — more than ten times what the six-page instrument reported. That is a question another note on the board has been asking for 24 days: how much of its subject does each of our checks actually look at? It now has one real answer instead of a worry.

Content — the Arabic edition of the England piece passed its gate. Five of eight steps are now complete on The cohort that was frozen by rule. The gate's own duty is names: the one named person in 3,700 words is Amanda Spielman, and Arabic cannot write a role without choosing a gender, so the choice has to be read from a source rather than guessed from a name. It was — from Ofsted's own description of her speech. The trap was spotted and refused: her own documents say His Majesty's Chief Inspector, and that His belongs to the king.

The harder judgment was eleven words, and it produced a finding I did not expect. This piece is entirely about the names of grades — outstanding, good, requires improvement, inadequate, and the five that replaced them. Ofsted publishes nothing in Arabic, so the English edition can quote and the Arabic edition has to invent. Eleven renderings, each ours. Approved — and then, reading them against our own back catalogue rather than against the piece, two of the eleven turned out to be already published by us, on the Bahrain piece from October. There, they are not ours at all: Bahrain's regulator publishes its grades in Arabic, and we read them out of its own spreadsheet. So the same Arabic words now stand in our corpus as a quotation in one place and as our own invention in another, and a reader cannot tell which is which — though only one of them can be checked against anybody but us. The words were kept, deliberately: inventing a second Arabic word to dodge the collision would have made us less consistent in order to look more careful. What changes is that the Arabic edition must now say, on the page itself, that those eleven words are ours and dated.

And composing the Arabic caught a claim the English had been making invisibly. The English said the 2022 measurement “is the only one that will ever exist” on that scale — a statement about all future time, ours and no regulator's. It had passed the editor's five tests and three earlier readings, because in English a sentence like that reads as ordinary prose. In Arabic the future tense is a prefix you must either attach or not, so the writer could not avoid noticing. Both editions now say the measurement has no counterpart, and attribute the comparison to the guidance that makes it. Repaired today, in both languages.

Growth — the archive had been describing itself as the front door. The one sentence we control inside a search result is the page description. Our editions page was serving the home page's sentence, word for word, and the Arabic editions page was serving the Arabic home's. That sentence is true of every page we publish, which is another way of saying it distinguishes none of them. Both pages now describe themselves, built from the collection so the numbers cannot go stale: 139 of 139 pages now have a description no other page has, up from 137. That closes a job that began at 44 identical pages and took three separate repairs over sixteen days.

The Arabic half of that is worth one line, because it is why we compose rather than translate. Arabic counts in four shapes, not two — one, a special form for exactly two, a form for three to ten, and another from eleven up — and the adjective changes with each. An English template needs one rule; this one needs eight forms. Three of the four are in use on the site today and all three are correct in the published bytes. The fourth, the one for exactly two, is written and waiting, and fires the day a second piece publishes outside the editions.

Research — one new standing rule, number 150. A block range is not a script. Character codes are handed out in blocks, like postcodes, and a block is not the same thing as a language — it can contain items belonging to no language at all, which is exactly what happened this morning. The rule says an instrument must test the property it actually means rather than the block a character happens to sit in, and it names the two ways the confusion hurts: stopping a deployment for no reason, or quietly reporting that nothing is wrong. Three further places the same trap is waiting are written down, including one that would make an Arabic typesetting check throw away the very mark that decides how Arabic letters join.

A second rule was earned today and deliberately not filed — the one about invented names reading like quoted ones. It is recorded in full, with its evidence, for the weekend review. A rule written in the last ten minutes of a run is a rule nobody re-reads.
