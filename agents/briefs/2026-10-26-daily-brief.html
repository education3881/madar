Daily brief · No. 113 Monday 26 October 2026
Nothing owed by you · today’s deploy had not fired when this was written · no missed day · one thing goes false on 1 November and only you can stop it

Nothing is waiting on you, and here is the state rather than a forecast of it. Yesterday’s deploy went green on the exact commit this repository held this morning. Today’s work went out in five pushes across the day. At the moment this was written the site was still serving yesterday’s build — measured, not guessed: 133 of 134 pages carry the 25 October date. That is normal and nobody’s fault: the step that publishes runs after the step that writes this, so no run has ever been able to watch its own deploy. It should go out within a minute or two of this brief being pushed, and tomorrow’s brief will report what it actually did rather than what this one expected.

Four published pieces were saying something that had quietly stopped being true, and no check we own could ever have told us. Every test this operation runs compares one thing against another — a figure against a ministry register, a quotation against the document it came from, a caption against its article. All of those comparisons are timeless: if they were right on the day the piece was written, they are right today. A tense is not. A sentence that says a thing will happen in September is true in July and unverified in October, and not one byte has changed on either side. Nothing fails. No link dies. The page simply goes on promising.

Nine sentences across four pieces, both languages, corrected this morning. The fix is not a tense change — it is the rule this publication already lives by, announced is not measured, applied to a verb. Where we had written Ofsted will apply a new model from September, we now write Ofsted’s own 12 June update put the change from September: true today, true next year, and claiming nothing about an outcome nobody here has read yet.

And one decision is yours, with five days on the clock. The home page tells every reader that the current issue is October 2026. On 1 November that sentence becomes false by itself, and nothing in the repository can make it true — it needs to know what the next edition is. That question is GitHub issue 13, opened yesterday.

The weekly review changed how work is chosen, and it mattered within a day

The review found that our work queue had been sorting by age for three weeks, and that age cannot tell the difference between a question nobody has answered yet and a page that is currently telling a reader something false. In nineteen consecutive choices made by that rule, not once had the operation picked up a live defect — because a fault our own tools found yesterday is, by construction, newer than every question they asked last week. The review split the queue so that anything currently wrong goes first.

This morning that change reached its first reader. Under the old rule today would have gone to a question about stylesheets; under the new one it went to four published pieces that were wrong. Twenty-four hours is a short time to see a procedural decision pay.

Today

A served tense expires, and the date is not the defect

2026-10-26 · Monday · five functions, five pushes
Content & Quality — nine sentences, four pieces, both editions

The item we picked up had been raised eight days ago in these words: seventy-eight published claims carry a date that has not yet arrived, and nothing has ever re-read one. The instruction was right and the thing it asked us to count was the wrong thing. A date that has not arrived misleads nobody; the page is correct, the reader is fine, and that pile shrinks on its own as the calendar moves. It can never reach zero either, because every piece we will ever publish cites a dated plan.

The pile that is wrong now is the opposite one: a date that has arrived, carrying a verb that still points past it. We found eighteen candidates in English. Thirteen of them are not defects, and refusing to touch them matters as much as fixing the rest. A quotation does not expire — the Zambia piece quotes an election notice saying ‘The polls shall take place on Thursday, 13th August 2026’, and “correcting” that would be putting words in someone else’s mouth. Reported speech does not expire either: The New Times reported in August that the two extra years will let graduates qualify at A1 is a claim about a newspaper, not about the future.

Five sentences in English and four in Arabic were genuinely ours and genuinely expired. Ras al-Khaimah, Türkiye (twice), Australia and England. All nine rewritten, both editions, in the first push of the day.

The Arabic edition was right where the English one was wrong, and for a structural reason

One of the four pairs disagreed with itself, and the Arabic side was the correct one. The English said from September 2026, Iqra will run in Ras al-Khaimah’s private schools. The Arabic said the same thing with a verb form that carries no tense at all — it reads equally as “runs” — so it never expired.

English forces a writer to commit to a tense; Arabic does not. So the English composer had to guess an outcome and the Arabic composer was free not to, and the edition that was not forced is the one still true nine months later. We have found the same asymmetry three times this month from the other direction — Arabic forced to go back to the source where English could take a shortcut. This is the first time the freedom ran the other way.

It also means this defect is findable in English and not in Arabic by the same method. English marks the future with a separate word, will, which a computer finds instantly. Arabic marks it with a single letter glued to the front of the verb — and that letter is also the first letter of ordinary words, so our first attempt returned years, six, Sierra Leone and Ceará and missed every real case. Written down as ruling 117, with the instruction that any future check must report the Arabic half as unmeasured rather than as clean.

Content — one register re-read, and it answered

Correcting a tense makes a sentence honest; it does not tell the reader what actually happened. So one of the four was taken the rest of the way. Australia’s assessment authority publishes its own calendar, and the row our piece cites — national results, August 2026 — now reads “August 2026 (published)”. Read in the page’s own served text today, and independently confirmed word-for-word by one of our own checks against the live bytes.

The piece now says the release happened on schedule and says plainly that we have not read the results. The numbers belong to a dated follow-up, never to a quiet rewrite. The other three registers are queued, not forgotten.

Growth — a correction a machine cannot see did not travel

Having fixed four pages, the obvious question: does anything tell a search engine they changed? The sitemap did. The pages themselves did not. Zero of our 88 article pages carried a “last modified” date in their machine-readable description — so every article has been declaring, to the one consumer that reads that description rather than the sitemap, that it has not been touched since the day it published. For the Australia piece, that meant July.

Fixed by feeding the article pages the same date source the sitemap already uses — the repository’s own history, never a build clock and never the publication date, both of which we rejected for the sitemap two months ago for exactly the reasons they are wrong here. 88 of 88 now carry it, and 0 of 88 disagree with the sitemap.

The proof is this morning’s own asymmetry. The English Ras al-Khaimah page reads 26 October; the Arabic one reads 25 October — because the English sentence was corrected today and the Arabic one was deliberately left alone. A page that did not change does not claim to have changed. That is the one property a build clock can never have, and it was demonstrated on a real pair rather than an invented example.

Quality — the gate set ran four times and bit once, on our own work

Four clean builds today: all thirty-six checks that gate the deploy, zero failures. It went red once in between, and that is the part worth reporting — a table row in our own rulebook was written with a missing column, and the check that exists to catch exactly that failed the build on it. A check that only ever reddens on faults we inject at it has not been shown to work. This one caught a live one, filed by the run that owns it.

A second check reddened on the new Australian quotation, correctly: we had quoted a register that nothing had ever been asked to verify. Verifying it moved our citation ledger from 170 registers checked to 192, and confirmed both English quotations present in the live bytes.

Tomorrow

Which of our claims live in the code rather than in the writing?

2026-10-27 · Tuesday
The question tomorrow starts with

Today’s sweep read 1,545 dates across every article in both languages. That is the population every one of our checks reads — and it is not the population a reader is served. The home page’s claim that the current issue is October 2026 is not written in any article. It lives in a small code file, it is served to every visitor, and it sits outside all thirty-nine of our checks.

So: which claims does this publication make in code rather than in writing, and on what date does each one become false? The edition label and its “current” flag, the footer’s copyright year, any year hard-coded in a template or a feed. Cheap to enumerate, and never once enumerated.

The board stands at twenty items, up two. It is growing faster than it is being cleared, and the review has already put a date on where that ends.
