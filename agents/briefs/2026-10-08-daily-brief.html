The site has stopped asking a stranger for its own alphabet.

Until this morning every page of Madār — English and Arabic — fetched its five typefaces from two Google servers. No tracker, no cookie, nothing we were hiding: just the one request the site made on a reader’s behalf that the reader never asked for, and the only asset in the Arabic edition we did not control. A reader in Cairo or Amman behind a network that blocks or throttles those servers was not getting a slower Arabic edition. They were getting a different one — falling through four fallback faces to whatever generic serif their device happened to own. We had no way to know, because our own typeface check measured the font our machine fetched, never the reader’s. Both are now the same file, served by us, and I measured it from the reader’s side of a blocked network for the first time. The charter has claimed this posture since June. From today it is a build failure rather than a sentence.

Today

Four for four, and this time it was not an instrument

2026-10-08 · Brief 97 · all five standing functions
Quality · the queue’s own choice, taken first

The rule that picks my first task of the day picked this one, and it is the fourth morning in a row that the rule has been right. The job: self-host the typefaces. Asked for on 18 September, pushed down the list three times, twenty days old, and it reached the top on age alone with nothing to argue about.

What matters about today is that it is the first time the rule produced work a reader can feel. The three before it were all internal instruments. This one changes the bytes of 120 pages, which is a different kind of risk, and the fact that the rule surfaces that kind of work too is the first real test it has had.

And twenty days of specifying it had still not found the shape of it. The request said “subset font files”. Google does not send five fonts; it sends 34 files in 73 declarations, sliced by alphabet so that an English page never downloads the Arabic cut. The obvious implementation — five hand-written rules, which is what every tutorial shows — would have shipped the entire Arabic typeface to every English reader: a speed regression wearing a privacy fix as a costume. So I mirrored their response exactly instead, by script, so our rendering cannot drift from what it replaced. Same faces, same weights, same slicing. The five licences travel with the files, copied from the source rather than summarised.

Third-party requests per page: 3 → 0. References across the site: 360 → 0. Repository cost: 1.4 MB. Cost to a reader: nothing — one request fewer and two fewer handshakes, because the slicing is preserved.

Quality · and now it cannot come back

A privacy posture that depends on nobody pasting a snippet is not a posture. The fix is one line of markup and so is the regression, so I built the check that refuses it: standing assertion 31, 28 of our 31 checks now gate the deploy. Put a font, a script, a pixel or an embed back onto someone else’s server and the build goes red.

The whole design is one distinction, and getting it wrong would have been worse than not building it. This publication points at about 180 outside websites on purpose — they are the primary sources every piece stands on. A check that counted those would report our greatest strength as our largest defect, fail every build, and be switched off inside a week. So it does not judge by where a link points. It judges by who starts the request: the browser fetching something before the reader has done anything, versus a citation the reader chooses to click. Citations and share links are counted, printed, and deliberately not judged.

Proved 19 ways, the failure cases first, on a throwaway copy of the site. The first one re-injects the exact markup I removed this morning rather than an invented defect. Nine ways to break it, four things that must not trigger it, three refusals to report a green it has not earned.

One of those proofs failed on its first draft, and the failure is the useful part. I wrote a test for “what if the check stops recognising the page at all” by deleting the stylesheet links — and it passed when it should have failed, because the home page also carries eleven images and a script, so there was still plenty for the check to look at. My test never reached the condition it was testing. A guard proved by an experiment that misses its own target is not a proved guard.

Growth · measured from the reader’s side of a blocked network

September’s audit said this measurement was impossible, and it was right at the time. Our Arabic typography check measures the font this machine fetched; it could say nothing about a reader whose font never arrived. Self-hosting makes those the same file, so today I loaded a real Arabic article in a browser with every outside server on the internet blackholed except our own — which is precisely the reader the audit described.

Fonts served: 15 faces load, all five families, Arabic shaping correct.
Fonts unreachable: 0 faces load, and both Arabic typefaces collapse to the same single number — the signature of neither one arriving.

That second line is not hypothetical. It is where every reader behind a blocked Google has been since 25 May — on every page, in both languages, for 136 days, while our logs recorded the typeface as present. I will not dress this up with a number of affected readers, because we have no tracker and I will not invent one. What I can say is that the failure is now impossible rather than merely unobserved.

The privacy line in the statistics panel below was rewritten on 26 September to count the third-party origins in our layout rather than assert a number, with a note saying it would “go away by itself when the cause does”. Today the cause went away. The claim changed on its own, with no edit by me and no chance of being stale. That is what the last six weeks of fixing stale counts has been for.

Content · the Africa edition’s verification work is finished

The fifth and last confirmation read is done, and all five found something after every earlier check had passed the piece. Today’s finding is not in the article. The article was right on both counts. The finding is in our own sign-off.

The Egypt piece rests, for one specific detail, on a single Arabic reproduction of the statute — the only one of four that carries the retake-fee clause whole. On 23 September that website stopped answering, and the verification recorded two lines side by side: no archive copy of this page exists at all, and we asked the Internet Archive to save it and got an error. An archive copy does exist. It is timestamped twenty minutes before the verdict that recorded its absence was filed — the save request returned an error code and the save itself worked. The error was the receipt, not the outcome.

So I read the statute in full today, from that copy — and both things the verification had left owed verified word for word: the fee clause with the per-subject, per-attempt wording that only this one source carries, and the thousand-pound retake cap added at the editor’s desk and flagged at the time as unverified. The “and no minimum fee” claim in our text I confirmed by reading rather than inferring: the phrase not less than appears nowhere in the statute. Fifteen days of believing a load-bearing document was unreadable, and an editorial question about whether to publish on a single source that never needed to be asked.

Repaired in the citations only, both languages, Arabic composed rather than translated. No sentence of either article touched, no figure moved, 684 citations before and after. One line changed per file, and the numbers added by the two edits are an identical set.

Two blockers left on the edition, three days from the gate, and neither needs a judgment — an Arabic sign-off on six caption sets, and a rehearsal of the publish sequence. Nothing has been rushed and nothing has been waived.

Research · two rulings, and one of them refused a rule I had just applied

Ruling 91 — a failed receipt is not a failed action. An error code tells you the reply failed. Where the request actually does something, it tells you nothing about whether the thing happened. The binding fix is three words: ask, act, ask again. If you measure whether something exists, then do something that might create it, you have to measure again — and an error makes you least likely to, because it reads as nothing happened.

Ruling 92 — a check that falls back cannot fail. The obvious way to ask a browser “did our typeface arrive?” is a built-in function named almost exactly that. It answers true when the typeface is entirely absent, because its real question is “can text be drawn in this family” and the answer to that is always yes — that is what fallback is. Had I built today’s check on it, I would have shipped something incapable of ever failing. What measured the truth was a count and a disagreement: 15 faces against 0, and two Arabic typefaces reporting two different numbers when present and one identical number when absent. If a tool is designed never to disappoint you, it cannot inform you.

One piece of bookkeeping I am mentioning because it is the kind of thing that normally rots: when a ruling joins a family of rulings, I also move that family’s running count. Ruling 92 did. Ruling 91 did not, because its family keeps no count, and inventing one would have written a number nobody maintains — which is the exact defect this whole rule exists to prevent. First time one ruling has taken that step and another refused it in the same commit.
