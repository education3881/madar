Manager → Founder

Writing Egypt in Arabic took a number out of the English.

The Egypt piece became a pair today — composed in Arabic, not translated, and gated. Somewhere in that work a sentence had to be rebuilt rather than carried across, and rebuilding it exposed a figure the English had been leaning on: “the 120-year-old examination.”

No source we read says 120. It is the kind of fact that feels too settled to check, and it survived a draft, a recon and four days because nothing forced anyone to look at it again. Arabic forced it: you cannot write that phrase without choosing a construction, and choosing made it visible.

The nearest registers we could find point at 1887 — which would be 139 years, not 120 — and they are newspapers, not documents. So we did not correct one unsourced number with another. We struck it. The sentence keeps every bit of its argument and none of its arithmetic.

Row 5 of Edition 05 is now bilingual. “The doors were published before the exams existed” / «نُشِرت الأبوابُ قبل أن توجد الامتحانات» — composed in Arabic from the same registers the English was written from, which is why its legal quotations are the statute’s own words and not a translation of ours. Arabic Editor’s gate: PASS. Held, like the whole wave.
The measurement that matters most on a pair: 39 figures on each side, and not one figure on either side that the other does not carry. This is the first pair in the edition clean in both directions — the four before it each had English-only tokens, because they carried grade labels Arabic renders as words. This piece has none; its numbers are dates, money, percentages and article numbers, and all of them cross.
The finding, and it went backwards into the English
The struck number is described above. Two further decisions, both about names, both recorded rather than quietly made: the minister is named محمد عبد اللطيف‎ and the MP فريدي البياضي‎, because Arabic registers we read today carry them — for these two the Arabic is the original and the English is the transliteration. A third name, a journalist’s byline, is left in Latin: no Arabic register for it could be found, and very probably Arabic is how a person’s name gets invented.
Quality — the question we left ourselves yesterday, answered
Every QA pass now ends by writing down what it cannot see, and the next run tests that before adding anything. Yesterday’s note said: we check the order of our lists; we have never checked the order of anything else a reader receives in sequence. Three surfaces named. Two turned out to be incapable of going wrong, and we proved it rather than assuming it. The third was wrong.
The browse index sorts its rows, and where two rows tie it broke the tie by asking the computer to compare two words — without saying in which language. That question has a default, and the default comes from the machine. Same code, same words, two machines: Türkiye sorts before Turkmenistan on one and after it on the other. We have had a Türkiye piece since July. It is kept off that index today only by a rule that a country needs two pieces before it gets a page — which is a fact about how much we have written, not a promise about anything.
And one that readers could actually see
The same read found that every Arabic browse page has been printing its dates in Western digits — 7 يوليو 2026 — next to an Arabic home page printing ٢٨ يوليو ٢٠٢٦. Seven days, nine pages, in the edition where it is most obviously wrong. One route file asked for the wrong flavour of Arabic. Fixed, and now checked on every build.
Our Arabic check had read those pages clean every single day. Nothing was wrong with it: it looks for English letters, and a date is digits. The recurring lesson of the last month, in a new costume — a check can only fail on what it enumerates.
Growth — what the wave does to the navigation, read in advance
Five held pieces will flip in one commit. The browse index is rebuilt from the published set every build, so that commit rewrites the navigation. Nobody had asked what it rewrites it to. Now computed: one new page in each language (/browse/country/sudan/), no page lost, no existing address changed, and English and Arabic identical page for page.
The least eventful answer available, and worth having in writing: the risk was never the new page, it was an existing address moving under a reader mid-flip. Nothing moves. Africa goes 5 → 10 — not the 5 → 11 a comment in the code predicts, because that comment describes the edition and the flip describes the drafts.
Research — ruling #59
A comparator with no locale asks the machine. Where a build sorts, formats or folds text, name the language and the numbering explicitly — every default is read off the runner. Yesterday’s ruling asked is the order determined?; today’s asks determined by what? Filed with its register range reconciled in the same commit, which is the rule Sunday wrote after the register drifted ten rulings behind.
Both checks were proved twice before landing — green on the real build, and red on a defect deliberately introduced, with the introduction itself verified to have changed the file. One of them bit on the real defect rather than a planted one. And the new check’s first run corrected the check: it had been miscounting three topics because it only understood one of the two ways we write a list in a file.

The question tomorrow tests first
Three rulings now cover how our lists are ordered, and all three are about the comparison. Nothing has looked at the other side: the value being compared. Every date in this publication is a bare YYYY-MM-DD with no timezone, parsed as midnight UTC, on a machine set to UTC, for a publication whose calendar is Dubai.
The test: does any surface — the sitemap, the feeds, the printed date, the week buckets in the panel above — disagree with the file about which day a piece was published? It costs one script, and every ordering we have proved this week is only as true as its answer.
