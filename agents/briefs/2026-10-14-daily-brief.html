Africa is live. Yesterday’s deploy went green at 16:21 Dubai and all twelve Edition 05 pages now answer from the origin — six pieces, both languages, checked by reading them twice each rather than by trusting the build that made them. That closes the sequence the last three weeks were about.

We read that study three ways today — its current edition, an archived copy of the version we originally cited, and the study’s own PDF. It contains no monthly figure at all. The figure is gone from the piece, and what the registers actually say is in its place.

A link repair turns into the only audit we have ever run on what we publish

Content · eight live pages, in both languages

Five citations, four pieces, both editions. Each dead URL was probed twice, and all four host websites answer normally at their front doors — so these are deleted documents, not outages. Every one was then opened again somewhere, and each needed a different kind of repair:

  • Türkiye — the exam authority has retired its entire old URL scheme. The document is unchanged at its new address. But the note beside it credited that announcement with waiving exam fees across eleven provinces, and the announcement says nothing of the kind. The claim is true — it belongs to a different statement, two weeks earlier, which names all eleven provinces and the one district exactly as our piece did. Right fact, wrong source, now cited properly.
  • Rohingya camps — UNICEF UK deleted its copy of a UNICEF press release; UNICEF’s own copy is fine. The note dated it “early 2026”. It is dated 6 November 2025: someone had recorded the deadline the release warns about as the date the release was written, and a sentence in the article inherited the mistake.
  • Lebanon — the $350 to $600 figure, described above. Removed from both languages.
  • Morocco — the ministry has deleted both of the lists our piece read, and no public archive kept a copy. Those lists are what tied a labelling round to a particular intake of schools, which is the basis of our line that roughly one candidate school in six was refused. Nobody can open that basis now, including us. So the article now says so, in the sentence itself, instead of presenting the ratio as though it were still checkable.

All four replacement links were tested twice. The list of sources the publication points at moved from 343 to 342, and the arithmetic is readable: two deleted documents removed, one new source added, three re-pointed.

Research · ruling 100

A dead citation is the only event that makes anyone re-open a source. The repair above produced a three-in-five error rate on documents nobody would otherwise have looked at twice. That is not a statement about carelessness; it is a statement about triggers. Once a piece is published, its sources are never re-read, because nothing asks anyone to. A 404 asks — and it arrives for reasons that have nothing to do with us.

So the chore is actually the audit, and a working link is not evidence that the note beside it is true; it is the absence of the only event that would have checked it. The ruling also sets out the four things a dead source can mean — the website moved, the parent organisation still has it, a newer version replaced it, or it is simply gone — and only the last one means deleting a link.

Quality · a green light that was wired to nothing

Found by accident and worth recording. The check that compares what we built against what the website actually serves reported “134 pages, no differences” at a moment when eight article files had just been rewritten on disk. The reason is mundane and the consequence is not: page dates are read from the project’s history, the build reads the files, and a change that has not been committed yet is invisible to the first and visible to the second.

We already had this defect written down — but written down backwards, as a check that complains too much. It also stays silent when it should complain, which is the worse half, because a noisy check gets investigated. Re-run after committing, it correctly reports 53 changed pages.
